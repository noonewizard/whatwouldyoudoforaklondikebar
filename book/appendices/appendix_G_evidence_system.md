# APPENDIX G
## Evidence Classification System

The method used throughout this book, set out formally so that a reader can apply it
independently — which is, in the end, the point of the exercise.

---

### I. The source hierarchy

| Level | Category | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Primary sources: period documents, manuscripts, original editions, contemporary observation | Load-bearing evidence |
| **B** | Scholarly secondary sources: university presses, peer-reviewed journals, recognized specialists | Interpretation, framing, correction |
| **C** | Critical editions of primary texts, with apparatus | Access to A with scholarly support |
| **D** | Popular, partisan or derivative material | **Evidence of belief only**, and labeled as such in the text, not merely in a note |

**Two rules override the hierarchy.**

**The rule of direction.** A source is primary for its own period and secondary — often
unreliably so — for any earlier one. Blavatsky is a primary source for 1880s Theosophy and
a Level D source for ancient Egypt. Pike is a primary source for nineteenth-century American
Masonry and a Level D source for the ancient mysteries. Anderson's 1738 *Constitutions* is a
primary source for what Grand Lodge believed in 1738 and a weak secondary source for 1717.

**The rule against circularity.** No claim is admitted on the authority of a source that is
itself quoting a source the author has not consulted. Circular sourcing — a claim that
appears authoritative because it has been repeated rather than because it was ever verified
— is the characteristic failure of this literature.

---

### II. The six questions

Applied, in order, to any claim that something old was carried into something newer.

**1. Provenance.** Where is the physical object? Which archive, which shelfmark, which
edition? Who has held it since? A claim supported by a document nobody can point to is not
supported.

**2. Chronology.** Does the timeline permit the influence? This is the cheapest test and it
is skipped constantly.

**3. Transmission.** Can you name the route — a person, a text, a translation, a purchase, a
meeting? *Availability is not transmission.* That a book was in print in a country tells you
someone could have read it. It does not tell you anyone did.

**4. Intertextuality.** Does the later source quote, paraphrase or share distinctive
language with the earlier one? Resemblance is weak; quotation is strong; shared
idiosyncratic error is decisive.

**5. Institutional continuity.** Did the organization actually persist? Organizations that
exist collect dues, keep lists, rent rooms, quarrel and leave paper.

**6. Anachronism.** Does the claim contain something that could not yet have existed? A
symbol turning on an English initial cannot be Egyptian. A Kabbalistic system with tarot
attributions cannot predate the tarot.

---

### III. The governing rule

**A parallel is not a descent.**

If an element in one tradition resembles an element in another, that is a fact about the two
elements. To convert it into a fact about history requires a route (question 3) or textual
dependence (question 4).

Human beings across all cultures use light for knowledge, darkness for ignorance, height for
authority, the circle for completeness, and death-and-return for transformation. These recur
because human bodies and human societies recur. Their presence in two places is nearly
uninformative. It is the *unusual* features that carry evidential weight — a shared oddity,
a distinctive detail with no independent reason to exist.

---

### IV. The five claim-types

Never to be exchanged silently.

| Type | Form | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|
| **Historical** | "Practitioners believed X." | Their books, letters, rituals |
| **Phenomenological** | "Practitioners reported experiencing X." | First-person testimony |
| **Psychological** | "Ritual of this kind may produce effect Y." | Theory, hedged; never a diagnosis of an individual |
| **Sociological** | "Ritual of this kind creates structure Z." | Records: membership, accounts, networks |
| **Metaphysical** | "Mechanism X objectively exists." | **Never asserted by the narrator, in either direction** |

The characteristic failure of writing on this subject — in credulous and debunking books
alike — is the silent slide from *they believed* to *they experienced* to *it happened*.

---

### V. Verdict vocabulary

| Verdict | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Established** | Documented, uncontested, evidence available |
| **Strong** | Well evidenced; minor disagreement about scope |
| **Mixed** | Partly supported; the claim as usually stated overreaches the evidence |
| **Disputed** | Competent scholars disagree; evidence cuts both ways |
| **Speculative** | Chronologically possible; no transmission evidence |
| **Unsupported** | Fails the questions; no evidence exists |
| **Refuted** | Positively disproved, or withdrawn by its own proponents |

---

### VI. Signaling in the text

**Reconstruction must be marked.** Where prose reconstructs an atmosphere rather than
reporting a source, the text says so — by an explicit frame, by conditional grammar, or by a
note. Never by tone alone.

**Uncertainty must be stated.** "The evidence establishes," "the surviving sources
indicate," "this is disputed," "there is no surviving evidence that," "the chronology makes
this difficult to sustain" — used precisely, not decoratively.

**Attribution must be specific.** Not "scholars agree" but "Stevenson argues," "Hanegraaff
reframes," "Butler's library evidence shows." If the scholarship cannot be named, the
sentence should not be written.

**Unverified material must be flagged.** Where this manuscript could not verify a citation,
a quotation's wording, or a page reference, it carries **[SOURCE VERIFICATION REQUIRED]**
rather than an invented particular. Nothing in this book has been fabricated to fill a gap.
