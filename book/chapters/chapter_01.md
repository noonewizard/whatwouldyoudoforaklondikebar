# PART I — THE DOOR

# CHAPTER ONE
## The Lodge at Midnight

It was not midnight.

That is the first thing to get out of the way, because almost everything written about
that afternoon has wanted it to be midnight, and it was not. The record is specific, and
the record says half past four.

Elias Ashmole kept a diary the way some men keep accounts. He noted the hour he was
born, the hours of his marriages, the hours at which he took physic and the hours at
which it worked. He was an astrologer, and to an astrologer a time is not a detail; it
is the thing that makes an event interpretable. So when he recorded what happened to him
at Warrington in Lancashire on 16 October 1646, he recorded the minute:

> 4H. 30' P.M. I was made a Free Mason at Warrington in Lancashire, with Coll: Henry
> Mainwaring of Karincham in Cheshire.

Then, as was his habit, he listed the men who were present: Richard Penket the Warden,
James Collier, Richard Sankey, Henry Littler, John Ellam, Richard Ellam, and Hugh
Brewer.[^1]

That is the entry. Eight names, a place, a date, and a time. It is, by a wide margin,
the best-documented moment in the early history of English Freemasonry, and it tells us
almost nothing.

---

Half past four on the sixteenth of October in the north of England: the light is going.
Not gone — the sun would set within the hour — but low and thin and coming in sideways,
the particular gray-gold of a Lancashire autumn afternoon. It had been a wet month. The
Mersey, which runs past Warrington on its way to the sea, would have been high.

I am reconstructing, and I want to flag that I am reconstructing, because this book is
going to ask the reader to distinguish between what a source says and what a writer has
supplied, and it would be dishonest to start by blurring the line myself. The diary does
not tell us what the weather was, or which building they met in, or whether there were
candles. It tells us the hour, and the hour tells us the light was failing. Everything
else in that paragraph is inference from a latitude and a date.

What we can say with confidence is stranger than the atmosphere.

Warrington in 1646 was a town of perhaps two thousand people. It had a bridge, a market,
and a recent memory of being fought over — it had been stormed and besieged during the
civil war, and the war, though nobody could be quite sure of it yet, had just ended.
Oxford had surrendered to Parliament in June. The King was in Scottish custody. The
country was in that peculiar suspended condition of a war that has stopped without
anything being settled.

What Warrington did not have, so far as anyone has been able to establish, was a stone
building trade of any consequence. There was no cathedral going up, no castle under
repair, no body of professional masons who would have needed a lodge in the original
sense of the word — a hut or shed beside a building site, where the men who cut stone
ate, slept, stored their tools, and controlled entry to their trade.

And the seven men listed in Ashmole's diary were not masons. They have been traced.
They were local gentry and men of modest property: a Penket, a Sankey, two Ellams,
names that belong to Warrington's landowning and mercantile families rather than to its
building trades.[^2] The one possible exception is Hugh Brewer, who may have been a
working man. The rest were not cutting stone, and neither was Ashmole, and neither was
the man made alongside him.

That man deserves a sentence of his own. Colonel Henry Mainwaring of Karincham had
fought in the war. So had Ashmole. They had fought on opposite sides. Mainwaring was a
Parliamentarian officer; Ashmole was a royalist who had held a commission in the King's
ordnance at Oxford and had been driven out of it by the surrender four months earlier.
They were, moreover, connected by marriage — Ashmole's wife was a Mainwaring.

So the scene, stripped of romance, is this. In a small Lancashire town, in the last light
of an October afternoon, a defeated royalist and a victorious parliamentarian officer
were admitted together into a society of men who were not stonemasons, in a place with
no stonemasonry to speak of, four months after a civil war that had killed a larger
proportion of the English population than the First World War would kill three centuries
later.

Nobody wrote down why.

---

This is the point at which most accounts of Elias Ashmole become confident, and it is
the point at which I want to slow down.

The temptation is very strong, and it comes in two flavours.

The first says: *look at the man*. Elias Ashmole was an alchemist. In 1652 he would edit
and publish the *Theatrum Chemicum Britannicum*, a collection of English alchemical
poetry that remains a serious scholarly landmark. He collected manuscripts of John Dee.
He cast horoscopes for himself and others with relentless diligence. He was fascinated by
the Rosicrucian manifestos, which had been published in Germany when he was a child, and
he copied out a letter of application to the Fraternity of the Rosy Cross — an
organization that, as we shall see, did not exist. He was, in short, precisely the kind
of seventeenth-century Englishman whom a modern reader expects to find in an esoteric
society. Therefore, the argument goes, Freemasonry in 1646 must have had something
esoteric in it. Why else would he join?

The second temptation says: *look at the war*. Here is a royalist and a parliamentarian,
admitted together, four months after the fighting stopped, into a society whose whole
apparatus — secrecy, obligation, brotherhood, a promise of mutual aid regardless of
faction — looks designed for exactly this moment. Therefore Freemasonry in 1646 must have
been a device for reconciliation, a neutral ground in a shattered country.

Both arguments are attractive. Both have been made by serious people. And neither is in
the diary.

Ashmole wrote thousands upon thousands of words about himself. He recorded his dreams,
his ailments, the sigils he cast, the exact hour at which a toothache began to ease. On
the subject of what happened to him at Warrington, and what he thought it was, and why
he wanted it, he wrote nothing at all. He recorded the fact with the same neutral
precision he gave to the weather.

And then — this is the detail that ought to unsettle anyone who wants a tidy explanation
— he seems to have done nothing with it for thirty-five years. The next time a Masonic
lodge appears in his diary is March 1682, in London, when he attended a meeting at
Masons' Hall and noted that he was the senior Fellow present, since it had been
thirty-five years since his admission.[^3] Between those two entries, over more than
three decades in which he wrote almost daily, there is silence.

Whatever Freemasonry was to Elias Ashmole, it was not a preoccupation. It was something
he did once, in the failing light of an October afternoon, and then remembered thirty-
five years later with what reads like mild antiquarian satisfaction.

---

So we have a question, and it is worth being precise about its shape, because the shape
of this question is the shape of the whole book.

We do not have the question *what secret did Ashmole learn?* There is no evidence he
learned a secret, and the earliest surviving descriptions of what a candidate learned in
a seventeenth-century lodge — Scottish catechisms written down fifty years later, which
we will look at closely — describe a word, a handgrip, and a short set of questions and
answers. Useful for recognizing a fellow craftsman on a strange building site. Not a
revelation.

We have, instead, a harder and more interesting question: *why did men who had nothing
to do with the building trade want to be admitted to the building trade's private
institution?*

Because that is what was happening, all over Britain, in the middle of the seventeenth
century. In Scotland, where the records are far better, non-operative members — men
described in the minutes as "gentlemen" — begin appearing in lodges of working masons
from 1600 onward. In England the evidence is thinner and later, but by the 1640s men
like Ashmole are being "accepted" into a craft they will never practice. Something about
the masons' lodge — its oath, its privacy, its brotherhood, its old written legends
tracing the craft back through Euclid to the building of Solomon's Temple — was
attractive to men who had no professional use for it at all.

That attraction is the engine of everything that follows in this book. Over the next
three hundred pages we will watch it pull in, successively: Scottish lairds and English
antiquaries; London Newtonians and Parisian aristocrats; a Bordeaux merchant conducting
angelic invocations; Prussian alchemists; a free Black leatherworker in Boston; a
Confederate general with a library of eight thousand volumes; a Victorian coroner; and
a poet who told the world he was the Beast of Revelation. They all wanted in, and they
all wanted in for different reasons, and the institution accommodated every one of them
without ever quite telling any of them what it meant.

---

There is a version of this book that answers the Ashmole question. It would say: he
joined because the lodge preserved the Hermetic and alchemical wisdom he had spent his
life pursuing, and he recognized it. It would note that he was an alchemist and a
Freemason, and treat the conjunction as a channel.

I am not going to write that sentence, and the reason is worth stating once, clearly,
here at the start, because it will govern fifty subsequent chapters.

Elias Ashmole was an alchemist and a Freemason. That is a documented fact about a man.
It is not a documented fact about an institution. It tells us that one person could
contain both interests; it does not tell us that the two things were connected, any more
than a modern astronomer who plays the cello demonstrates a link between astronomy and
chamber music. To get from *a man held both* to *the two were joined*, you need
something else: a text, a transmission, a route. You need to be able to say by what
means, in what year, through whose hands.

Nobody has produced that for 1646. Not for want of looking.

What we have instead is an absence with a very precise shape — and absences of precise
shape are, for a historian, among the most useful objects in the world. They tell you
where the answer is not, which is the only reliable way of narrowing down where it
might be.

---

The light goes out of that Warrington room at about half past five. The eight men go
home. Ashmole writes his entry, in the private shorthand of a man who records
everything, and then does not mention the subject again until he is sixty-five years
old.

What he had joined was not ancient, not Egyptian, and not — in any sense he would have
recognized — magical. It was a small, strange, locally-run association with medieval
roots in a trade he did not practice, and it was in the middle of becoming something
else entirely.

To understand what it became, and why a hundred years later men would be performing
theurgic operations inside its structures, and two hundred years later would be
reconstructing the Kabbalah in its temples, we need to sort out three things first.

We need to know what the word *magic* has actually meant to the people who used it,
because it has not meant one thing, and almost every confusion in this subject begins
with the assumption that it has.

We need to know what was actually secret in a Masonic lodge — and why publishing the
secrets, repeatedly, in cheap print, over four decades, did nothing whatever to the
institution.

And we need a method: a way of telling, when somebody says *this is very old*, whether
it is.

Those are the next four chapters. Then we can go back to the beginning, which is not
Egypt, and not Jerusalem, and not a Templar commandery, but a set of Greek philosophical
dialogues written in Roman Egypt in the second century of the Christian era, which
Europe would spend three hundred years mistaking for something eleven centuries older.
