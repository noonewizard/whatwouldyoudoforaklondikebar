# CHAPTER TWO
## What We Mean When We Say Magic

In the year 1509 a young German soldier-scholar of twenty-three sat down to write a book
that would make him famous, notorious, and eventually miserable. Its title was *De
occulta philosophia* — *On Occult Philosophy* — and it proposed that the universe was a
single connected body in which everything corresponded to everything else, that a
trained mind could read those correspondences, and that a person who understood them
could act.

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa called this *magia*. He also called it the highest of the
sciences, "the most perfect and chief science, that sacred and sublimer kind of
philosophy," and he meant it. To him, magic was not a deviation from natural philosophy.
It was natural philosophy, completed: the study of nature in its lower storey, of the
heavens in its middle, and of the divine in its upper, all at once.

Two hundred years later, an Englishman using the same word would have meant a village
woman muttering over a sick cow, and would have used it to mean *fraud*.

A hundred and fifty years after that, a Frenchman who had trained for the priesthood and
then written revolutionary pamphlets would pick the word up again and mean something
neither Agrippa nor the Englishman would have recognised: a science of the will, in which
the operator's own imagination was the instrument and the operator's own transformation
was the point.

And fifty years after *that*, a young Englishman with money and an appetite for scandal
would add a *k* to the end of the word to distinguish what he did from stage conjuring,
and define it as "the Science and Art of causing Change to occur in conformity with
Will" — a definition so capacious that it includes baking bread, and which he cheerfully
noted did include baking bread.

Four men, four centuries, one word, four incompatible meanings. This is the problem.

---

Nearly every confusion in the subject of this book begins here. People argue about
whether Freemasonry is "magical" without establishing what either party means, and so
they argue past each other indefinitely. The Mason hears *magic* and thinks: spells,
demons, superstition — no, obviously not. The critic hears *magic* and thinks: ritual,
secrecy, symbolism, invocation — well, obviously yes. Both are responding accurately to
the word as they have received it. Neither is describing anything historical.

So before we go any further, we need to take the word apart.

---

### The trouble with the category

Let me put the hardest point first, because it is the one most often skipped.

A significant body of modern scholarship holds that "magic" is not a description of
anything in the world at all — that it is, and has almost always been, a term of
exclusion. Randall Styers has traced in detail how the category was constructed in the
modern period precisely as the negative space against which religion and science defined
themselves: magic is what *they* do, over there, wrongly.[^1] Bernd-Christian Otto and
Michael Stausberg, surveying two centuries of attempts to define it, reach a similar
conclusion about the scholarly definitions themselves: most of them turn out, on
inspection, to encode the definer's assumptions about what proper religion looks
like.[^2]

There is real force in this. Watch the word travel through a single culture and you will
see it move with the politics rather than with the practice. The same operation is
*prayer* when the monastery does it and *sorcery* when the widow does it. The same
astrological medicine is *natural philosophy* when a Florentine priest publishes it under
a Medici's protection and *witchcraft* when a cunning-woman charges sixpence for it. The
word does not track a method. It tracks who is permitted.

If that were the whole story, the honest response would be to stop using the word.

But it is not quite the whole story, and the reason is important. Some people called
themselves magicians. Agrippa did, deliberately and at length, and staked a reputation on
it. Éliphas Lévi did. Aleister Crowley built an entire public identity on it. When a
practitioner adopts the label, the label stops being purely an accusation and becomes,
whatever else it is, a piece of evidence about what that person thought they were doing.

So the working rule of this book is: *magic* is not a thing in the world. It is a word
people used, and the history of what they meant by it is a large part of the history we
are actually telling. Wherever the word appears here it will be qualified, and wherever
somebody in this book uses it, I will try to say what they meant.

---

### The varieties, and what distinguishes them

Here is the vocabulary, assembled roughly as an early modern reader would have
understood it. It is worth a little patience, because these distinctions do real work
later.

**Natural magic** is the manipulation of hidden properties in physical things — the
*occult* qualities, in the strict sense of *hidden*, not *sinister*. Why does a magnet
attract iron? Why does this herb reduce fever and that one kill? Before the mechanical
philosophy of the seventeenth century, these were genuinely mysterious, and the tradition
that studied them was called natural magic. Much of it is simply pre-modern science, and
a good deal of it worked.

**Celestial** or **astral magic** adds the heavens: the doctrine that the planets shape
terrestrial things, and that a practitioner can draw down a favourable influence by
assembling the right materials at the right time. Marsilio Ficino's medical advice to
melancholy scholars — sunlight, gold, music in the Solar mode — is astral magic, and he
published it under a cardinal's nose.

**Sympathetic magic** is the underlying logic of both: like affects like, and things once
in contact remain connected. The category is a nineteenth-century anthropologist's, not a
Renaissance practitioner's, and should be used carefully.

**Talismanic magic** is astral magic made portable: an image engraved on the correct
metal, at the correct hour, to hold an influence.

**Ceremonial magic** is where the temperature changes. Here the operator does not
manipulate properties but addresses *persons* — spirits, angels, intelligences, demons —
with names, seals, circles, and formulae. This is the magic of the medieval grimoires, of
the *Key of Solomon*, of John Dee and Edward Kelley kneeling at a table in the 1580s
trying to get the angels to speak clearly. It is also the direct ancestor of what the
Golden Dawn would do in a rented room in London three hundred years later.

**Theurgy** — literally "god-working" — is the most important term in this chapter and
the most frequently mangled. In the third and fourth centuries, the Neoplatonist
Iamblichus argued that ritual operations could raise the soul toward the divine, and he
was emphatic that this was *not* the low sorcery of the marketplace. Theurgy is
performed, it is ritual, it uses names and materials — and its practitioners spent
considerable energy insisting it was categorically different from magic. That insistence
is itself a permanent feature of this history. Almost everyone in this book who does
something we would call magic denies that it is magic. It is worth noticing how often,
and why.

**Thaumaturgy** is wonder-working: the production of visible marvels. Where theurgy aims
at the operator's ascent, thaumaturgy aims at the spectator's astonishment.

**Alchemy** and **astrology** sit adjacent to all of this, sometimes inside it and
sometimes proudly outside it. Both were, for most of their history, technical disciplines
with their own literatures, apprenticeships and standards. Neither should be flattened
into "magic."

And then there are the modern constructions. **Occultism** is a coinage of the
mid-nineteenth century, not an ancient category. **Magick** with a *k* is Crowley's, from
around 1904. **Hermetic Qabalah** is a Victorian and Edwardian system that shares a name,
and a fraction of a vocabulary, with Jewish Kabbalah. Using any of these terms for a
period before it existed is not shorthand. It is an anachronism, and anachronism is the
single most productive source of error in this entire field.

---

### The four sentences

There is one more piece of equipment to install before we go on, and it is the most
important in the book.

When somebody says "magic works," they may be saying any of five completely different
things. These get confused constantly, including by careful people, and once they are
confused an argument can run for a century without anyone noticing that the two sides are
not discussing the same proposition.

**The historical claim.** *Practitioners believed X.* This is a statement about the
contents of people's minds in the past, and it is established by ordinary historical
evidence — their books, their letters, their rituals. It carries no implication whatever
about whether X is true. When this book says that eighteenth-century Masons believed
their Craft descended from Solomon's Temple, that is a historical claim, and it is
straightforwardly demonstrable.

**The phenomenological claim.** *Practitioners reported experiencing X.* Also a
historical statement, but about experience rather than belief, and it requires
first-person testimony. When a Golden Dawn initiate wrote that during the Vault ceremony
she felt something she could not name, that report is evidence of the report. It is not
evidence of a cause.

**The psychological claim.** *A ritual of this kind may produce effect Y in participants.*
This is a modern theoretical statement, hedged, and it must never be turned into a
diagnosis of a specific dead individual. We can say what the literature on ritual and
memory suggests. We cannot say what happened in the mind of Elias Ashmole at half past
four on 16 October 1646, and any book that tells you otherwise is making it up.

**The sociological claim.** *A ritual of this kind creates social structure Z.* This is
often the best-evidenced of the five, because it leaves records: membership lists,
charity accounts, business partnerships, marriage patterns, political alignments. Much of
what Freemasonry demonstrably *did* in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries belongs
here.

**The metaphysical claim.** *A supernatural mechanism objectively exists and operates.*
This is not a historian's claim to make. It is not that I think it is false; it is that
nothing in my evidence could establish it either way. **The narrator of this book will
never assert one, in either direction.**

Those five sentences are the grammar of everything that follows. The single most common
failure in the literature on Freemasonry and the occult — in the credulous books and the
debunking books equally — is the silent slide from one to another: from *they believed*
to *they experienced* to *it happened*. Watch for it. I have tried very hard not to do
it, and where I have come close I would rather the reader caught me than not.

### Why this matters for a lodge

Return, with this equipment, to the room in Warrington.

Was what happened to Elias Ashmole magic?

The question now decomposes into several answerable ones. Did the ceremony involve the
manipulation of occult natural properties? No evidence of it. Did it address spirits by
name? No evidence of it — and, as we shall see, the earliest surviving ritual texts are
conspicuously free of anything of the kind. Did it involve ritual, secrecy, symbolic
objects, an oath, and a staged transition from ignorance to knowledge? Yes,
unambiguously, on the evidence of the catechisms written down within living memory.

So: not ceremonial magic. Not theurgy. Not astral or talismanic operation. But a
structured ritual technology aimed at transforming a person's status, and — this is the
part that will matter enormously later — built on a symbolic architecture of darkness and
light, death and raising, that would prove capable of holding almost any content poured
into it.

Agrippa would not have called that magic. Lévi, three hundred years later, absolutely
would have — and did. Crowley's definition includes it without effort.

Which tells us something about Freemasonry, and rather more about the word.

The word did not stay still. Between the 1640s and the 1850s it changed its meaning
under everybody's feet, moving from *the manipulation of nature's hidden properties* to
*the transformation of the operator through will, symbol and imagination*. And a
Freemasonry that was not magic under the first definition became, without altering a
single one of its rituals, magic under the second.

That migration is one of the two or three most important events in this book, and we will
watch it happen in Part VI, in the hands of a French ex-seminarian who had spent his
twenties in prison for writing about the poor.

But it did not happen out of nothing. The materials Lévi built with had been lying around
Europe for three centuries, and to understand where he got them we will have to go back
past the lodge entirely — past Warrington, past Scotland, past the whole institution of
Freemasonry — into a set of Greek dialogues that Europe read as ancient Egyptian
scripture for a hundred and forty-three years after a Swiss scholar had proved they were
nothing of the kind.

First, though, the secret. Because there is a widespread assumption that a society which
keeps secrets must have something worth keeping, and the history of Freemasonry offers an
unusually clean test of that assumption. The secrets were published — completely,
repeatedly, cheaply, and over four decades — and nothing happened at all.

That is worth understanding before we go any further.
