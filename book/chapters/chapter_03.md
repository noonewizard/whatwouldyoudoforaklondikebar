# CHAPTER THREE
## The Problem of the Secret

In 1730 a London printer issued a pamphlet of about thirty pages, priced at sixpence,
called *Masonry Dissected*. Its author was a man named Samuel Prichard, who described
himself on the title page as a "late Member of a Constituted Lodge," and who had done
something that ought, by every reasonable expectation, to have destroyed Freemasonry.

He printed the secrets.

Not some of them. All of them, as far as anyone can tell: the questions and answers of
the catechism, the words, the grips, the signs, the shape of the ceremony, and — for the
first time in print — the legend of the third degree, the story of the murdered architect
of Solomon's Temple. Prichard swore an affidavit before a magistrate that it was a "true
and genuine copy." It sold enormously. It went through three editions in under a
fortnight and was still being reprinted, pirated and translated decades later.[^1]

Freemasonry's response was to grow.

Over the following century the institution expanded across Europe, the Americas, and
eventually most of the inhabited world, acquired kings and emperors as members, built
enormous buildings, and became the model for hundreds of imitators. The secrets remained
available in any bookshop the entire time. They are available now, free, in ten seconds,
to anyone reading this sentence with a phone nearby.

Something is obviously wrong with our idea of what the secret was.

---

### The four things "secret" can mean

It helps to separate them.

**Informational secrecy** is the ordinary kind: a fact that is concealed because knowing
it confers advantage. A safe combination. A troop movement. The identity of a source.
This kind of secret is destroyed by publication, permanently and completely.

**Functional secrecy** is a fact concealed because concealment performs a job. The
original Masonic secrets were largely of this type. In the medieval and early modern
building trades, a mason arriving at a strange site needed to demonstrate that he was
what he claimed to be — trained, entitled, entered into the craft. A word and a grip did
that. The Scottish catechisms of the 1690s, our earliest ritual evidence, are substantially
concerned with exactly this: how to recognise a brother, what to say, what to answer.[^2]
This kind of secret is damaged by publication, but only in proportion to how many
impostors bother to read the pamphlet.

**Performative secrecy** is different in kind. Here the concealment is not protecting
information; it is *doing something* to the participants. A candidate who is told he
will learn something, made to wait, made to swear, and then told it, has had an
experience. The content is almost irrelevant to the experience. Anyone who has watched a
child be told a very ordinary fact under conditions of great ceremony knows that the
ceremony, not the fact, is what lands. This kind of secret is not damaged by publication
at all, because reading it in a pamphlet is not the same event as receiving it in a room.

**Boundary secrecy** is what the sociologist Georg Simmel described in 1906, in an essay
that remains the best short thing ever written on the subject. Simmel's insight was that
the secret society's secret is frequently *the fact of having one*. The content matters
less than the line it draws. A group with a boundary has an inside; people on the inside
are bound to each other by their shared position relative to the line; and the line is
maintained not by the difficulty of learning the secret but by the ritual of having been
admitted to it.[^3]

Prichard destroyed the first kind and dented the second. He could not touch the third or
the fourth, and those were the ones carrying the weight.

---

### What was actually secret

We can be fairly precise about this, because we have the texts.

The earliest surviving Masonic catechism, the Edinburgh Register House Manuscript, was
written down in 1696. It is short. It describes two degrees — Entered Apprentice and
Fellow Craft — and its contents are: a set of questions and answers about the lodge, its
form, its officers and its furnishings; a word; a sign; and a manner of giving them. Two
further Scottish texts from within about twenty years, the Chetwode Crawley and Kevan
manuscripts, agree closely.[^4]

That is the material. A word, a sign, some questions. It is not a philosophy. It is not
a cosmology. There is no doctrine in it, no theology beyond the ordinary piety of the
period, nothing that could reasonably be described as an esoteric teaching.

What there is, and what is easy to miss because we are looking for content, is
*structure*. The catechism is not an essay; it is a call and response, learned by heart,
performed between two people, in a specific order, in a specified space. The lodge is
described with an orientation and furniture. There is a form of admission. There is an
oath. There are consequences for breaking it, described in language of memorable
physical violence — and we should note in passing that the bloodcurdling penalties in the
Masonic obligations, which have provided anti-Masonic writers with three centuries of
excellent material, are symbolic formulae of a type found in other trade and legal oaths
of the period, and were never enforced by anybody.

So: what was secret was not a body of knowledge. It was a *procedure*, and membership of
the group that performed it.

This has an important consequence for everything that follows. When eighteenth- and
nineteenth-century writers began asserting that Freemasonry preserved ancient wisdom,
they were not lying about something they had seen. They were looking at a structure with
no specified content and supplying the content themselves. The lodge's silence about what
its symbols meant was not a cover for a hidden teaching. It was a vacancy — and vacancies
attract furniture.

---

### Why concealment produces authority

There is a mechanism here worth stating plainly, because it recurs in every chapter of
this book.

Concealment generates three things almost automatically.

It generates **value**. Anything withheld acquires worth in proportion to the effort
required to obtain it. A candidate who has waited, paid, been proposed, been balloted,
been blindfolded and been sworn will not conclude that what he has received is trivial.
He cannot conclude that, without concluding something unflattering about himself. This is
not manipulation; it is simply how people work, and it operates identically in
universities, guilds, professions and armies.

It generates **authority**. If I possess something you do not, and the only route to it
runs through me, I have a form of power that requires no coercion at all. Every graded
system in this book — Masonic, Rosicrucian, theosophical, magical — runs on this, and
every one of them, without exception, eventually suffers a crisis in which somebody
demands to know who is at the top and on what warrant.

And it generates **mythology**, which is the one that matters most here. An opaque
institution is a screen, and people project onto screens. What they project depends
entirely on what they already fear or hope. The anti-Masonic writer of 1798 looked at the
lodge's silence and saw revolutionary conspiracy. The Catholic controversialist of 1884
looked at the same silence and saw devil-worship. The occultist of 1888 looked at it and
saw a surviving initiatic tradition. The modern internet looks at it and sees a global
elite. In each case, the *content* of the projection tells you about the projector and
nothing about the lodge — and in each case the projection was possible only because the
institution declined to say what it meant.

Freemasonry, in other words, manufactured its own mythology as a byproduct of its
procedure. Nobody planned this. It is a consequence of the design.

---

### The problem this creates for a historian

Now the awkward part.

Secrecy does not only produce mythology. It produces holes in the evidence, and those
holes have a particular and treacherous property: they are shaped exactly like the thing
you are looking for.

If a tradition conceals its practices, then the absence of documentation for a practice
is not, by itself, evidence that the practice did not occur. That is a genuine
methodological difficulty and I do not want to wave it away. It is the strongest card in
the hand of anyone arguing for a hidden transmission, and it deserves a serious answer.

The answer has three parts.

First, **secrecy is never total, and it leaks in predictable directions**. Organisations
keep minutes. Members write letters. Rituals must be taught, which means they must be
transmitted, which means they exist somewhere in a recoverable form. Disgruntled members
publish. Governments raid. Widows sell papers. The eighteenth-century Bavarian Illuminati
were as secretive an organisation as this history contains, and we possess their internal
correspondence because the Bavarian state seized it and printed it in 1787. When an
organisation genuinely existed and genuinely operated, it almost always left a trail.
*Total* absence of evidence, over centuries, across multiple countries, is itself a fact
requiring explanation.

Second, **the claim of secrecy is testable in its own right**. If an order asserts that
it has transmitted a teaching in secret since antiquity, we cannot examine the secret —
but we can examine the assertion. When was it first made? By whom? What did they gain?
Did anyone contradict them at the time? Does the teaching contain anything that could
not have existed at the claimed date? That last question is the most powerful tool in the
book, and we will use it repeatedly. A symbol that turns on the English letter G cannot
be Egyptian. A Kabbalistic system that assigns tarot trumps to the paths of the Tree of
Life cannot predate the tarot.

Third, **the burden sits where it always sits**. A claim that something was transmitted
is a positive claim about the past. Positive claims require evidence. "You cannot prove
it did not happen in secret" is not an argument; it is the abandonment of argument, and
it would license anything whatever.

---

### The test case

Which brings us back to Prichard, and to the four decades that followed him.

Between 1723 and 1762 the Masonic secrets were published in English at least a dozen
times and in French at least as often. *A Mason's Examination* appeared in a London
newspaper in 1723. *The Grand Mystery of Free-Masons Discover'd* followed in 1724.
Prichard's *Masonry Dissected* in 1730 was the most complete and the most successful. In
France, the Abbé Pérau's *Le Secret des Francs-Maçons* appeared in 1742 and was itself a
bestseller. *Three Distinct Knocks* came in 1760, *Jachin and Boaz* in 1762 — the latter
going through edition after edition for the rest of the century.[^5]

These were not obscure documents. They were cheap, popular, widely translated, and
frequently accurate. Anyone in Europe who wanted to know what happened in a Masonic lodge
could find out for the price of a bottle of wine, at any point after 1730.

And Freemasonry's membership rose continuously through the entire period.

There is only one conclusion available. Whatever people were joining for, it was not the
information. They could have had the information for sixpence and stayed home. They
wanted the room, the ritual, the oath and the company — which is to say, they wanted
precisely the parts that a pamphlet cannot supply.

That is a fact about Freemasonry. It is also, as we will see in Part VII, a fact about
ritual in general, and it explains a great deal about why the form proved so durable and
so portable.

But the exposures are useful to us in a second way, and it is the reason this book can be
written at all. Because the secrets were published, we know what the ritual contained at
specific dates. We can watch the third degree appear. We can watch symbols enter and
others fall away. We can date things.

That capacity — to say *this element is present in 1730 and absent in 1723* — is the
difference between history and speculation in this field. In the next chapter we will use
it, and reconstruct, from those published sources, what it was actually like to be
admitted.
