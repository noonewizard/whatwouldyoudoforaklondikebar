# CHAPTER FOUR
## The Night of the Initiation

What follows is a reconstruction, and I want the reader to know exactly what kind.

Every element in it is drawn from documents that were published between 1723 and 1762 —
the exposures described in the last chapter — and from the Scottish catechisms of the
1690s. Nothing is invented. But no single source describes a whole evening, the sources
disagree with one another in places, and practice varied by lodge, by country and by
decade. What I have assembled is a composite: a plausible English ceremony of the middle
of the eighteenth century, built from published evidence, in which every individual
component is attested somewhere and the sequence is my own arrangement.

It is not a transcript. No such transcript exists. And it is not the ritual of any lodge
working today, in any jurisdiction, anywhere — those have changed considerably, and they
differ from each other more than most outsiders imagine.

With that said.

---

### Waiting

The first thing is the waiting.

He has been proposed by someone who knows him, and the lodge has voted on him in his
absence. He has been told the date. He arrives at a tavern — because in the 1740s that is
where lodges met, in an upstairs room hired for the evening, with the innkeeper's boy
guarding the stair — and he is put in a small room by himself, and the door is shut.

He can hear them. That is part of it. Muffled voices, the scrape of chairs, laughter at
something, then not laughter. Someone knocks on something. The sound of a group of men
doing something orderly on the other side of a door, while you sit alone in your
shirtsleeves, is doing work on you before anybody has said a word.

He is not in his own clothes, or not entirely. He has been asked to remove metal — coins,
buckles, anything of value — and to loosen or remove certain garments. His left breast is
bare. One knee is bare. One foot is unshod, or partly so. Accounts differ on the details
and the details differ by lodge, but the principle is constant across all of them: he is
*not properly dressed*, and he knows it, and he is about to be taken somewhere in that
condition.

Then he is blindfolded. A "hoodwink," the sources call it.

And then a cord is put around his neck: the cable-tow.

---

### The door

Consider what has been arranged, purely as a matter of engineering.

He cannot see. He is partly undressed in a room full of clothed men. He has a rope around
his neck, held by someone else. He has been stripped of money and valuables, which means
he has nothing to offer and nothing to defend. He does not know what is about to happen.
And he has come here voluntarily, at the invitation of a friend, which means he cannot
easily interpret his own fear as sensible.

Every one of those conditions is a documented element of the eighteenth-century
ceremony, and every one of them is a well-understood instrument for producing heightened
attention. Whoever assembled this — and we will spend Part III trying to work out who —
either understood that very well or arrived at it by a long process of practical
refinement. I incline to the second, but it makes little difference to the effect.

Somebody knocks on the door on his behalf. The door opens. A voice asks who comes here.

He does not answer; his guide answers for him. He is described to the room in the third
person while standing in it — a poor candidate in a state of darkness, who comes of his
own free will, properly prepared, humbly soliciting.

And then, in most of the printed versions, something sharp is presented to his naked
breast. A compass point, or a sword. He cannot see it. He can feel it.

The question he is asked, at that moment, is whether he comes of his own free will.

He says yes. Of course he says yes. But he says it with a point against his chest, in the
dark, and the *yes* he gives is not the same yes he gave his friend over dinner three
weeks earlier.

---

### The journey

He is led around the room. Not once — three times, or more, depending on the degree and
the working, always in the same direction, always with the same pauses. At each cardinal
point something happens: a question, a knock, a challenge.

This is the part that looks most arbitrary to a modern reader and is in fact the most
consequential.

He cannot see. Therefore the only map he is acquiring is a map of movement — turns,
distances, the direction of voices, the sound of his own bare foot on boards. He is
learning the room with his body before he ever learns it with his eyes. When the blindfold
finally comes off, the space will already be familiar in a way he cannot account for, and
the sensation of recognising a place he has never seen is itself part of what the ceremony
produces.

He is also, without being told so, being taught that the room has an order. There is an
east and a west. There are stations. There are people at them with authority. He learns
the shape of the lodge's hierarchy by walking it, in the dark, before anyone explains it.

---

### The obligation

He is brought to the centre and made to kneel.

The posture is specified — one knee bare and bent, one foot in a particular position, the
hands placed on an open book with a square and a pair of compasses laid upon it. He
cannot see any of this. It is arranged for him.

And then he repeats, phrase by phrase after the Master, an oath.

He swears to conceal and never reveal. He swears it under penalties that are described in
graphic anatomical detail, and which, as noted in the last chapter, nobody has ever
carried out and which belong to a recognisable family of early-modern oath formulae. He
promises to aid a brother. He promises not to wrong one.

Then he kisses the book.

I want to note something about this moment that the exposures cannot capture and that
ritual theory can. He has just made a serious promise, aloud, in a room full of witnesses,
while kneeling, blindfolded, in a state of ritual undress, with his hands on a Bible.
Whatever he thinks about the content of the promise, the *act* of making it under those
conditions is not easily reversible. He has committed himself with his voice and his body.
People do not readily walk that back.

Arnold van Gennep, writing in 1909 about rites of passage in dozens of cultures, called
this phase *liminality* — from *limen*, a threshold. The candidate is no longer what he
was and not yet what he will be. Victor Turner, half a century later, observed that
people in this condition are characteristically stripped of status, made equal to one
another, and unusually open to whatever the ritual is about to give them.[^1]

I offer those two names as a lens, not as a proof, and I want to be careful here. Van
Gennep and Turner were describing other material, and neither had eighteenth-century
London in mind. They give us a vocabulary for what this design does. They do not license
us to say what any particular man felt. I do not know what the man kneeling on those
boards felt. Nobody does, because he did not write it down — and the very few initiates
who did write about it afterwards tended to write in the formulae the ritual had just
taught them, which is a problem of its own.

---

### Light

And then, on a word from the Master, the blindfold comes off.

Everything about the preceding forty minutes has been arranged so that this moment lands.
He has been in the dark for the better part of an hour. The room is lit by candles.
There are men standing around him, in aprons, in an arrangement he half recognises
because he has walked it. In front of him is the book, and on the book a square and a
pair of compasses.

And he is told — the phrasing varies, but the substance is constant — that these are the
lights by which he is to work: one to guide his faith, one to measure his actions, one to
keep his desires within bounds.

That is the secret. That is what an hour of darkness, a rope, a blade at the chest and a
bloodcurdling oath have been protecting.

It is, viewed one way, absurdly slight. A book and two draughtsman's tools and a piece of
moral advice that any preacher would give for free.

Viewed another way, it is the entire mechanism of this book in a single image. The
information is nothing. The information was never the point. What the ceremony has done
is take an ordinary moral commonplace and make it *unforgettable*, by arranging for it to
arrive at the end of an hour engineered to produce maximum receptivity, in a room the
candidate has learned with his body, from men to whom he has just sworn an oath, at the
instant his sight is restored.

He will remember that square and those compasses for the rest of his life. He will see
them on buildings and on gravestones and on watch-fobs and they will mean something to
him that they do not mean to the person beside him. Fifty years later he will be able to
describe the moment the blindfold came off.

That is not information transfer. That is something else, and the rest of this book is an
attempt to work out what to call it.

---

### What is not there

Before leaving the room, it is worth listing what a reader arriving from the conspiracy
literature might expect to find and does not.

There is no invocation of any spirit, angel or demon. There is no magic circle. There are
no barbarous names of power, no planetary hours, no talismans, no seals. There is no
Kabbalah. There is nothing Egyptian. There is no reference to the Knights Templar. There
is nothing that a Christian clergyman of the period would have found doctrinally alarming,
and a good many Christian clergymen of the period were members.

The furniture is a Bible, a square, and a pair of compasses. The vocabulary is the
building trade and the Old Testament. The moral content would not embarrass a
schoolmaster.

And yet the *structure* — darkness, threshold, ordeal, oath, revelation, new status — is
the structure that will be picked up, a hundred and fifty years later, by men who will
fill it with invocation, planetary hours, Hebrew letters, Egyptian god-names and every
grimoire they can lay hands on.

They did not find that content in the lodge. They brought it. The lodge gave them
somewhere to put it.

---

### A caution, stated once

I have described this ceremony at length because the reader cannot follow the rest of the
argument without knowing what a Masonic initiation actually consisted of. But I want to
repeat the warning I began with, in stronger terms.

This is a reconstruction from eighteenth-century printed sources. It is not a description
of any modern working. Present-day rituals differ substantially between England,
Scotland, Ireland, France, the American jurisdictions, and everywhere else — and the
differences are not trivial. Some elements described above have been softened, some
removed, some were never used in some countries at all. A Mason reading this chapter may
recognise a great deal and may also find things that have never formed part of his
experience. Both reactions would be correct.

The point of the exercise is not to expose anything. There is nothing here that has not
been in print for two hundred and sixty years. The point is to establish, from the
documentary record, what kind of machine we are dealing with — because in about twelve
chapters' time we are going to watch people start rebuilding it, and we need to know what
they were working from.

Which leaves one more piece of equipment to install. We know what the ceremony was. We
know what was secret and what was not. What we do not yet have is a way of testing the
enormous claims that would later be made about where all this came from.

That is the next chapter, and it is the most useful thing in the book.
