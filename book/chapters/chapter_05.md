# CHAPTER FIVE
## How to Tell a Tradition from a Story

In 1614 a Swiss scholar named Isaac Casaubon, working in London on a commission from
King James, published a demolition.

He had been reading the *Corpus Hermeticum*: a body of Greek philosophical dialogues
attributed to Hermes Trismegistus, an Egyptian sage whom Renaissance Europe believed had
lived in the time of Moses, or possibly before him. For a hundred and forty years these
texts had been read as the oldest theology in the world, a pagan revelation that
anticipated Christianity, evidence that God had spoken to Egypt as well as to Israel.
Marsilio Ficino had interrupted his translation of Plato to render them into Latin first,
because his patron judged them more urgent. They had reshaped European thought about
nature, the soul, magic and the antiquity of wisdom.

Casaubon read them in Greek and noticed that they were not old.

His argument was philological and it was devastating. The vocabulary was wrong: these
dialogues used Greek words that did not exist in the archaic period. The style was wrong:
it was the style of late antique philosophical prose, not of anything from the second
millennium before Christ. The content was wrong: the texts quoted or paraphrased Plato,
which a contemporary of Moses could not have done, and showed the marks of Jewish and
Christian ideas. And the transmission was wrong: there was no manuscript tradition
carrying these texts back into deep Egyptian antiquity, only Greek copies of the Roman
imperial period.

His conclusion — which no competent scholar has ever overturned, and which modern
editors state as a matter of course — was that the *Corpus Hermeticum* was written in
Greek, in Roman Egypt, somewhere between the first and third centuries of the Christian
era.[^1]

Not ancient. Contemporary with the Gospels.

---

Casaubon's demonstration is the founding case study of this book, and not because of what
it proved. It is the founding case because of what happened next, which was very nearly
nothing.

The Hermetic texts went on being read as ancient wisdom. They went on being cited as
Egyptian. Two hundred and fifty years later, in 1871, Albert Pike would be building
elaborate arguments on the antiquity of the Hermetic tradition. Two hundred and seventy
years later the founders of the Golden Dawn would name their order after it. Today you
can buy a book, published this year, that describes the *Corpus Hermeticum* as
pre-Mosaic.

A tradition can survive the destruction of its own credentials, indefinitely, if enough
people need it. That is a fact about human institutions and it is not a criticism of
anybody in particular. But it means that a historian who wants to know whether a claimed
transmission actually occurred cannot rely on how widely it is believed, how old the
belief is, how sincere the believers are, or how much sense it makes. The belief and the
transmission are separate objects, and they must be investigated separately.

This chapter is about how.

---

### The six questions

Here is the apparatus. It is not original to me — it is standard historical method,
assembled for this particular job — and it consists of six questions, applied in order.
Whenever this book confronts a claim that something old was carried into something newer,
these are the questions it asks.

**1. Provenance. Where is the physical object?**

Not "where is the tradition said to have come from" — where is the manuscript, the
letter, the minute book, the printed edition? Who has held it since? What is its
catalog number? A claim supported by a document nobody can point to is not supported.

This question alone disposes of a startling proportion of the literature. The Golden
Dawn's authority rested on correspondence with a German adept named Anna Sprengel. Where
are the letters? They were produced, and examined, and they are wrong — the German is
poor in ways a German adept's would not be, and the addresses do not check out.[^2] The
Rite of Strict Observance rested on the authority of Unknown Superiors. Where is the
warrant? Baron von Hund spent thirty years being asked and never produced one.

**2. Chronology. Does the timeline permit it?**

Astonishingly often, it does not. This is the cheapest test available and it is skipped
constantly. If a claim requires an organization to have influenced something that
happened before the organization existed, the claim is finished, and no amount of
symbolic resonance will repair it.

**3. Transmission. Can you name the route?**

A person, a text, a translation, a meeting, a purchase, a theft — something. This is the
question this book asks most often, because it is the one the literature most reliably
cannot answer. *Availability is not transmission.* That Agrippa's *De occulta philosophia*
was in print and purchasable in seventeenth-century England tells us an English Mason
*could* have read it. It does not tell us that one did, and it certainly does not tell us
that what he read entered the ritual.

The corrective method here is one I will use repeatedly, and I take it from the
historian Alison Butler, who applied it to the Golden Dawn. Instead of asking what ideas
were in the air, she reconstructed what books were physically on the shelves — the actual
catalogd contents of the founders' libraries.[^3] That is transmission evidence. Ideas
in the air are not.

**4. Intertextuality. Does the later source use the earlier one?**

Resemblance is weak. Quotation is strong. Paraphrase is strong. Shared idiosyncratic
error is strongest of all — if two texts make the same odd mistake, one of them copied
the other.

This is the test that settled the question of Albert Pike's sources. For a century it was
possible to argue about how much of *Morals and Dogma* was Pike's own thought. Then
Arturo de Hoyos went through it line by line against the books Pike had been reading, and
demonstrated that large tracts of it are unattributed transcription, substantially from
Éliphas Lévi.[^4] That is not an opinion about influence. That is a collation.

**5. Institutional continuity. Did the organization actually persist?**

Organizations that exist do things. They collect dues, keep lists, rent rooms, quarrel,
split, and leave paper. A claim of unbroken institutional descent across a gap of
centuries, with no dues, no lists, no rooms and no quarrels, is a claim that an
organization existed in a manner unlike every other organization in recorded history.

The Templars were suppressed between 1307 and 1312 and their last Grand Master was burned
in 1314. Freemasonry's first documented grand lodge dates from the 1720s. That is a gap of
four centuries. Something can be asserted across such a gap; nothing can be demonstrated
across it without evidence from inside it.

**6. Anachronism. Does the claim contain something that could not yet exist?**

This is the sharpest instrument in the set, because it can kill a claim outright with a
single observation.

Some examples that will recur. A Masonic symbol whose meaning depends on the English
letter G cannot have originated in a culture that did not use the Roman alphabet. A
Kabbalistic system that assigns the tarot trumps to the paths of the Tree of Life cannot
predate the tarot, which is a fifteenth-century Italian card game. An order claiming
Rosicrucian descent from before 1614 is claiming descent from a document that had not been
written. A ritual that uses the Authorised Version's English cannot be older than 1611.

Anachronism detection requires knowing when things were invented, which is tedious and
unglamorous and is most of what historical training consists of.

---

### The rule

From those six questions follows one rule, which I am going to state in its own paragraph
because it is the single most important sentence in this book after the thesis itself.

**A parallel is not a descent.**

If a Masonic symbol resembles an Egyptian one, that is a fact about the two symbols. To
convert it into a fact about history, you need a route — questions three and four above.
Without a route, what you have is a resemblance, and resemblances are cheap. There are
only so many shapes. Human beings across all cultures have used light for knowledge,
darkness for ignorance, height for authority, the circle for completeness, the square for
stability, and death-and-return for transformation. These are not proprietary. They recur
because human bodies and human societies recur.

The dying-and-rising figure is a useful demonstration. Freemasonry's third degree centers
on the murder and raising of a master builder. Osiris is killed and restored. Adonis dies
and returns. Christ is crucified and rises. Alchemical texts describe a putrefaction
followed by a rebirth. The pattern is everywhere.

Does that mean Freemasonry took its central legend from Egypt?

Ask the six questions. Provenance: where is the document connecting them? None. Chronology:
possible in principle, since Egypt is older. Transmission: name the route — nobody can.
Intertextuality: does the Masonic legend quote or paraphrase any Egyptian source? No; its
materials are biblical and drawn from the craft's own medieval legends. Institutional
continuity: none whatsoever. Anachronism: the legend first appears in print in 1730, and
the Egyptology that would have made a conscious borrowing possible did not exist in Europe
until after Napoleon's expedition of 1798.

The parallel is real. The descent is unsupported. Both of those statements are true at
once, and holding both is the discipline this book is asking the reader to acquire.

### Why traditions get invented

One more thing before we begin the history proper, because the six questions tell you
*whether* something was invented and not *why*, and the why is genuinely interesting.

In 1983 the historians Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger published a book arguing that a
great many traditions that present themselves as immemorial are in fact recent, often
strikingly recent, and that they tend to be manufactured in periods of rapid social
change, by people who need continuity with a past that is slipping.[^5] Scottish clan
tartans, most royal ceremonial, a good deal of what looks like folk custom: eighteenth
and nineteenth century, frequently with identifiable authors.

The eighteenth century, which is the century in which almost everything in this book was
built, was exactly such a period. Old structures of religious authority had broken; new
ones had not consolidated. A man who wanted to belong to something with roots had to find
something with roots. And if he could not find one, the period was remarkably willing to
supply the deficiency.

This is not, mostly, fraud. That distinction matters and I want to make it carefully.
When Andrew Michael Ramsay told an audience of French Masons in 1737 that their order
descended from crusader knights, he was not necessarily lying; he may well have believed
a version of it, and he was certainly performing a recognized eighteenth-century genre in
which an institution's dignity was established by giving it an antique genealogy. When
William Wynn Westcott produced letters from a German adept who did not exist, that was
something closer to fraud — but even there, the fraud was in the service of a conviction
he appears genuinely to have held, that the material he was working with was real and
merely needed a pedigree in order to be taken seriously.

The historian Anthony Grafton, in a short and brilliant book on forgery in Western
scholarship, observed that forgers and critics have always developed together: each
advance in the art of fabrication provokes an advance in the art of detection, and the
tools scholars use to catch fakes were largely built by people trying to catch fakes.[^6]
Casaubon's philology is one of those tools. So is everything in this chapter.

---

We now have what we need.

We know what happened at Warrington in 1646, and how little it tells us. We know what the
word *magic* has meant, and how violently it has changed. We know what was secret in a
lodge and why publishing it changed nothing. We know what an eighteenth-century initiation
consisted of. And we have six questions and one rule.

So we can go back to the beginning — the real beginning, which is not a lodge and not a
temple and not a pyramid, but a set of Greek dialogues written in Roman Egypt at about
the time the Gospel of John was being composed, which a Florentine priest would translate
into Latin in 1471 under the impression that he was recovering the oldest wisdom in the
world.

Europe would believe him for a hundred and forty-three years.

Some people still do.
