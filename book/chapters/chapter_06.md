# PART II — BEFORE THE LODGE

# CHAPTER SIX
## Hermetic Egypt

Cosimo de' Medici was dying, and he wanted something read to him first.

In 1463 a monk named Leonardo da Pistoia arrived in Florence from Macedonia with a bundle
of Greek manuscripts he had bought for the Medici collection. Among them was a codex
containing fourteen short philosophical dialogues attributed to Hermes Trismegistus.
Cosimo had, at that point, a scholar in his household engaged on the largest translation
project in Europe: Marsilio Ficino was rendering the complete works of Plato into Latin
for the first time.

Cosimo told him to stop and do the Hermetic texts first.

Ficino did. He finished the translation in a few months and it was printed in Treviso in
1471 under the title *Pimander*. It went through sixteen editions before the century was
out — more than the Plato.[^1]

Most of the intellectual history of Western esotericism begins with that interruption.

---

### What the texts actually say

It is worth pausing on the content, because it is frequently invoked and rarely
described.

The *Corpus Hermeticum* is not a system. It is an anthology of seventeen treatises
(fourteen in Ficino's codex, the rest recovered later), written by different hands, not
always in agreement. They take the form of teachings delivered by Hermes Trismegistus —
"thrice-greatest Hermes," the Greek name for the Egyptian god Thoth — to his son Tat, or
to a disciple named Asclepius, or received by Hermes himself from a divine mind.

The first and most famous treatise, *Poimandres*, describes a vision. A great being
appears to the narrator and shows him the origin of things: light, then a descending
darkness, then the formation of the cosmos by a divine Mind. Humanity is made in the image
of that Mind, falls into matter through love of its own reflection, and is therefore
double — mortal in body, immortal in essence. The purpose of human life is to recognise
this, and to ascend.

The ascent is described concretely. The soul rises through the spheres of the seven
planets, and at each one it surrenders something: at one sphere, the capacity to grow and
diminish; at another, the machinery of desire; at another, ambition; at another, arrogance;
at another, recklessness. Stripped of these, it enters the eighth sphere with the powers
that remain and becomes, in the text's phrase, god.

Elsewhere in the collection: the world is alive and good, or in one treatise the world is
the fullness of evil; God is knowable, or God is beyond knowing; matter is a prison, or
matter is the beautiful garment of the divine. The anthology contradicts itself
constantly, because it is an anthology.

Two ideas run through nearly all of it, and they are the two that will travel.

The first is **correspondence**: that the human being is a small model of the cosmos, that
what is above answers to what is below, and that this answering is not metaphor but
structure. The formula appears in the *Emerald Tablet*, a short Hermetic text of Arabic
transmission that became the most quoted sentence in the history of alchemy: *as above,
so below*.

The second is **ascent through knowledge**: that a person can be transformed, in this
life, by understanding — and that the transformation is graded, staged, passing through
levels, each of which requires the surrender of something belonging to the level below.

Hold that second idea in mind. It will be sitting in a London lodge room in 1888, wearing
different clothes.

---

### Was any of it Egyptian?

Here is where the discipline of the last chapter earns its keep.

The texts present themselves as Egyptian. They are set in Egypt; their protagonist is a
Hellenised Egyptian god; one famous passage in the Latin *Asclepius* laments the coming
end of Egyptian religion in terms so moving that Renaissance readers wept over it.

They were also written in Greek, in the Roman period, by people steeped in Platonism and
Stoicism, and they show unmistakable contact with Jewish scripture. Casaubon established
this in 1614 and it has never been seriously disputed.

But "not ancient Egyptian" is not the same as "not Egyptian at all," and the honest answer
is more interesting than either slogan. Garth Fowden's study of the Hermetic literature
argues for a genuinely Egyptian milieu: Greek-speaking, Hellenised, but located in Egypt,
drawing on native priestly traditions as well as on Greek philosophy, produced by a
society in which both were available.[^2] The *Corpus* is not a translation of pharaonic
scripture. It is a late antique Egyptian product, written by people who lived where the
temples were and who knew what was in them.

So the accurate statement — and this book will be pedantic about it, because everything
downstream depends on it — is: **the Hermetic texts are Greek philosophical writings of
the Roman era, produced in a Hellenised Egyptian environment, which Renaissance Europe
mistook for scripture of the second millennium before Christ.**

The mistake was not stupid. There was no philology capable of catching it until Casaubon
built one. And the mistake was enormously productive: it gave Europe permission to take
pagan wisdom seriously, because if Hermes had written before Moses, or alongside him, then
this was not paganism but a parallel revelation.

---

### The doctrine that made everything possible

That permission had a name, and the name matters more than any individual doctrine in this
book.

Ficino and his circle called it *prisca theologia* — ancient theology. The claim was that
God had disclosed a single truth at the dawn of things, and that this truth had been
carried down through a chain of sages: Hermes Trismegistus, then Orpheus, then Pythagoras,
then Plato. Different names, different languages, one revelation. Pico della Mirandola
would add the Kabbalah to the chain, arguing that Moses had received an oral teaching
alongside the written law. Later writers would add the Druids, the Brahmins, the Chaldeans,
and anybody else who could be made to fit.

Look at the structure of that claim, because it is the load-bearing wall of everything
this book examines.

It says: *the truth is old, it is one, it is scattered across traditions in disguised
forms, and a sufficiently learned person can recognise it beneath the disguises.*

Every element of that is a licence. If the truth is old, then novelty is suspect and
antiquity is authority. If it is one, then any resemblance between two traditions is
evidence of their common source rather than a coincidence. If it is disguised, then the
absence of explicit evidence is exactly what you would expect. And if only the learned can
see it, then disagreement is a symptom of the objector's insufficiency.

That is a framework in which no claim can fail. It is the precise opposite of the six
questions in Chapter 5, and it was the dominant intellectual mode of European esotericism
for four centuries. When Albert Pike assembles comparative mythology in 1871, when
Blavatsky assembles world religions in 1877, when a modern writer notes that the Masonic
square resembles an Egyptian set-square and treats the resemblance as proof — they are all
operating inside *prisca theologia*, usually without knowing its name.

I want to be fair to it. It produced real scholarship: comparative religion, comparative
mythology and eventually Indo-European linguistics all grew out of the impulse to find the
single source. And it produced genuine intellectual generosity — Ficino's willingness to
find truth in a pagan Egyptian is not nothing, in a century that burned people for less.

But as a method for establishing what actually happened, it is worthless, because it
cannot distinguish between a real connection and a resemblance. It was designed not to.

---

### What this has to do with a lodge

Nothing, yet. That is the point, and I want to state it here because the temptation to
jump the gap is enormous.

In 1471 there is no Freemasonry. There are masons' lodges in Scotland and England — sheds
by building sites, trade organisations with customs and legends — and there is nothing in
them that connects to Ficino's Florence in any documented way. The Old Charges of the
English masons, which had been circulating for eighty years by then, do contain a mythic
history of the craft, and that history does reach back to Egypt: it has the seven liberal
sciences preserved on two pillars before the Flood, and Abraham teaching geometry to the
Egyptians, and Euclid instructing the sons of Egyptian nobility in the mason's art.[^3]

That is a real Egyptian reference in a real Masonic document of the fourteenth century.
It is also, demonstrably, drawn from medieval chronicle traditions and biblical
commentary — Josephus, and the standard medieval universal histories — and not from any
Hermetic source. It is the ordinary learned furniture of the period, not an esoteric
inheritance.

The two Egypts — the mason's legendary Egypt of Euclid and geometry, and the Hermetic
Egypt of Thoth and the ascent of the soul — are in Europe at the same time, in different
rooms, doing different jobs, for people who did not know each other.

They will be fused. It will happen in the eighteenth century, and we will be able to name
some of the people who did it. But it has not happened yet, and any account that lets it
happen early is telling you a story rather than a history.

---

### The residue

So what does the Hermetic literature actually contribute to what follows?

Three things, and they are substantial.

It contributes **a vocabulary of correspondence** that will be the working language of
Renaissance magic and, four centuries later, of the Golden Dawn's entire system of
attributions.

It contributes **the model of graded ascent** — the soul rising through levels, shedding
something at each — which is the conceptual shape of every initiatory hierarchy in this
book, whether or not the people building them had read a word of Hermes.

And it contributes **the authority of the ancient**, the *prisca theologia*, the assumption
that old is true and that resemblance is descent. That is the assumption this book exists
to take apart, and it is worth knowing that it entered Europe through a translation that a
dying banker asked for out of turn.

Before we can follow the vocabulary forward, though, we need the other half of it. The
Hermetic texts describe an ascent but do not explain how the cosmos is arranged to permit
one. That architecture — the graded universe, emanating downward from a source, with
ritual as the technique for climbing back — was built by Greek philosophers in the third
and fourth centuries, and one of them invented a word for what he was doing precisely in
order to insist that it was not magic.
