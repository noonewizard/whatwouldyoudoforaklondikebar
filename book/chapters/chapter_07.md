# CHAPTER SEVEN
## The Ladder of Emanations

Some time in the middle of the third century, in Rome, a philosopher from Egypt who
refused to tell anyone his birthday or sit for a portrait began teaching that reality
overflows.

Plotinus's position, worked out in the essays his student Porphyry collected as the
*Enneads*, was that everything proceeds from a single source — he called it the One, and
insisted it could not properly be named or described at all, since naming implies
distinction and the One is prior to distinction. From the One comes Intellect, the realm
of pure forms. From Intellect comes Soul. From Soul comes the sensible world, and at the
far end of the sequence, matter, which is not so much a thing as the point at which the
overflow runs out.

The crucial word is *emanation*. Plotinus was careful that this is not creation in the
biblical sense — nothing is decided, nothing is made. It is more like radiance: the sun
does not choose to shine, and the light at the edge of its reach is not a separate
substance but the same thing, attenuated.

Which means that everything in the universe is continuous with its source, arranged in
descending order, and — this is the part that will matter for the next fifteen hundred
years — the sequence can be run backward.

---

### The architecture

It is worth seeing what that geometry provides, because it is provided to everyone who
comes afterward.

You get **levels**. The cosmos is not flat. It has an order of dignity, and things occupy
positions in it.

You get **continuity**. Because everything descends from the same source, every level
answers to every other. The Hermetic doctrine of correspondence acquires, in Neoplatonism,
a mechanism.

You get **a direction**. Down is dispersal into multiplicity; up is return to unity. The
soul's proper motion is upward.

And you get **the possibility of ascent**. Since the soul is itself an emanation, it
retains a relation to what is above it. Plotinus thought this ascent was achieved by
philosophy and contemplation: by turning inward and upward through disciplined thought
until the soul touches its source. Porphyry reports that his teacher attained this union
four times in the years they knew each other, and reports it in the tone of a man
recording something remarkable and slightly embarrassing.

That is the Plotinian package: a graded cosmos, ascended by thinking.

Within a century, a disagreement would split it open, and the disagreement is the
ancestor of everything in this book.

---

### Iamblichus and the invention of theurgy

Iamblichus of Chalcis, active in the early fourth century, was a Neoplatonist who thought
Plotinus and Porphyry had got something fundamentally wrong.

The argument, set out in the treatise we call *On the Mysteries*, runs roughly like this.
The human soul is not, as Plotinus held, partly undescended — it does not keep one foot in
the Intellect. It has come the whole way down. It is thoroughly embodied, thoroughly
enmeshed in matter, and therefore *it cannot think its way home*. Contemplation is a human
activity, and human activity operates at the human level. No amount of it will cross a
boundary that is ontological rather than intellectual.

What can cross that boundary is action initiated from above.

Iamblichus argued that the gods had placed, throughout the material world, tokens and
signatures — *sunthēmata*, symbols — which are not representations of higher realities but
literal extensions of them, deposited in matter. A particular stone, plant, scent, sound or
name is not a picture of a divine power. It is that power's presence at the material level.
And ritual conducted with the correct tokens does not persuade or compel the divine; it
supplies the conditions under which the divine, which is always already acting, can act
*here*.

He called this *theourgia*: god-working, as against *theologia*, god-talking. And he was
emphatic, at length, and with considerable heat, that it was categorically distinct from
*goēteia* — sorcery, the manipulation of powers for private ends by people who do not
understand what they are handling.[^1]

Two features of this position deserve attention, because both will recur on nearly every
page that follows.

**First: ritual is epistemology.** Iamblichus is not saying that ritual is a helpful
supplement to philosophy. He is saying that there are things which cannot be known by
thinking and can be known by doing — that certain kinds of knowledge are available only
through performed action, and are not reducible to propositions afterward. This is the
oldest and most rigorous statement in the Western tradition of the claim that initiation
transmits something a book cannot.

Whether or not one believes his metaphysics, that claim about the relation of doing to
knowing is exactly the claim a Masonic lodge makes structurally, without ever arguing for
it, fifteen hundred years later.

**Second: the practitioner denies that it is magic.** Iamblichus performs ritual with
material tokens and divine names to produce contact with non-human intelligences, and
spends a great deal of the treatise explaining that this is not what the magicians do.

Watch for this. Almost everyone in this book does it. The eighteenth-century theurgist
Martinez de Pasqually will insist his operations are religious, not magical. Éliphas Lévi
will distinguish *haute magie* from the superstition of sorcerers. The Golden Dawn will
distinguish its work from that of charlatans. Aleister Crowley will respell the word to
distinguish his from stage conjuring. Freemasonry, when accused of magic, will deny it
absolutely — and will be telling the truth as it understands the term.

The denial is not evasion. It is a real claim about categories. But it does mean that
"did they call it magic?" is a nearly useless question, and that anyone using
self-description as a criterion will get the history wrong in both directions.

### The ladder as a technology

Now the observation this chapter exists to make.

A graded cosmos implies graded access. If reality has levels, and the soul ascends through
them, then a tradition that undertakes to assist the ascent will have levels too. Its
teaching will be staged. Its practitioners will occupy positions. There will be those
further up and those further down, and the further up will know things the further down do
not, and will decide when they are ready.

That is not a mystical proposition. It is an organizational consequence of a cosmological
premise, and it produces an identical institutional shape every single time, regardless of
content.

Consider what the shape delivers. It gives a candidate a map of his own progress. It gives
an institution a mechanism for controlling information, and therefore for controlling
people. It converts the abstract idea of spiritual development into something with a
scoreboard. And it generates authority automatically, because the question "who is
qualified to advance me?" has only one possible answer.

Every system in this book has this shape. The Masonic three degrees have it. The
eighteenth-century high degrees have it to an almost comic extreme — thirty-three of them
in the Scottish Rite, ninety-odd in some of the nineteenth-century rites of Memphis and
Misraim. The Gold- und Rosenkreuz has it. The Golden Dawn has it, mapped explicitly onto
the Kabbalistic Tree of Life. Crowley's A∴A∴ has it. Theosophy has it in the form of the
Masters.

Did they all get it from Iamblichus?

Almost certainly not, and this is exactly the discipline of Chapter 5 in action. The
graded structure is available from too many directions to be traced to one: from
Neoplatonism, yes, but also from the church's own hierarchy of orders, from the guild
sequence of apprentice-journeyman-master, from military rank, from the universities'
degrees, and from the plain practical observation that you cannot teach everything at
once.

What we can say — and this is both weaker and more useful than a claim of descent — is
that the Neoplatonic ladder gave Europe a *cosmological justification* for a structure it
would have built anyway for practical reasons. The apprentice becomes a master because
that is how a trade works. The initiate ascends through degrees because that is how the
universe is shaped. When those two rationales met, in a masons' lodge that had stopped
containing masons, the result was a machine that could be read in either register, by
different members, at the same time, without either of them being wrong.

That is not a small thing. It may be the single most important structural fact about
Freemasonry, and it explains why the same institution could satisfy a Whig physicist in
1723 and a theurgist in 1761.

### What survived, and how

The Neoplatonic inheritance reached the Renaissance by a route worth noting briefly,
because it is a real transmission and we should point at one when we have it.

Plotinus, Porphyry, Iamblichus and Proclus were read by Christian writers who adapted
their structure wholesale. The anonymous author who wrote under the name Dionysius the
Areopagite — sometime around the year 500 — produced a Christian angelology arranged in
three triads of three, an ecclesiastical hierarchy mirroring it, and a theology of ascent
through unknowing, all of it Neoplatonic architecture in Christian dress. Because he was
believed to be the Athenian convert named in the Book of Acts, his authority was nearly
apostolic, and his hierarchies became standard medieval furniture.[^2]

So the ladder entered Christian Europe not as pagan philosophy but as orthodox theology,
under a false name, and stayed. When Ficino translated Plotinus in the 1480s — after the
Hermetica, after the Plato — he was not introducing something alien. He was supplying the
originals of a structure Europe had been using for a thousand years.

That is what a documented transmission looks like: named texts, named translators, datable
editions, a traceable route, and a mechanism of error we can specify. It took five
sentences. When an equivalent route exists for a claim in this book, I will give it in five
sentences. When I do not give it, it is because it does not exist.

Which brings us to Florence, and to a priest-physician who thought he could adjust a man's
temperament by arranging his room, and to his brilliant, reckless young friend who in 1486
proposed to defend nine hundred theses against all comers and got himself condemned by the
Pope.
