# CHAPTER EIGHT
## Ficino, Pico, and the Renaissance Turn

In 1489 Marsilio Ficino published a book of medical advice for intellectuals.

*De vita libri tres* — *Three Books on Life* — was addressed to scholars, and its
diagnosis was that they are a sickly population. They sit still. They think too hard. They
are dried out and cooled by the labour of study, which makes them melancholic, and
melancholy is governed by Saturn, the cold and heavy planet.

The first two books offer the sort of advice you would expect: diet, exercise, sleep,
moderation, the avoidance of night work. Sensible physician's counsel, some of it still
good.

The third book proposes that the scholar can improve his condition by drawing down the
influence of the Sun.

Not metaphorically. Ficino's instructions are practical. Surround yourself with solar
things: gold, saffron, and the colour yellow; heliotrope and other plants that follow the
sun; cockerels, which announce it. Take solar substances internally. Arrange your room to
admit morning light. And — the recommendation that has fascinated everyone who has read
him since — make music in the Solar mode, sing hymns to the sun, accompany yourself on a
lyre, and do it at the right astrological hour.[^1]

This is astral magic, published by a Catholic priest, dedicated to a prince of the
Church, printed under his own name, in the most culturally conspicuous city in Europe.

Ficino was nervous about it. He inserted disclaimers. He explained that he was describing
natural properties rather than invoking spirits, that the influence being drawn was a
physical emanation like heat or light rather than a personal power, that he was reporting
what ancient authorities said rather than recommending it. He got into some trouble
anyway, though less than he might have. But he published, and the book was read across
Europe for a century and a half.

The significance is not that a Renaissance priest believed in astrology — nearly everyone
did, including his critics. The significance is the *frame* he built to make operative
magic intellectually respectable.

---

### The frame

Ficino's argument, assembled from the Hermetic and Neoplatonic materials of the last two
chapters, runs like this.

The cosmos is a continuous, living whole, emanating from its source, arranged in levels
that correspond. Between the soul of the world and the bodies in it there is a mediating
substance — the *spiritus*, a fine vapour, the same stuff that mediates between soul and
body in a human being. Because the correspondences are real, and because the mediating
substance is real, the influence of a higher level can be concentrated at a lower one by
assembling things that share its quality.

Nothing in that sequence requires a spirit to be summoned or a demon to be bargained with.
It requires only that the universe be the kind of thing the Hermetic and Platonic texts
say it is. Magic becomes a branch of natural philosophy — the applied end of a metaphysics
everybody already accepted.

That move is the Renaissance turn. It is what separates the fifteenth century from the
medieval grimoire tradition, in which a magician stood in a circle and compelled named
spirits and knew perfectly well he was doing something the Church forbade. Ficino's magic
is not forbidden, because on his account it is not addressed to anybody. It is gardening.

Whether the distinction survives contact with his actual recommendations — those hymns,
sung at those hours, to that planet — has been debated since the 1490s. Ficino himself
seems to have been uneasy. But the *frame* held, and it is the frame, not the practice,
that travels.

Four hundred years later, the Golden Dawn will construct an enormous system of
correspondences — colours, plants, metals, perfumes, god-names, tarot cards — on exactly
Ficino's logic, and will insist, exactly as he did, that what it is doing is a science of
nature rather than a traffic with spirits. And they will have read him, or read people who
had.

---

### The boy who wanted to argue with everyone

Ficino's younger associate was a different kind of person entirely.

Giovanni Pico della Mirandola was a count, extremely wealthy, extremely clever, and
twenty-three years old when he announced in 1486 that he would defend nine hundred theses
in Rome against all comers, at his own expense, and would pay the travel costs of anyone
who wished to come and dispute with him.

The theses were drawn from Latin scholastics, Greek philosophers, Arab commentators,
Hermetic writings, and — the innovation — the Jewish Kabbalah. The introductory address he
composed for the occasion, later titled *Oration on the Dignity of Man*, is among the most
anthologised texts of the Renaissance, and its famous passage has God telling Adam that he
has been given no fixed place, no fixed form and no fixed function, and may make of
himself whatever he wills.[^2]

The disputation never happened. A papal commission examined the theses, found thirteen of
them problematic, and when Pico defended himself the whole set was condemned. He fled,
was arrested in France, was rescued by Lorenzo de' Medici, and died at thirty-one,
probably poisoned.

What survives, for our purposes, is a method and a claim.

The method is **concordance**: the conviction that apparently incompatible traditions are
saying the same thing in different idioms, and that a sufficiently learned reader can
demonstrate it. Nine hundred theses from a dozen traditions, harmonised. This is the
*prisca theologia* of Chapter 6 turned into an operating procedure, and it is the direct
ancestor of every comparative-mythological synthesis in this book — Pike's above all.

The claim is the one that makes Pico matter more than his method. He asserted, in two of
the condemned theses, that no science gives greater assurance of Christ's divinity than
magic and Kabbalah.[^3]

Read that again, because it is genuinely startling. A Catholic count in 1486 is arguing
that Jewish mystical texts and operative magic are the best available *proofs of
Christianity*.

---

### What Pico thought he had found

The reasoning ran through the *prisca theologia*. If God gave a single revelation at the
beginning, and if Moses received an oral teaching alongside the written Law, then that
oral teaching — which Pico identified with the Kabbalah — must contain the original
disclosure. And if the original disclosure is true, it must, when correctly read, testify
to Christ.

Pico therefore went looking in Hebrew texts for Christian doctrine, and, with the aid of
converted Jewish scholars and a considerable amount of ingenuity, found it. Techniques of
letter-permutation and numerical equivalence which Jewish Kabbalists had developed for
their own purposes could be pointed at the name of Jesus and made to yield results.

Two things must be said about this, and the second is more important.

The first is that it is a misreading. Jewish Kabbalah is not a proto-Christian system, and
the interpretive operations Pico performed on it would have been unrecognisable to the
tradition's own practitioners. Modern scholarship on the Kabbalah — Scholem, Idel, Dan —
describes something with its own internal history, its own problems, and no relation to
the use Pico made of it.[^4]

The second is that the misreading was consequential out of all proportion. Because of
Pico, Kabbalah entered Christian European intellectual life not as an object of study but
as an *instrument*. And what entered was not Jewish Kabbalah. It was a Christian
adaptation, built for Christian purposes, using selected techniques and a selected
vocabulary, which would be transmitted through Reuchlin, then through Knorr von
Rosenroth's Latin anthology of 1677, then — at a fourth remove and in English — into the
Golden Dawn.

This is the reason Chapter 10 exists, and the reason this book is pedantic about the
difference between Kabbalah, Cabala and Qabalah. The chain is real. Every link can be
named. And the thing that comes out at the far end is not the thing that went in.

---

### The turn, stated

What the Florentines accomplished, between roughly 1471 and 1500, can be put in four
propositions. They are not doctrines so much as permissions, and each one is still
operating in the material this book covers four centuries later.

**That the oldest wisdom is the truest.** Therefore antiquity confers authority, and any
new system will want an ancient pedigree.

**That all wisdom traditions agree beneath their surfaces.** Therefore resemblance is
evidence of common source, and the harmonising of traditions is scholarship rather than
invention.

**That the cosmos is a connected system of correspondences.** Therefore operative work on
one level can affect another, and a table of attributions is a practical instrument.

**That magic, properly understood, is philosophy and not superstition.** Therefore an
educated person may do it without ceasing to be respectable.

Those four permissions are the intellectual capital that the sixteenth century will spend.

And it will spend it very fast. Within a generation of Pico's death, a young German with a
gift for synthesis and no instinct whatever for self-preservation will take all four
propositions, add the medieval grimoire tradition that Ficino had carefully excluded, and
write the single book that will carry this entire complex into the modern world — into
Elizabethan England, into Rosicrucian Germany, into a nineteenth-century coroner's private
library, and into the ritual of an order that would define Western magic for a hundred
years.

He was twenty-three when he wrote it, and he spent the rest of his life dealing with the
consequences.
