# CHAPTER NINE
## Agrippa and the Occult Philosophy

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa von Nettesheim had one of the worst careers of the sixteenth
century.

He was a soldier, a physician, a lawyer, a diplomat, a lecturer, a court astrologer, and
a municipal advocate, usually not for long. He was accused of heresy in Italy, chased out
of Metz for defending a woman on trial for witchcraft, employed and then abandoned by the
mother of a king, imprisoned for debt in Brussels, and died in his late forties in
Grenoble, probably poor.

He also wrote, at twenty-three, the book that transmitted Renaissance magic to everybody
who came after.

*De occulta philosophia libri tres* was drafted around 1509–10 and circulated in
manuscript for two decades before Agrippa allowed it into print in 1533. By then he had
also published a bitter, brilliant work called *On the Uncertainty and Vanity of the Arts
and Sciences* (1530), which attacked essentially every field of human knowledge including
magic, and which has puzzled readers ever since — did he recant, or was the attack a
rhetorical exercise, or was he simply a man capable of holding two positions at once?
Scholars still argue. He published both books, and let the contradiction stand.[^1]

It is the first one that mattered.

---

### The three books

Agrippa's organisation is his most consequential contribution, and it is elegantly simple.
The universe has three levels — elemental, celestial, intellectual — so magic has three
divisions, and he gives each a book.

**Book One: natural magic.** The elemental world. The hidden properties of stones, plants,
animals, metals; sympathies and antipathies; the four elements and their qualities; the
virtues of particular substances. This is the Ficinian material, systematised and vastly
expanded, and much of it is indistinguishable from the natural history of its day.

**Book Two: celestial magic.** The world of the stars. Here Agrippa supplies the
mathematics — proportions, harmonies, the numbers from one to twelve and everything
associated with each, the planetary magic squares, the characters and seals of the
planets, the construction of talismanic images. This book is where the tables are, and the
tables are the reason the work was copied for four hundred years.

**Book Three: ceremonial magic.** The intellectual world. Divine names, the orders of
angels, the hierarchy of spirits, the Kabbalistic material that Pico had introduced, the
means of purification and consecration, and the techniques by which an operator may
address intelligences above the level of the stars.

Book Three is where Ficino's careful line gets crossed. Ficino had gone to some trouble to
say that he was drawing down impersonal influence, not addressing persons. Agrippa
addresses persons. He gives their names, their sigils, their hierarchies and the
conditions under which they may be approached. He also folds in Pico's Christian Cabala,
so that the divine names of Hebrew scripture become the operative vocabulary of a
Christian magician.

The result is a synthesis of three streams that had previously run separately: Renaissance
Neoplatonic natural philosophy, the medieval grimoire tradition of angel and spirit
conjuration, and Christian Kabbalah. Nobody had put all three in one place before. Almost
everybody afterwards used Agrippa's arrangement, because the arrangement made the material
usable.

### What a table of correspondences is for

I want to dwell on Book Two for a moment, because its most-copied feature is also the most
misunderstood.

Agrippa gives, for each number and each planet, long columns: the divine name of that
number, the angelic order, the sphere, the metal, the stone, the animal, the plant, the
part of the body, the human faculty. Jupiter runs to tin, sapphire, the eagle, the liver,
a particular magic square, a particular angel, and so on down the column.

To a modern eye this looks like arbitrary list-making, or at best like a mnemonic. It is
neither. Given the metaphysics of Chapters 6 and 7, the table is a *technical document*.
If the cosmos is a single emanating continuum in which every level answers to every other,
then these columns are not associations. They are a map of the actual joints of reality —
the places where the levels touch. Assembling the right column at the right hour is the
practical consequence of the theory, in the same way that a chemist's table of reagents is
the practical consequence of atomic theory.

This is why tables of correspondence are the single most persistent artefact in Western
esoteric practice. Agrippa's columns are copied, expanded and rearranged for four hundred
years, and in 1909 Aleister Crowley publishes a book that is nothing *but* columns —
*Liber 777*, a table of correspondences running to dozens of categories, explicitly in
this lineage, which practitioners still use.

The continuity there is real and documentable. When we reach the Golden Dawn I will show
the route.

### The journey of one book

Which brings us to the question this book cares about more than any other: where did
Agrippa's *Occult Philosophy* physically go?

It went, first, into Latin editions across the sixteenth century, and then into English.
A translation attributed to "J.F." appeared in London in 1651 — the middle of the English
Revolution, in the same decade in which Elias Ashmole was publishing his alchemical
anthology and a masons' lodge in Warrington was admitting gentlemen.[^2] That translation
stayed in circulation. It was reprinted. Second-hand copies were bought and sold. It was
the standard English magical text for two hundred and fifty years.

And in the 1880s a London coroner named William Wynn Westcott had a copy, along with two
hundred and eighty-five other books of a similar kind, in a private library that Alison
Butler has catalogued.[^3]

That is a transmission. Not "ideas in the air" — a printed book, in a named language, in a
named man's collection, which we can show he owned and which we can show he used. When this
book claims that Renaissance magic reached the Victorian occult revival, this is the
evidence, and it is the kind of evidence I have been asking for since Chapter 5.

Note carefully what the same evidence does *not* establish.

It does not establish that Agrippa reached Freemasonry. The English *Occult Philosophy* of
1651 was available to any literate Englishman with a few shillings, including members of
masons' lodges. Availability is not transmission. To show that Agrippa entered the Craft
one would need his vocabulary, his structure or his tables in Masonic ritual — and they are
not there. The catechisms of the 1690s and the exposures of 1723–1762 contain no planetary
seals, no angelic hierarchies, no magic squares, no divine names used operatively, and no
correspondences. The Craft's furniture is a Bible, a square and a pair of compasses.

Agrippa was on the shelf for two centuries beside the lodge, and did not get in.

He got in later, through a different door, carried by different people — and one of the
purposes of this book is to establish exactly when that door opened. The answer is the
nineteenth century, and the person holding it is Éliphas Lévi.

### The ambivalence, and what it teaches

One more thing about Agrippa, because it bears on how this whole literature should be
read.

The man who wrote the great synthesis of Renaissance magic also wrote, twenty years later,
that the magical arts are vain, that their practitioners are deceived, and that the whole
edifice of human learning is a tower of presumption. He then published the magical work
anyway, with a prefatory letter acknowledging that he had written it young.

The standard readings are: that he genuinely changed his mind; that the attack was a
fashionable sceptical exercise of a kind the period enjoyed; that he was protecting
himself; or that he held both views, magic being true in principle and corrupt in
practice.

I have no confident answer, and I mention it because it is a useful corrective to a habit
of mind this subject encourages. We tend to sort historical people into believers and
sceptics, and then to read everything they wrote as expressing their category.
Sixteenth-century intellectuals did not necessarily work that way. A man could compile the
most comprehensive magical handbook of his century and also think most magicians were
fools, and see no contradiction, because he was not defending a team.

That ambivalence — serious engagement combined with serious doubt — is, incidentally, the
posture of this book. I did not get it from Agrippa, but I am glad to find it there.

---

The Renaissance magical synthesis is now assembled: Hermetic cosmology, Neoplatonic
architecture, Ficinian correspondence, Pico's concordance and his Kabbalah, Agrippa's
three-storey organisation and his tables.

One component of that mixture has been named repeatedly and never examined. Pico brought
it in; Agrippa built Book Three on it; Knorr von Rosenroth will anthologise it in 1677;
Mathers will translate Knorr into English in 1887; and the resulting system will be called,
by people who have never read a word of Hebrew, the most ancient wisdom of the Jews.

It is time to look at what Kabbalah actually is, and at the four-step chain by which
something else entirely arrived in a London temple under its name.
