# CHAPTER ELEVEN
## The Alchemical Work

In the 1650s, in a laboratory in London, a young American named George Starkey was trying
to make a substance he called the sophic mercury, and he was keeping notes.

The notes survive. They are not mystical. They record quantities, temperatures, the number
of hours a vessel was kept at a given heat, what happened, what went wrong, and what he
proposed to try next. He worked with Robert Boyle. He supplied materials and techniques to
men whose names are in the history of chemistry. He also published, under the name
Eirenaeus Philalethes, alchemical treatises written in the dense allegorical idiom of the
tradition — dragons, kings, doves, the green lion devouring the sun — which were read
across Europe as mystical texts and were, in fact, encoded laboratory procedures.[^1]

That double existence is the thing about alchemy that a reader coming from the popular
literature will least expect and most needs to know.

---

### Against the psychological reduction

For most of the twentieth century, educated opinion held that alchemy was not really about
matter. It was about the soul: a system of spiritual transformation cast in chemical
metaphor, the furnace a symbol of the self, the philosophers' stone a symbol of
enlightenment. Carl Jung gave this reading its most influential form, treating alchemical
imagery as a projection of unconscious processes and the opus as an account of
individuation.

It is a beautiful theory. As history, it is largely wrong, and its wrongness has a
traceable origin.

The work of Lawrence Principe and William Newman over the past three decades has
established, through the unglamorous method of reading the technical literature and
repeating the experiments, that early modern alchemy — *chymistry*, to use the period's own
undifferentiated term — was a laboratory practice pursuing material goals with material
methods. The allegorical language was not mystification for its own sake. It was
*Decknamen*: cover-names, a professional cipher protecting valuable procedures, exactly as
a modern laboratory protects a process. Principe has taken notoriously obscure passages and
performed them, and got the described results — a "philosophical tree" growing in a flask,
from reagents identified by decoding the allegory.[^2]

Where, then, did the spiritual reading come from? Principe and Newman trace it to the
nineteenth century: to Mary Anne Atwood, whose *A Suggestive Inquiry into the Hermetic
Mystery* (1850) proposed that the true alchemical work was interior, and to the occult
revival that followed, which found this reading congenial. A. E. Waite adopted a version of
it; Jung inherited it and gave it a psychological vocabulary.[^3]

So the "spiritual alchemy" that popular books present as the tradition's inner meaning is
in fact its Victorian reinterpretation — which places it, chronologically, in Part VI of
this book rather than Part II.

Two qualifications, because the corrective can be overdone. First, the practitioners were
not materialists in our sense; they thought matter was alive, that metals grew in the earth,
and that the same principles governed a furnace and a soul. Distinguishing "material" from
"spiritual" goals imposes a boundary they did not have. Second, some alchemical writing
genuinely is devotional — the German Lutheran tradition around Jakob Böhme used alchemical
vocabulary for frankly theological ends. The point is not that spiritual alchemy never
existed. It is that it was one strand among several, and that the twentieth century
mistook it for the whole.

---

### The work

The technical vocabulary, briefly, because the imagery will recur.

**Prima materia**: the first matter, the undifferentiated substance from which the work
begins. Identifying it is the tradition's central puzzle, and the texts are deliberately
unhelpful — it is said to be common, despised, found everywhere and recognized by nobody.

**Sulphur, mercury, salt**: the three principles. Not the everyday substances but the
qualities they exemplify — combustibility, volatility and fixity, roughly. Paracelsus's
addition of salt to the older sulphur-mercury pair gave the sixteenth century its working
chemical theory.

**Solve et coagula**: dissolve and coagulate. The fundamental rhythm. Break down, then
reassemble. Repeat.

**The stages**, usually given as three and sometimes more: *nigredo*, the blackening — the
matter putrefies, goes black, and appears dead; *albedo*, the whitening — purification;
*rubedo*, the reddening — the completion. Between them lie further operations, and the
number and order vary by author because the authors were describing different procedures.

**The stone**: the goal. Capable of transmuting base metals into gold; in some accounts
also a universal medicine.

And through all of it, the death. The nigredo is not a metaphor added for color. It is the
descriptive heart of the work: the matter must be killed, must rot, must go black and
stink, before anything can be made of it. The illustrated alchemical books of the sixteenth
and seventeenth centuries render this with striking literalness — a king and queen in a
coffin, a corpse in a flask, a skull, a black sun. The *Rosarium Philosophorum* of 1550 has
a woodcut sequence in which the royal pair are united, die, lie entombed while a soul
departs upward, and are then revived.

Death, then raising.

---

### The coincidence, tested

Now the confrontation this chapter exists for.

The central drama of the Masonic third degree is the murder of a master builder, his burial,
the discovery of the grave, and his raising. It is a ritual death and a ritual resurrection,
performed on a candidate who plays the part.

Alchemy's central image is a death and a resurrection.

The resemblance is real. It has been noticed by every writer on the subject since the
eighteenth century, and it is one of the load-bearing arguments for an alchemical
Freemasonry. So let us run the six questions of Chapter 5 on it, in order, and see what
survives.

**Provenance.** Is there a document connecting the composition of the Hiramic legend to any
alchemical text? No. Not a manuscript, not a letter, not a lodge minute, not a marginal
note. Nothing has ever been produced.

**Chronology.** Does the timeline permit influence? Yes, easily. The alchemical literature
is centuries older than the third degree, which appears in print in 1730. Chronology is the
one test the claim passes without difficulty, and it is the weakest of the six.

**Transmission.** Can anyone name the route — a person, a text, a meeting? No. We do not
know who composed the third degree. That is one of the real mysteries of Masonic history:
the central ritual of the world's largest fraternal organization has no known author and no
datable moment of composition. It appears, fully formed, in a hostile pamphlet in 1730, and
there is no surviving draft.

**Intertextuality.** Does the Masonic legend quote, paraphrase or share distinctive
language with alchemical sources? No. Its materials are identifiable and they are
elsewhere: the biblical account of the building of the Temple in 1 Kings and 2 Chronicles,
which supplies Hiram the craftsman; the medieval Old Charges, which supply the craft's
legendary history; and the ordinary dramatic repertoire of the period. The vocabulary is
architectural and biblical throughout. There is no sulphur, no mercury, no nigredo, no
stone.

**Institutional continuity.** Was there an organization carrying alchemical teaching into
the lodges? Not in the relevant period. In the 1770s there will be — the Gold- und
Rosenkreuz, which we come to in Chapter 28, and which is a genuine, documented, institutional
link between alchemy and Masonic structure. But that is forty years *after* the third
degree is in print, and the influence runs the other way: an alchemical order recruiting
through Masonry, not alchemy entering the Craft.

**Anachronism.** Does the claim require anything impossible? Not strictly. But it is worth
noting that the spiritual-transformation reading of alchemy — the reading that makes the
parallel look meaningful — is itself a nineteenth-century development. To say that the
third degree "is" alchemical transformation is to read an eighteenth-century ritual through
a Victorian interpretation of a seventeenth-century practice.

**Verdict.** One test passed, four failed, one inconclusive. The parallel is real; the
descent is unsupported.

---

### Why the parallel exists anyway

Which leaves a genuine question. If nobody borrowed, why do they look alike?

Because death-and-return is not a proprietary idea. It is one of the small number of
patterns available to any tradition that wants to represent fundamental change, and it
recurs across cultures with no contact whatever, for reasons that are not mysterious:
human beings die, seeds are buried and come up, the sun goes down and returns, and anyone
constructing a symbol of transformation reaches for the same material because there is not
much other material.

The alchemists reached for it because their matter actually did go black and then change.
The composers of the Masonic third degree reached for it because they were dramatising the
completion of a craft and the loss of its master. Christian culture had been soaked in it
for seventeen centuries. Everyone reached for it.

When a pattern is this widely distributed, its presence in two places is nearly
uninformative. It is the *unusual* features that carry evidential weight — a shared oddity,
a shared error, a distinctive detail with no independent reason to exist. The third degree
and the *Rosarium* have none in common.

---

### What alchemy does contribute

It would be wrong to end on a negative, because alchemy's contribution to this history is
substantial. It simply arrives later and through a different door.

It contributes **a vocabulary of interior transformation** which, once Atwood and the
Victorians had spiritualised it, became available to every occultist of the revival and was
read back into everything, including Masonry.

It contributes **the most powerful image-bank in Western esotericism**. The engraved
alchemical emblems of the seventeenth century — Maier's *Atalanta Fugiens*, the *Rosarium*,
the Rosicrucian plates — are the visual language that the nineteenth and twentieth centuries
will recycle endlessly.

And it contributes **the Gold- und Rosenkreuz**, a real order, with real alchemy, which
required Masonic membership and thereby produced one of the genuine documented junctions
between the Craft and an operative esoteric practice.

That order takes its name and its mythology from a set of pamphlets published in the German
city of Kassel between 1614 and 1616, announcing a secret brotherhood dedicated to the
reformation of the world.

The brotherhood did not exist. The author later called the third pamphlet a joke. And the
template it established — invisible superiors, a hidden founder, a tomb, a coming
transformation — would be used, and reused, and reused, by almost every organization in the
remainder of this book.
