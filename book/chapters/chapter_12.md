# CHAPTER TWELVE
## Rosicrucian Fire

In 1614 a printer in Kassel issued a small book containing a general call for the
reformation of the world, a satirical piece translated from the Italian, and — bound in
with them, almost as an appendix — a pamphlet of about twenty pages announcing that a
secret brotherhood existed.

The *Fama Fraternitatis* told a story. A German nobleman, called only Brother C.R., had
traveled in his youth to Damascus, to the Arabian city of Damcar, to Fez and to Spain,
learning from sages in each place a wisdom that Europe had lost. He returned, was ignored
by the learned, and withdrew to found a fraternity of eight members. They were to travel,
to heal the sick without payment, to wear no distinctive habit but the dress of the
country they were in, to meet once a year, and each to find a successor. Their existence
was to remain hidden for a hundred years.

C.R. died at the age of a hundred and six and was buried in a place his brothers did not
know. And then, the *Fama* says, a hundred and twenty years later, a door was discovered
behind a memorial tablet. Behind the door was a vault of seven sides, lit — though it was
underground and sealed — by an artificial sun in the ceiling. Every surface was covered
with figures and inscriptions. In the center stood an altar, and beneath the altar,
uncorrupted, lay the body of the founder, holding a book.

The pamphlet ended by inviting the learned of Europe to make themselves known to the
Fraternity, who would answer.[^1]

---

### What happened next

What happened next is one of the strangest episodes in European intellectual history.

Europe answered. In enormous numbers.

Over the following decade several hundred books, pamphlets and broadsheets appeared in
response — addressed to the Brethren, defending them, denouncing them, applying for
membership, claiming membership, or attempting to work out who they were.[^2] A second
manifesto, the *Confessio Fraternitatis*, appeared in 1615, elaborating the doctrine and
sharpening its anti-papal edge. A third text, the *Chymische Hochzeit Christiani
Rosencreutz* — the *Chemical Wedding* — followed in 1616, and is a different kind of
object altogether: a strange, funny, beautifully constructed allegorical novel in which
the narrator is invited to a royal wedding and spends seven days undergoing tests, watching
ceremonies, witnessing executions and a resurrection, and being made a Knight of the Golden
Stone.

Applicants published open letters because they had no address to write to. Elias Ashmole,
forty years later and in England, would copy out such a letter of application. In Paris in
1623, placards appeared announcing that the Brethren of the Rose Cross were visiting the
city invisibly, and the resulting panic produced pamphlets, sermons, and a satirical
response from someone who may have been Descartes's circle.

No one ever found a member. No one ever received a reply.

---

### Who wrote them

The authorship is not, in fact, a great mystery. The *Chemical Wedding* is the work of
Johann Valentin Andreae, a Lutheran theologian of Württemberg, who acknowledged it in his
autobiography and described it as a *ludibrium* — a jest, a game, a piece of play.[^3] He
is generally thought to have had a hand in the other two as well, working with a circle at
Tübingen; the details are debated, but the milieu is not.

Andreae's circle were young Lutheran intellectuals in the years immediately before the
Thirty Years' War, steeped in Paracelsian medicine, alchemy, Christian mysticism and the
hope of a general reformation of learning and religion. Their pamphlets were a call dressed
as a report: they described the brotherhood they wished existed, in the hope that describing
it would summon it.

In one sense it worked perfectly. Within a decade of the *Fama*, "Rosicrucian" was a
European word.

In another sense it produced three hundred years of confusion, because a great many readers
declined to treat the announcement as fiction, and some of them founded organizations.

---

### The five Rosicrucianisms

Because this is where the confusion starts, let me set out the distinctions this book will
enforce.

**One: the manifestos.** Three texts, 1614–1616, Kassel and Strasbourg. Documents, not an
organization.

**Two: the furore.** The 1610s–1620s reaction across Europe: hundreds of publications,
applicants, denunciations, the Paris placards. Entirely real, and entirely a response to a
fiction.

**Three: the eighteenth-century orders.** From the 1750s onward, and particularly the
Gold- und Rosenkreuz, a genuine, organized, initiatory body with degrees, officers,
alchemical instruction and political influence in Prussia. This is real Rosicrucianism —
and it postdates the manifestos by a hundred and forty years.

**Four: the nineteenth-century revivals.** The Societas Rosicruciana in Anglia, founded in
1866, open only to Master Masons; and the Golden Dawn, which presented itself as the outer
order of a Rosicrucian body.

**Five: modern organizations** claiming ancient Rosicrucian descent.

Collapsing any two of these produces nonsense. Collapsing one and three produces the single
most persistent error in the field: the belief that a Rosicrucian order existed in the
seventeenth century and fed into Freemasonry.

---

### The inversion

Here is the correction, and it is one of the load-bearing findings of this book.

The popular account runs: Rosicrucianism → Freemasonry. Seventeenth-century secret
brotherhood, going underground, resurfacing in the lodges of the eighteenth century.

The documented sequence runs the other way.

There was no seventeenth-century Rosicrucian organization to feed into anything. The
manifestos describe a fiction; the furore found nobody; and the extensive scholarly search
for a real brotherhood behind the pamphlets — Carlos Gilly's bibliographical work on the
manuscripts and printings, Roland Edighoffer's studies of Andreae — has produced a circle
of Tübingen intellectuals rather than an order.[^4]

Meanwhile, the first genuinely institutional Rosicrucianism appears in Central Europe in
the middle of the eighteenth century, by which time Freemasonry had been organized in
London for a generation and had spread across the continent. And the Gold- und Rosenkreuz
did not merely postdate Masonry. It *required* it: a candidate had to be a Master Mason
before he could be admitted.[^5]

Christopher McIntosh's study of the order makes the relationship explicit. Masonry was the
recruiting ground and the structural model; the Rosicrucian material was the content poured
into it.

So: **Freemasonry did not descend from Rosicrucianism. Freemasonry carried Rosicrucianism
into the modern world.** The same inversion will hold, with the same evidence, for
Hermeticism, for Kabbalah, and for ceremonial magic. It is the thesis of this book, and the
Rosicrucian case is where it can be demonstrated most cleanly, because the dates are so
unambiguous.

---

### The template

And yet the manifestos matter enormously, which is the paradox worth sitting with. A text
describing an organization that never existed became the most influential organizational
document in the history of Western esotericism.

What it supplied was a **template**, and once you have seen its components you will find
them everywhere in the rest of this book.

*A founder in the distant past, wise, traveled, and dead.* C.R. — Christian Rosenkreutz —
becomes Hiram Abiff, becomes the Unknown Superiors, becomes the Mahatmas, becomes the
Secret Chiefs.

*A wisdom acquired in the East and lost in Europe.* This structure — the West has forgotten
what the East preserved — is the organizing assumption of the entire nineteenth-century
occult revival, and of Theosophy in particular.

*A hidden tomb, discovered.* The seven-sided vault with its artificial sun reappears,
astonishingly literally, as the Vault of the Adepti in the Golden Dawn's Adeptus Minor
ceremony: a seven-sided chamber, an altar, a body. Mathers and Westcott built it out of the
*Fama*, and they knew exactly what they were doing.[^6]

*Invisible superiors who cannot be contacted but who authorise everything.* This is the
most consequential component, because it solves a structural problem that every esoteric
organization faces: where does authority come from? If it comes from a living person, that
person can be argued with. If it comes from concealed superiors, it cannot be questioned,
only obeyed — or rejected entirely, which is what tends to happen eventually. Baron von
Hund spent thirty years unable to produce his. Westcott forged letters from his. Mathers
claimed contact with his and the Order tore itself apart over it in 1900.

*A coming general reformation.* The promise that the world is about to change, and that the
initiate is early.

Six components, published in 1614 in a pamphlet its probable author called a jest, and
still in service three hundred years later.

---

### Böhme, briefly

One more figure belongs here, because his vocabulary travels with the Rosicrucian current
and because he is otherwise easy to lose.

Jakob Böhme was a shoemaker in Görlitz who, around 1600, had an experience he spent the
rest of his life trying to describe: a sudden seeing-into the ground of things, reportedly
occasioned by sunlight on a pewter dish. What he wrote, beginning with *Aurora* in 1612,
is difficult, original, and written in a private vocabulary borrowed partly from alchemy —
he uses sulphur, mercury and salt as principles of the divine nature itself.

His importance for this book is indirect but real. Böhme supplied the German and then the
French tradition with a *Christian* esotericism: interior, visionary, theologically serious,
and not dependent on magic or on ancient pedigrees. Through Pietism he shaped a century of
German religious feeling. And through Louis-Claude de Saint-Martin, who translated him into
French, he shaped the most theologically ambitious current in eighteenth-century
Freemasonry — the Martinist and Rectified Scottish tradition of Chapter 27.

Böhme never joined anything. He is the reminder that not every current in this history runs
through an organization.

---

Part II is now complete, and we can state what it establishes.

By 1700, Europe possessed a full inventory of esoteric materials: a Hermetic cosmology
mistakenly believed to be Egyptian; a Neoplatonic architecture of graded ascent; a
Ficinian doctrine of correspondence; Pico's method of concordance; Agrippa's operative
synthesis; a Christianised Kabbalah at two removes from its source; an alchemical practice
with a death-and-rebirth grammar; a Rosicrucian template for the invisible order; and
Böhme's interior Christianity.

All of it in print. All of it purchasable. None of it — not one item — documented as having
entered a masons' lodge.

That is the situation on the eve of the institution this book is actually about. In the
next part we go to Scotland, in the last years of the sixteenth century, and watch a royal
official with a talent for administration do something that nobody at the time thought was
remarkable and that changed everything.
