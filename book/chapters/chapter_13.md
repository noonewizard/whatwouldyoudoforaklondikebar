# PART III — THE MASONIC MACHINE

# CHAPTER THIRTEEN
## Schaw's Statutes

On the twenty-eighth of December 1598, the Master of Works to the Crown of Scotland
signed a document regulating the masons of his kingdom, and thereby — without the least
intention of doing so — began Freemasonry.

William Schaw was a courtier. He had charge of the royal building works: palaces,
repairs, the machinery of a small monarchy's architectural ambitions. He had traveled on
the continent, he was a Catholic in a Protestant country, and he seems to have been the
kind of administrator who thinks institutions should be tidy. The statutes he issued in
1598 and again, more remarkably, in 1599 are administrative documents. They are about
apprenticeship terms, the number of apprentices a master may take, the quality of work,
the settling of disputes, the obligation of members to attend, and the fines for not doing
so.[^1]

They are also the founding charter of the modern lodge, and the reason is buried in the
detail.

---

### What the statutes actually do

Four things, and each of them is decisive.

**They make the lodge permanent.** In the medieval building trade a lodge was a shed. It
was the hut beside the cathedral where the masons kept their tools, ate, sometimes slept,
and conducted the business of the site. When the cathedral was finished, the lodge came
down. It was temporary by definition, because a building project is temporary.

Schaw's statutes assume something different: lodges attached to *places* rather than to
projects. Edinburgh's lodge. Kilwinning's lodge. Stirling's lodge. Institutions with
continuous existence independent of whether anything was being built.

**They make it write things down.** The statutes require lodges to keep records — to
enrol apprentices and fellows in a book, with names and dates and witnesses. This is the
single most consequential clause in the history of Freemasonry, and it is entirely
bureaucratic in motivation. Because of it, we have minutes. The Lodge of Edinburgh
(Mary's Chapel) has records running from 1599; the lodge at Aitchison's Haven from 1598.
They are the oldest continuous minutes of any such body in the world, and they exist
because a royal official wanted better paperwork.[^2]

**They make it a jurisdiction.** The statutes give lodges authority over their members —
to judge disputes, impose fines, and regulate conduct. A lodge becomes a small court as
well as a workshop.

**And they make it a body with secrets to test.** The 1599 statutes require that fellows
of craft be examined in "the art of memory and the science thereof," and that the warden
test them on it.[^3]

That last clause is the one everybody argues about, and it is worth stopping on.

---

### The art of memory

In the classical world, an orator with a long speech and no notes used a technique: he
imagined a building he knew well, placed vivid images representing his topics at specific
points along a route through it, and then walked the route mentally while speaking. The
images recalled the topics; the architecture kept them in order. The technique is
described in the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, in Cicero and in Quintilian, and it was taught
throughout the Middle Ages and the Renaissance.

By the sixteenth century it had grown considerably more ambitious. Giordano Bruno, among
others, proposed memory systems in which the imagined building was the cosmos itself, and
the images were not arbitrary but drawn from astrological and magical correspondence — so
that mastering the memory system would be mastering the universe's structure. Frances
Yates traced this development in *The Art of Memory*, one of the great works of
twentieth-century intellectual history.[^4]

Now: Schaw requires Scottish masons to be tested in the art of memory in 1599. Yates had
established that the art of memory in that period had occult dimensions. David Stevenson,
in *The Origins of Freemasonry*, put the two together and proposed that the Scottish lodge
had absorbed Renaissance esoteric ideas — that this is the moment when something more than
stonecutting enters the craft.[^5]

Stevenson's book is the finest thing written on early Masonic history and I rely on it
throughout. But I do not think this particular argument is proved, and I want to say why,
carefully, because it is the strongest candidate anyone has produced for an esoteric
element in Freemasonry before the eighteenth century.

Run the questions.

The clause says the fellows are to be tested in the art of memory. It does not say which
art of memory. There were two things that phrase could mean in 1599: the ordinary,
utilitarian mnemonic technique taught in every grammar school, which an illiterate or
semi-literate craftsman would need in order to hold a body of trade knowledge without
writing it down; and the elaborate Brunian cosmic version.

Which would you expect a Scottish master mason to be tested on?

The statutes' own context suggests the first. The surrounding clauses are about
apprenticeship terms and fines. The lodge's business was a trade. And the thing a mason
needed to memorize — the catechism, the questions and answers by which he could be
recognized — is exactly the kind of material the ordinary mnemonic art was for.

The transmission question is also unanswered. Schaw had traveled; he was educated; the
Scottish court had continental connections. It is *possible* that he knew the Brunian
tradition. Nobody has produced evidence that he did.

So the honest verdict is: **plausible, suggestive, and unproven.** I record it that way,
and it goes on the list in Chapter 52.

What I would add is that the case does not need to be proved for Schaw to matter. The art
of memory clause is interesting; the record-keeping clause is world-changing.

---

### The gentlemen arrive

Because here is what the records, once they exist, let us see.

In June 1600, the minutes of the Lodge of Edinburgh record the presence of John Boswell,
Laird of Auchinleck. He signs with a mark, as the masons do. He is not a mason. He is a
landowner.[^6]

That is the earliest documented attendance of a non-operative at a lodge meeting anywhere
in the world, and it happens within two years of Schaw's statutes, in a lodge that exists
to keep records because Schaw told it to.

Over the following century the pattern spreads. Scottish lodges admit lairds, merchants,
professional men — people with no intention of cutting stone. Sometimes a few, sometimes
enough to change the character of the body. By the later seventeenth century, some lodges
have more gentlemen than masons.

Why?

The honest answer is that we do not fully know, and the candidates are several. There is
patronage: a trade body benefits from having influential men associated with it, and
influential men benefit from the connection to a trade they may employ. There is
sociability: a lodge was a reason to meet, drink and dine in a society with few other
institutions for doing so across class lines. There is curiosity about a group with an
oath and a secret. There is local politics. And there is, possibly, the appeal of the
craft's own legendary history — the Old Charges, with their claim that masonry descends
from Euclid and the building of Solomon's Temple, which would have been read aloud at
admissions and which gave a small trade body a genealogy grander than any guild in
Scotland.

What there is no evidence for, in any Scottish lodge record of the seventeenth century, is
esoteric practice. No alchemy in the minutes. No Kabbalah. No astrology. No Hermetic
vocabulary. The minutes are about money, discipline, apprentices, and dinner.

---

### The first machine

Step back and look at what exists in Scotland by, say, 1650.

A network of permanent local bodies. Each with a written record. Each with authority over
its members. Each with an admission ceremony involving an oath. Each holding a body of
memorized material — a catechism of questions and answers, a word, a grip — which
distinguishes members from non-members. Each with a legendary history reaching to
antiquity. And each, increasingly, containing men who have nothing to do with the trade
the whole thing is nominally about.

That is Freemasonry. Not the symbolism, not the philosophy, not the three degrees — those
come later and elsewhere. But the *machine*: the structural apparatus that everything else
will be hung on.

And it was built, so far as any evidence shows, for entirely ordinary reasons, by a royal
administrator who wanted his kingdom's masons to keep proper books.

This is the pattern this book keeps encountering, and it is worth naming now that we have
a clean example. **The most consequential things in this history were generally built by
people who were not trying to build them.** Ficino translated the Hermetica because his
patron was dying. Andreae wrote a jest. Schaw wrote a regulation. In each case the object
produced went on to do something enormous that its maker neither intended nor lived to
see.

There is a version of history that finds this disappointing, because it prefers designers
to accidents. I find it the opposite. An institution that was designed can be explained by
its design. An institution that emerged has to be explained by what people actually did
with it, which is harder and more interesting, and which is the only kind of explanation
that ever holds up.

---

### The English question

One complication before we cross the border.

If Scotland has the lodges, the records and the ceremony from 1598, and England has almost
no lodge records before the late seventeenth century, does that mean English Freemasonry
is Scottish in origin?

Stevenson's answer is essentially yes, and the institutional evidence supports him. But
England has something Scotland does not: the Old Charges. The manuscript constitutions of
the English masons — the Regius poem of about 1390, the Cooke manuscript of about 1410, and
more than a hundred later copies — are a body of legendary and regulatory text with no
Scottish equivalent of comparable age. And it is the Old Charges, not the Scottish statutes,
that supply the mythology: Euclid, the two pillars, the Temple, the descent of geometry.

So the honest reconstruction is a convergence rather than a single origin. Scotland
supplies the institution; England supplies the legend; and the fusion happens somewhere in
the seventeenth century, in ways the surviving evidence lets us see only in outline.

Which brings us back to a room in Warrington in 1646, and to the English side of the story
— where the records are worse, the evidence is thinner, and the men who joined are, for
that very reason, more interesting.
