# CHAPTER FOURTEEN
## Ashmole's Warrington

The English evidence is bad, and it is worth understanding *how* it is bad, because the
shape of the gap has been filled, repeatedly, with whatever the filler wanted to find.

Scotland has lodge minutes from 1598. England has essentially nothing comparable until the
1720s. There are a handful of references — a diary entry here, a company record there, a
naturalist's paragraph — and then a silence of decades, and then, quite suddenly, a fully
organized Grand Lodge with a printed constitution.

What England does have, in abundance, is manuscripts of a different kind.

---

### The Old Charges

More than a hundred copies survive of a text that circulated among English masons from the
late fourteenth century onward. Its oldest version is a poem of some 800 lines, written in
Middle English around 1390, now British Library Royal MS 17 A.I and known as the Regius
manuscript; the next oldest is the Cooke manuscript of about 1410. The later copies, in
prose, run through the fifteenth, sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and into the
eighteenth.[^1]

They have a consistent shape. An invocation. Then a legendary history of the craft. Then a
set of charges — regulations of conduct — which the candidate was to hear read and to
swear to observe.

The history is the part that matters here, and it is worth summarising accurately, because
it is routinely misrepresented in both directions.

It begins before the Flood. The children of Lamech discover the sciences, and, knowing that
the world will be destroyed by either fire or water, inscribe them on two pillars — one of
marble that will not burn, one of brick that will not sink — so that the knowledge will
survive. After the Flood the pillars are found. Geometry passes to Egypt, where Abraham
teaches it; Euclid systematizes it and instructs the sons of Egyptian nobility in the
mason's craft. The craft passes to Israel, where Solomon employs eighty thousand masons on
the Temple. Thence to France, and thence, with St Alban and later with King Athelstan, to
England.

So: yes, the Old Charges contain Egypt. Yes, they contain the Temple. Yes, they contain
antediluvian pillars preserving knowledge from destruction.

And no, none of it is Hermetic, esoteric or occult. Every element is traceable to the
ordinary learned furniture of medieval England: the two-pillars story comes from Josephus,
mediated through medieval universal chronicles; the Euclid-in-Egypt material comes from
the standard histories of the liberal arts; the Temple comes from the Bible.[^2] These are
the sources a literate cleric of 1400 would have had. The craft legend is a trade's
self-flattery assembled from available learning, and it is exactly what a guild would
produce.

But it does something important. It tells a working mason that his trade is not a trade.
It is the practical form of geometry, which is one of the seven liberal sciences, which
were preserved from the Flood, and which built the house of God. A man who has heard that
read aloud at his admission has been told that he belongs to something ancient and
significant.

That is not an esoteric doctrine. It is, however, exactly the kind of thing that attracts
a gentleman with antiquarian interests.

---

### The three witnesses

For England before 1700 we have essentially three kinds of testimony.

**The diaries.** Ashmole's, discussed in Chapter 1: made a Free Mason at Warrington on 16
October 1646 with Colonel Henry Mainwaring, in company with seven men who were not masons,
in a town with no significant building trade; and then, thirty-five years later, attending
a lodge at Masons' Hall in London in March 1682. Ashmole is our best English witness and he
tells us nothing about content.

**The company records.** The London Company of Masons — the livery company, a trade
organization of a different kind — has references from the 1620s onward to something called
the Accepcion or Acception: a body within or alongside the Company into which men could be
"accepted," including men who were not members of the Company at all. Payments are
recorded. It cost more to be accepted if you were not a member.[^3] This is thin evidence,
but it is evidence of a non-operative practice in London a generation before Warrington.

**The naturalists.** In 1686 Robert Plot published *The Natural History of
Staffordshire*, and in the course of surveying the county's customs devoted several
paragraphs to what he called the Society of Free-Masons. He reports that the custom is
spread more or less all over the nation; that persons of the most eminent quality do not
disdain to be of the fellowship; that admission involves a meeting of at least five or six
of the ancients of the Order, a collation, gloves given to the candidate and to his wife,
and the communication of certain secret signs by which members recognize one another
anywhere. He also reports that they have a written history of the craft — he clearly had
seen a copy of the Old Charges — and he thought the history was nonsense, saying so at
some length.[^4]

Plot is a hostile and slightly amused witness, which makes him valuable. If there had been
anything scandalous, magical or heterodox on offer, he would have said so; he was
perfectly willing to say the craft's history was fabulous.

John Aubrey, in a manuscript note of the same year, records that the fraternity of
Free-Masons was to have a convention at St Paul's, and mentions Sir Christopher Wren among
those to be adopted.[^5]

That is the English seventeenth century. Two diary entries, some company accounts, a
skeptical naturalist and an antiquary's note.

---

### What can be said

From that thin material, three statements can be made with confidence, and they are worth
separating from everything that has been built on top of them.

**One: non-operative membership was real, and earlier in England than is often assumed.**
The London Acception from the 1620s, Warrington in 1646, Plot's "persons of the most
eminent quality" in 1686. Men who were not masons were joining masons' lodges across most
of the century.

**Two: the content, so far as any witness describes it, was signs, words, a history and an
oath.** Plot describes recognition signs. Ashmole describes nothing. The Scottish
catechisms, which are the only contemporary ritual texts we have from anywhere in Britain,
describe a word, a grip and a catechism. Nobody in the seventeenth century, friendly or
hostile, describes anything a modern reader would call occult.

**Three: nobody wrote down why they joined.**

That third point is the one I want to press, because the gap it leaves is where most of the
mythology lives.

We have, in Elias Ashmole, a man who was an alchemist, an astrologer, a collector of John
Dee's manuscripts, a copier of Rosicrucian application letters — and a Freemason. It is
almost irresistible to conclude that the last item belongs with the others.

But consider what else Ashmole was. He was an antiquary who collected everything: coins,
curiosities, charters, heraldry, the founding documents of the Order of the Garter, on
which he wrote an enormous book. He was a joiner of things. He was a man who documented
customs. A small, old, secretive society with a written legendary history reaching back to
Euclid and Solomon is, for a person of that temperament, an object of interest entirely
independent of whether it contains any wisdom.

Both readings are available. Neither is documented. And the correct historical statement is
that we do not know, which is unsatisfying and true.

---

### The shape of the silence

One last observation, because it will matter enormously in the next chapter.

Between Plot in 1686 and the printed constitutions of the 1720s, English Freemasonry is
almost invisible. There are scattered references. There is no institutional record. And
then, in a period of about six years, the following things happen: a Grand Lodge is either
formed or consolidated in London; a minister named James Anderson is commissioned to
compile a constitution; the constitution is printed in 1723 with a mythological history,
a set of charges, and a religious formula of startling breadth; the first exposures appear
in the newspapers; and the institution begins expanding at a rate that will carry it
across Europe within twenty years.

Something happened in London between about 1716 and 1723.

We know less about what it was than almost anyone assumes, and the reason is that our
principal source for it is a man writing fifteen years afterward with an institutional
interest in the answer.
