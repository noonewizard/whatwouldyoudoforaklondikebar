# CHAPTER FIFTEEN
## 1717, or 1721

Every organization needs a birthday, and Freemasonry's is printed on the front of its own
buildings.

The story is told in James Anderson's *New Book of Constitutions*, published in 1738. Four
old London lodges — meeting at the Goose and Gridiron alehouse in St Paul's Churchyard, the
Crown near Drury Lane, the Apple-Tree Tavern in Covent Garden, and the Rummer and Grapes
in Westminster — came together at the Apple-Tree, constituted themselves a Grand Lodge,
and on St John the Baptist's Day, 24 June 1717, at the Goose and Gridiron, elected Anthony
Sayer as their Grand Master.[^1]

From that meeting, the account runs, descends every regular Grand Lodge on earth. In 2017
the United Grand Lodge of England celebrated its tercentenary on that basis, with a great
deal of ceremony and a number of commemorative objects.

There is a difficulty. The difficulty is that Anderson's 1738 account is the only evidence
that the 1717 meeting ever happened.

---

### The evidentiary situation

Set out plainly, it is this.

Grand Lodge's own minutes begin on 24 June 1723. Nothing survives from before that date.

Anderson's first *Constitutions*, printed in 1723 — that is, within six years of the
claimed foundation, by a man writing for the body itself — does not mention the 1717
meeting at all. It gives the charges, the history, the regulations. It does not say the
Grand Lodge was founded in 1717.[^2]

The 1717 narrative appears for the first time in the second edition of 1738: fifteen years
after the first, twenty-one years after the event, written by the same author, who by then
had a considerable interest in establishing the institution's continuity and his own place
in it.

There is no contemporary newspaper report of the 1717 meeting. No lodge minute. No letter.
No diary entry. Nothing.

Apply the first of our six questions — *where is the physical object?* — and the answer is:
there is one book, printed twenty-one years afterward, by an interested party.

That does not make the story false. Institutions frequently record their own beginnings
late and imperfectly, and Anderson was writing within the lifetime of participants who
could have contradicted him. But it does mean that 1717 is a *claim*, not a fact, and it
should have been treated as one long before it was.

---

### The diarist

In 2016 and 2017 two historians, Andrew Prescott and Susan Mitchell Sommers, published work
proposing a different date, and their principal external evidence is a diary.[^3]

William Stukeley was an antiquary of real distinction — one of the founders of the Society
of Antiquaries, the man whose fieldwork at Stonehenge and Avebury produced records of
features that have since been destroyed, and a compulsive keeper of notes. On 6 January
1721 he wrote:

> I was made a Free Mason at the Salutation Tav. Tavistock street, with Mr Collins, Capt.
> Rowe who made the famous diving engine.

And he added an observation. He was, he said, the first person made a freemason in London
for many years, and they had had difficulty finding enough members to perform the
ceremony.[^4]

Sit with that for a moment.

The Salutation Tavern in Tavistock Street is a two- or three-minute walk from St Paul's
Churchyard. If a Grand Lodge comprising four lodges had been meeting in that immediate
neighborhood continuously since June 1717 — holding annual assemblies, electing Grand
Masters, constituting new lodges — then in January 1721 it should not have been difficult
to assemble enough Masons to initiate a candidate a few streets away. And a well-connected
antiquary who moved in exactly the circles from which the Grand Lodge's membership was
drawn should not have believed that he was the first man made a Mason in London for years.

Stukeley was not arguing a case. He was writing a private note about his own evening. That
is precisely what makes it valuable: a disinterested contemporary observation that is very
hard to reconcile with the received account.

---

### The alternative

What Prescott and Sommers propose in its place is that the effective foundation of the
Grand Lodge was the installation of John Montagu, second Duke of Montagu, as Grand Master
at Stationers' Hall on 24 June 1721.

The case for that date is circumstantial but coherent. It is the first Grand Lodge event
for which there is decent contemporary evidence. It marks the moment a nobleman took the
chair, which is when a tavern association becomes an institution with social weight and
national ambitions. It is immediately followed by everything that looks like organizational
activity: the commissioning of Anderson's *Constitutions*, the start of the minute book,
rapid expansion, and the first newspaper attention.

And on this reading, the 1717 story is not a fabrication so much as a *backdating* — the
projection of an origin a few years earlier than the reality, perhaps by conflating an
informal gathering of London Masons with the founding of a Grand Lodge, in a book written
two decades later by a man whose job was to supply the institution with a history.

---

### How to hold this

I want to be careful about how firmly to state the conclusion, because there is a
temptation to overclaim in both directions.

Some accounts of the Prescott and Sommers argument say it "conclusively proves" the 1721
date. That is stronger than the evidence supports. An argument from one diarist's
impression, however well placed, is not proof; Stukeley could have been mistaken about how
long it had been since a London initiation, or could have been describing a lodge
unconnected with the Grand Lodge's circle, or could have been in an unfashionable corner
of a world larger than he knew.

But the other direction is worse. The 1717 date rests on a single retrospective source
with an institutional motive, unsupported by any contemporary document, and contradicted by
the best contemporary observation we have. A historian who treated any *other* claim on
that evidence as established would be doing it wrong.

So the verdict this book adopts: **the 1721 case is stronger, the 1717 date should be
described as Anderson's claim rather than as a fact, and the question is genuinely open.**

It goes on the list in Chapter 52.

---

### Why this chapter matters more than a date

Here is the reason I have given a disputed anniversary two thousand words.

This is the one case in the entire book where we can watch a foundation myth being made,
at close range, with the documents in front of us — and where the institution making it is
not an occult order but the most respectable, best-documented and most bureaucratically
scrupulous body in this history.

Grand Lodge was not a conspiracy. It kept minutes. It printed its constitutions. Its
members were physicians and lawyers and Fellows of the Royal Society. And within
twenty-one years of its own beginning, it had acquired an origin story that its own
earlier publication does not support, which it then repeated for three centuries, which is
carved into buildings and printed on certificates and was celebrated worldwide in 2017.

Nobody had to lie for that to happen. It requires only an institution that wants a clean
beginning, an author willing to supply one, a gap of two decades, and the ordinary human
preference for a date over a muddle.

Now hold that observation up against the rest of this book.

If the *documented, minute-keeping, Enlightenment* end of this history acquired a false or
at least unverifiable origin date within twenty-one years — what should we expect of
organizations that kept no minutes, published nothing, swore their members to silence, and
had every incentive to claim antiquity?

We should expect exactly what we find. The Templar descent of the Rite of Strict
Observance. The Unknown Superiors nobody could produce. The German Rosicrucian adept who
never existed. The Egyptian mysteries. The unbroken chain from Solomon.

Anderson is the control case. He shows us how little pressure it takes, and how honest the
people involved can be, for a history to drift.

---

### A note on what did not happen

One more thing, because the 1717/1721 question is sometimes recruited into a larger and
sillier argument.

Nothing in this dispute suggests that Freemasonry is younger than commonly thought, or that
it was invented in the 1720s out of nothing. The Scottish lodges are documented from 1598.
The Old Charges run back to about 1390. Ashmole was initiated in 1646. Plot was describing
an established English custom in 1686. The Craft is unambiguously older than any Grand
Lodge.

What is at issue is the founding date of a *governing body* — an administrative
superstructure imposed on an existing practice. That superstructure mattered enormously,
because it standardized ritual, printed constitutions, chartered lodges abroad, and turned
a scattered custom into an international institution. But it did not create Freemasonry. It
organized it.

And in doing so, it produced the document that would define what Freemasonry was permitted
to mean for the next three hundred years — a printed book of 1723, containing a mythical
history, a set of regulations, and one sentence about religion that would prove to be the
most consequential sentence in Masonic history.
