# CHAPTER SIXTEEN
## The Religion in Which All Men Agree

In 1723 a Presbyterian minister from Aberdeen, working in London, published a book that
contained the following sentence about what a Freemason was obliged to believe:

> A Mason is oblig'd by his Tenure, to obey the moral Law; and if he rightly understands
> the Art, he will never be a stupid Atheist, nor an irreligious Libertine. But though in
> ancient Times Masons were charg'd in every Country to be of the Religion of that Country
> or Nation, whatever it was, yet 'tis now thought more expedient only to oblige them to
> that Religion in which all Men agree, leaving their particular Opinions to themselves.[^1]

It is the most important sentence in the history of Freemasonry, and probably the most
consequential sentence written by any Englishman in the 1720s that is not about property or
succession.

---

### What it does

Read it again and notice the manoeuvre.

It does not say that Masons may believe anything. It requires the moral law, and it rules
out atheism and libertinism. It is not a charter of free thought.

What it does is *relocate the requirement*. The old rule was: be of the religion of your
country. The new rule is: be of the religion in which all men agree.

And since the religion in which all men agree is, necessarily, whatever remains when you
subtract everything people disagree about, the sentence has the effect of removing
confessional doctrine from the qualifications for membership. Trinity, sacraments, papal
authority, predestination, the real presence, church government — the entire catalog of
things Europeans had been killing each other over for two centuries — are placed outside
the lodge door and described as "particular Opinions."

Anderson does not say what the religion in which all men agree consists of. He leaves it
undefined. Later Masonic usage would settle on belief in a Supreme Being, formulated as the
Great Architect of the Universe, and on immortality; the English Constitutions today
require belief in a Supreme Being as a condition of membership, and some continental
jurisdictions dropped the requirement in the nineteenth century, which is the origin of
the great regularity schism. But in 1723 it is deliberately vacant.

Vacancy, again. The same structural feature we found in the ritual.

---

### What it was for

Anderson was not a philosopher of toleration. He was writing for a specific group of men
with a specific problem, and the historian who has done most to identify them is Ric
Berman.

Berman's reconstruction of the circle around the early Grand Lodge — the Horn Tavern lodge
in Westminster above all — shows a tightly connected network: Fellows of the Royal Society,
Newtonians, Huguenot refugees, Whigs, physicians, natural philosophers and men of the
professions. John Theophilus Desaguliers is the emblematic figure: a Huguenot whose family
fled France after the revocation of the Edict of Nantes, an experimental philosopher who
demonstrated Newton's work publicly, a Fellow of the Royal Society, and Grand Master in
1719.[^2]

Berman's argument is that this group did not inherit Freemasonry so much as reconfigure it
— that the organization was radically reshaped by a small, largely self-appointed core
with a coherent social and political program.

Margaret Jacob, approaching from a different angle, places the same development inside the
emergence of European civil society: lodges as places where men of different ranks met on
declared equality, elected their officers, kept accounts, debated procedure, and practiced
a form of associational life that had few other outlets in the period. Her term for this
is the making of a new kind of public.[^3]

Both readings point the same way. The religious formula was not a mystical gesture. It was
the enabling condition for a social experiment: if you want a room in which a Huguenot
refugee, an Anglican clergyman, a Presbyterian Scot, a freethinking Whig and — within a
decade — a Jewish merchant can sit together without the conversation exploding, you must
have a rule that removes the subject.

---

### The cost

I want to state the achievement fairly and then state what it cost, because this book has
no interest in flattering its subject.

The achievement is real and should not be patronised. In 1723, within living memory of a
civil war fought partly over church government, in a country with penal laws against
Catholics and disabilities against Dissenters and Jews, a public organization declared that
it would not ask. Jewish members appear in London lodges within a few years of the
*Constitutions*.[^4] Continental lodges would admit across confessional lines in places
where that was socially extraordinary. The lodge was, for a great many eighteenth-century
men, the only room in their lives where the question did not come up.

The cost is the subject of most of Part VI.

A body that will not specify what it believes cannot object to what people decide it
believes. Anderson's formula purchased breadth by surrendering definition — and a symbolic
system with no authorised interpretation is a system that can be interpreted by anybody.

For a century that hardly mattered. Then, from the 1740s, the high degrees began supplying
content, and nobody had the standing to say no, because the institution had given up the
vocabulary of doctrine. By the nineteenth century, an American Mason could open a book
written by the head of his own Rite and find Kabbalah, Zoroastrianism, Hermeticism and
comparative mythology presented as the inner meaning of his degrees — and there was no
mechanism by which Freemasonry could say *that is not what this means*, because
Freemasonry had spent a hundred and fifty years declining to say what anything meant.

The vacancy that made the Craft tolerant made it colonisable. Those are the same property,
described twice.

---

### The rest of the book

The 1723 *Constitutions* is not only that sentence, and the rest repays attention.

It opens with a history of the craft that occupies nearly half the volume: the Old Charges'
legend, expanded, polished and extended, running from Adam — who is credited with geometry
written on his heart — through the Flood, Egypt, Solomon's Temple, the classical world, and
so to Britain and the present.[^5] It is antiquarian in manner and entirely uncritical. It
is also the mechanism by which the medieval craft legend, a trade's private self-flattery,
became a printed and widely distributed account available to every reader in Europe.

That matters more than its accuracy. From 1723 onward, anybody in Europe who wanted to know
what Freemasonry claimed about its own origins could buy the answer. The claim was: we
descend from the builders of the Temple, and our art is geometry, and our lineage runs to
the beginning of the world.

When Frenchmen began constructing high degrees in the 1740s, this was the raw material they
were working from. When nineteenth-century occultists looked for ancient wisdom in Masonry,
this was the text that told them it was there. Anderson's history is the source of the very
belief this book spends its length testing — and Anderson himself almost certainly regarded
it as antiquarian entertainment rather than as doctrine.

The volume also contains the Charges of a Free-Mason, the General Regulations, and — a
detail worth noticing — a set of Masonic songs with music, to be sung at the annual feast.
The founding document of the world's great secret society ends with drinking songs.

---

### Who Anderson was

James Anderson is a slightly absurd figure and I have some affection for him.

He was a Scot, an ordained Presbyterian minister of a London congregation, a compiler by
temperament. He lost money in the South Sea Bubble. He published, besides the
*Constitutions*, an enormous work of royal genealogy, which tells you what kind of mind he
had: the kind that likes descent, lineage and the tracing of things back.

He was commissioned to digest the old Gothic Constitutions into a new and better method,
and he did. Whether he also invented the 1717 story, or repeated what he had been told, or
conflated things he half remembered, we cannot say. What is clear is that the man who gave
Freemasonry its written history was a genealogist by inclination, working for an
institution that wanted a pedigree, at a moment when nobody could check.

That is not a conspiracy. It is a job description, and the results of it are still being
argued about three hundred years later.

---

We now have the institution: a Grand Lodge of uncertain birthday, a printed constitution, a
religious formula of unprecedented breadth, a legendary history in wide circulation, and a
membership of physicians, noblemen and natural philosophers.

What we have not yet examined is the thing they actually did when the door was shut. Not
the secrets — we covered those, and they were thin — but the *performance*: a room arranged
in a particular way, men standing in particular places, a script, a drama, and a candidate
in the middle of it.

That is where the machine does its work, and it is where we go next.
