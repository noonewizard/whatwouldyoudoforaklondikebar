# CHAPTER SEVENTEEN
## The Lodge as Theater

Consider the room.

An upstairs chamber in a tavern, hired for the evening. Perhaps thirty feet by twenty. A
table, chairs, candles, a fire if the season demands it. When the lodge is not sitting, it
is a room where people eat.

And then, for two hours, it is not.

The transformation is accomplished by almost nothing. Chairs are placed: one in the east,
raised if possible; one in the west; one in the south. The door is guarded. Something is
laid out on the floor — in the early eighteenth century, a design drawn in chalk and
charcoal on the boards, which the newest member erased with a mop and bucket at the end of
the evening, and which would later be replaced by a painted cloth and then by a framed
board hung on the wall.[^1] A book is opened on the table with two instruments laid on it.

That is the whole apparatus. A tavern room, some chairs, a drawing on the floor, and men
standing where they are told.

It works because of where they stand.

---

### Blocking

Theater people call the arrangement of bodies in a space *blocking*, and it is the first
thing a director does, because an audience reads position before it hears a word.

The Masonic lodge is blocked with great precision and the meaning of the positions is never
explained to the candidate. He learns it by occupying the space.

There is an east, and the principal officer sits in it, and light comes from there. The
association between east, sunrise, authority and knowledge does not need to be taught to
anyone raised in a European culture; it is already installed by every church the man has
ever entered. There is a west, with a second officer, and a south with a third. The
candidate enters from a direction that is not east. He is conducted around the room in a
fixed direction, stopping at stations. He is brought to the center. He kneels at the
center. When he rises he is placed somewhere he was not before.

None of this is stated as doctrine. All of it is experienced as fact.

Catherine Bell, writing about ritual in general, made the observation that does the most
work here: ritual, she argued, should be thought of not as a category of activity but as a
*strategy* — a way of acting that distinguishes itself from ordinary acting, and that
produces authority precisely by being formalised, repetitive and set apart. What ritual
does, on her account, is not communicate a message but structure a situation, and the
structuring is the point.[^2]

A tavern room in which men stand in cardinal positions, speak in fixed formulae and move in
prescribed directions is not a tavern room. Nobody has to say so.

---

### Script

The eighteenth-century lodge ran on memorized call and response.

The catechisms in the exposures give the form. *Are you a Mason? I am so taken and
accepted among brothers and fellows. How shall I know you to be a Mason? By signs and
tokens and perfect points of my entrance.* And onward, question by question, through the
form of the lodge, its supports, its covering, its furniture, its jewels, its situation.[^3]

Three features of this are worth naming.

**It is rhythmic and formulaic**, which is how oral material survives. The questions do not
vary. The answers do not vary. A man who learned them in 1730 could be examined in 1770 and
still have them. Before general literacy, this was simply how bodies of knowledge were
carried, and the fact that Masonic ritual was transmitted by memory rather than by book —
in most jurisdictions it is still learned by heart — is a direct inheritance of that world.

**It is dialogic.** Nobody lectures. The knowledge exists between two people and has to be
produced by both. You cannot possess the catechism privately; it only exists when it is
performed. That is a striking property for a body of secret knowledge to have, and it means
the knowledge is inseparable from the relationship.

**And it is a test.** The catechism is how a stranger is examined. Its social function is
identification, but its ritual function is something else: it turns membership into
something continuously demonstrable. You are a Mason to the extent that you can answer.

---

### Drama

By 1730 there is something in the lodge that is not a catechism at all.

The third degree, as reported in *Masonry Dissected*, contains a story with characters and
a plot, and — critically — the candidate does not hear the story. He *plays* it. He is the
master builder. He is accosted, struck, killed. He is laid out. He is buried. He is
searched for, found, and raised.

This is a different technology from question and answer, and it is the innovation that
makes Freemasonry what it became.

The theater historian's term for what the candidate undergoes is not quite available,
because ordinary theater has an audience watching actors. Here the audience *are* the
actors, and the protagonist has not read the script, does not know what happens next,
cannot see for part of it, and is the only person in the room who does not know how it
ends.

The effect is not that he learns a legend. It is that a legend happens to him.

And note what is required for this to work: a room arranged in advance, men who know their
parts, an order of events, properties, costume of a sort, and a candidate in a prepared
state. That is a production. The lodge of 1730 is a company of amateur players performing
the same piece repeatedly, each time for a single spectator who is also the lead.

---

### Kit

The material culture deserves a paragraph, because it is where the theater becomes visible
to us at this distance.

**The apron.** A working mason's protective garment, made of leather, entirely practical in
origin — and then made smaller, made of white lambskin, decorated, and turned into the
principal badge of membership. By the later eighteenth century, aprons were being painted
with elaborate symbolic scenes and are now museum objects. The trajectory from protective
clothing to ceremonial regalia is the whole history of Freemasonry in a single garment.

**The tracing board.** The design drawn in chalk on the floor, later painted on cloth or
board: a diagram containing the symbols of the degree, arranged as a composition. It is a
teaching aid, a stage set and an icon at once, and it is where the Craft's symbolism
becomes *visual* rather than verbal.

**The working tools.** Square, level, plumb-rule, gauge, gavel, trowel, compasses. Real
tools, or models of them, handled and explained.

**The book, with the square and compasses upon it.**

What strikes me about this inventory is its restraint. There is nothing exotic in it.
Nothing is imported. Every object is either a builder's tool, a builder's garment, or a
Bible. In a century that produced a great deal of elaborate ceremonial in other contexts,
the Craft's stage kit is the contents of a workshop.

The exoticism arrives later, with the high degrees, and it arrives as *addition* — swords,
crowns, coffins, Hebrew letters, crosses, eagles. When you see those, you are looking at
the eighteenth century's contribution, not at the Craft's inheritance.

---

### Why theater rather than teaching

A question worth asking directly: why go to this trouble? If the aim is to convey that a
man should be upright, why not say so?

Three answers, all supported by what we can observe.

**Because it is remembered.** A proposition heard is forgotten; an event undergone is not.
This is not a mystical claim, and it does not require any theory of ritual beyond ordinary
observation of how memory works. The literature on memory is consistent that events
combining strong emotion, novelty and bodily involvement are retained far better than
information received passively.[^4] The Masonic initiation is engineered — deliberately or
by long refinement — to combine all three.

**Because it produces commitment.** A man who has been through something and sworn an oath
in the middle of it is invested in a way that a man who has read a pamphlet is not. This is
sociologically banal and it is why fraternities, regiments, medical residencies and
religious orders all do a version of it.

**And because it does not fix a meaning.** This is the subtle one and it is the point of
this chapter.

If the lodge taught doctrine, its members would have to agree with the doctrine. Some
would not. The institution would fracture along the same lines as the churches it had so
carefully declined to imitate.

Instead the lodge presents an *experience*, and leaves the interpretation open. The
candidate is raised from a symbolic grave. What that signifies — the immortality of the
soul, the moral resurrection of the reformed man, the death of the old self, the
persistence of a craft beyond its masters — is not specified. Every man supplies his own,
and every man is right, because nobody has been given the authority to be wrong.

Which is the same vacancy we found in Anderson's religious formula, and in the secret
itself, and it is now possible to state the pattern as a general property of this
institution:

**Freemasonry withholds meaning at every level, and derives its durability from doing so.**

That property is why it survived the Enlightenment, the Revolution, the Anti-Masonic
panics, two world wars and the twentieth century. It is also, precisely, why it could be
filled with theurgy in 1761 and with Hermetic Qabalah in 1888 — and why, when those things
were poured in, no one inside the institution had the standing to object.

We have the room, the blocking, the script and the drama. What we have not asked is why the
whole thing is dressed as *building*. Of all the trades in Europe, why did this one produce
an initiatory system — and why did the science it claims as its own turn out to be the one
science that had, for two thousand years, been considered a route to God?
