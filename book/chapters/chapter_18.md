# CHAPTER EIGHTEEN
## The Geometry of Initiation

Above the door of Plato's Academy, according to a tradition that is probably later than
Plato, was an inscription: *let no one ignorant of geometry enter here*.

Whether or not it was ever carved, the sentiment is authentic to the tradition. In the
*Republic*, Plato argues that geometry draws the soul toward truth, because it compels the
mind to contemplate objects that cannot be seen — the perfect circle, the line without
width — and thereby turns it away from the world of appearances. Geometry is not useful
knowledge. It is a training in looking at the right kind of thing.

Two thousand years later, in a tavern room in London, a candidate is told that Masonry is
founded on geometry, that geometry is the noblest of the sciences, and that by it the
mason learns to trace the works of the Almighty.

The line between those two rooms is not direct. But the reason both exist is the same, and
it is worth understanding, because it explains why — of all the crafts in Europe — this one
produced an initiatory philosophy and the tanners and coopers did not.

---

### Why geometry and not something else

Three reasons, and they compound.

**First: geometry was the one liberal art with a trade attached.**

The medieval curriculum divided knowledge into the trivium — grammar, rhetoric, logic — and
the quadrivium — arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy. These were the seven liberal
sciences: the knowledge proper to a free man, as against the mechanical arts of those who
worked with their hands.

Masonry sat, uniquely, on the boundary. A mason worked with his hands and was therefore a
mechanic. But what he did was *apply geometry*. He set out a right angle, struck an arc,
calculated a load, raised a vault. The Old Charges make the identification explicit and
repeatedly: masonry *is* geometry, the science is the craft, and the craft is the science.

No other trade could say this. A weaver has no liberal art. A blacksmith has no liberal
art. A mason has one, and the Old Charges make sure he knows it.

That single fact does more to explain the emergence of speculative Freemasonry than any
theory of ancient transmission. Here is a trade whose own founding documents tell its
members that they practise a branch of philosophy. Give that trade a lodge system, a
written record and an oath, and then let educated gentlemen hear the Charges read aloud at
an admission — and the attraction is not mysterious at all.

**Second: geometry was the century's prestige science.**

The lodge that produced the 1723 *Constitutions* was thick with Fellows of the Royal
Society. Desaguliers demonstrated Newtonian mechanics for a living. Newton had, in living
memory, shown that the motions of the heavens obeyed mathematics — that the universe was,
literally, a geometrical object. In that world, a fraternity organised around geometry was
not archaic. It was modern.

This is a point that gets lost when Freemasonry is read backwards through its
nineteenth-century occult interpreters. The Grand Lodge of the 1720s was not looking
backward to Egypt. It was looking at Newton. Its Grand Master was a man who did experiments
in public. The symbolism of the square and compasses in that decade sits closer to the
Royal Society than to the Hermetic tradition, and the sensible reading of an institution
that describes God as the Great Architect of the Universe is that its members had read the
*Principia* and thought the universe looked designed.

**Third: geometry connected the workshop to God without any theology.**

And here is where it becomes useful in the way this book cares about.

The proposition *the universe is built according to measure* is available to a Catholic, an
Anglican, a Presbyterian, a Huguenot, a Jew and a deist. It is in the Book of Wisdom: God
ordered all things by measure and number and weight. It is in Plato's *Timaeus*. It is in
Newton. It commits you to nothing that divides Christians from each other or from anybody
else.

So the lodge acquired a sacred science that did not require a sacred doctrine — which is
precisely what Anderson's religious formula demanded. Geometry is the content that fits the
vacancy.

### The tools become moral

The mechanism by which this becomes a system of ethics is so simple that it is easy to
underrate.

A square tests whether an angle is true. A level tests whether a surface is horizontal. A
plumb-rule tests whether a line is vertical. A gauge divides a length. These are
instruments of *verification*: their entire function is to determine whether something
conforms to a standard that is external, impersonal and not negotiable.

Transfer that to conduct and you have a complete ethical vocabulary with no metaphysics in
it. To act on the square is to act correctly, and correctness is not a matter of opinion,
because a right angle is not a matter of opinion. To be upright is to be plumb. To meet
another man on the level is to acknowledge that the standard applies to both of you
regardless of rank — and in a society organised by rank, in a room containing a duke and an
apothecary, that is not a platitude.

This is the operation the Craft performs on every object it touches, and it is worth naming
because it recurs: **a physical instrument of measurement is converted into a moral
instrument of measurement, with the logic of verification carried across intact.**

It is not allegory in the loose sense of "this stands for that." It is an argument: the
moral order, like the physical one, has a standard against which things can be tested, and
the standard does not care who you are.

### The candidate as material

There is a second symbolic logic in the lodge, and it runs in the opposite direction.

The working tools are what the mason uses. But the mason is also, in the Craft's imagery,
what is worked on. The rough ashlar is a stone as it comes from the quarry: irregular,
unusable. The perfect ashlar is the same stone squared, smoothed and ready to be set into a
wall. The candidate is told that he is the first and should become the second.

So the initiate occupies both positions at once. He holds the square, and he is being
squared. He is the craftsman and the material.

This doubling is the most philosophically interesting thing in Craft symbolism, and it is
also the point at which the Masonic system comes closest to the traditions this book has
been tracking — without, so far as any evidence shows, having borrowed anything from them.

Consider. Alchemy's operator works on a material that the tradition tells him is also
himself. Iamblichus's theurgist performs an operation whose object is his own ascent.
Hermetic regeneration is a process the practitioner undergoes and conducts. And the Masonic
candidate is simultaneously mason and stone.

That is a real structural convergence, and I have no route to offer for it. Nobody has.
What I would suggest — and I offer it as a hypothesis, not a finding — is that it is a
convergence rather than a transmission: that any system which sets out to change a person
through their own deliberate action will arrive at the same doubling, because the doubling
is simply what self-transformation *is*. You cannot be improved without being both the
improver and the improved.

If that is right, then the resemblance between Masonry and the esoteric traditions at this
point is not a clue about the past. It is a fact about the logic of self-transformation,
which any sufficiently serious system will discover independently.

That hypothesis is one of the two or three things this book is genuinely arguing, and it
will be tested properly in Part VII.

### The building that is not there

One last observation before we go to the Temple.

Speculative Freemasonry is a system of building imagery practised by people who do not
build anything.

The lodge has a floor, pillars, a covering of clouded canopy or starry heaven, a
foundation, and an orientation. It has tools and materials and a design. The candidate
learns to square a stone that does not exist for a wall that will not be built, using
instruments he will never employ, in a structure that is described in detail and is
nowhere.

I do not think this was felt as a loss. I think it is the point. Once the building is
imaginary, the vocabulary is free — it can be applied to a man's character, to a
congregation, to a nation, to the cosmos, to the soul. The Craft became universal at
exactly the moment it stopped being about construction.

And it had, ready to hand, the greatest imaginary building in the Western tradition: a
structure described in the Bible in obsessive dimensional detail, destroyed twice, never
excavated, and reconstructed on paper by Renaissance architects who had never seen a stone
of it.
