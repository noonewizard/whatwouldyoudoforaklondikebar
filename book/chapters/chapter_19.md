# CHAPTER NINETEEN
## Solomon's Temple

No one has ever seen it. No stone of it has been identified. It stood, if the biblical
chronology is even roughly right, for about three hundred and seventy years, and it was
destroyed by the Babylonians in 586 BCE. The building that replaced it was rebuilt again
by Herod and destroyed by the Romans in 70 CE. The site is among the most politically
impossible places on earth to excavate.

What survives is a description — two of them, in 1 Kings 6–7 and 2 Chronicles 3–4, with
further material in Ezekiel's visionary temple and in Josephus — and the descriptions do
not entirely agree.

From that, Western Europe built a thousand years of architecture, a theory of proportion,
a political vocabulary, and the central symbol of the world's largest fraternal
organization.

The Temple is the most productive building in history, and it is not there.

---

### What the text gives you

The biblical account is oddly specific about some things and silent about others. It gives
dimensions: the house sixty cubits long, twenty wide, thirty high. It gives the porch, the
side chambers, the windows, the cedar lining, the gold. It gives the inner sanctuary — the
holy of holies, a cube of twenty cubits, containing the ark and two enormous carved
cherubim.

And it gives two pillars, standing before the porch, cast in bronze, with elaborate
capitals of lily-work and pomegranates and chain-work. They are given names, which is
unusual and has never been satisfactorily explained: the right-hand pillar Jachin, the
left-hand pillar Boaz.[^1]

It also gives a workman. Solomon sends to Tyre for a man skilled in bronze — the text
calls him Hiram, or Huram, a widow's son of the tribe of Naphtali, whose father was a man
of Tyre — and this craftsman makes the pillars and the vessels.[^2]

That is the entire biblical basis for the Masonic legend. A craftsman named Hiram, of
mixed descent, a widow's son, working in bronze. He is not the architect. He is not
murdered. Nothing whatever happens to him. He makes the pillars and the text moves on.

What happens between that verse and the drama performed in a London tavern in 1730 is the
subject of Chapter 22. Here I want to establish something prior: why the *building* came to
carry so much weight.

---

### The perfect building

The reason is theological and it is very old.

If God specified the dimensions — and the text is emphatic that the plan was given, to
Moses for the tabernacle and to David for the Temple — then those dimensions are not
arbitrary. They are revealed proportion. A building made to divine specification is a
disclosure of divine order in the same way that scripture is.

From that premise, an entire tradition follows. If the Temple's proportions are revealed,
then reconstructing them is theology. If they encode a pattern, then the pattern may be
found elsewhere — in the human body, in the cosmos, in music. And if a Christian church
can be built to those proportions, then the church participates in the revelation.

Medieval and Renaissance architecture took all of this seriously and literally. The Temple
is invoked at Saint-Denis and in the cathedral tradition that followed. Renaissance
theorists of proportion worked with it. And in the 1590s and early 1600s, two Spanish
Jesuits, Jerónimo Prado and Juan Bautista Villalpando, produced an enormous illustrated
commentary on Ezekiel containing a full architectural reconstruction of the Temple —
engraved plans, elevations, sections, orders of columns — arguing that it had been built in
a divinely revealed order of which the classical orders were a degenerate memory.[^3]

Villalpando's Temple is a magnificent and entirely imaginary Renaissance palace. It was
also enormously influential: reproduced, copied, argued with, used by architects, and
consulted for two centuries.

So by 1700, educated Europe possessed a detailed visual idea of Solomon's Temple. It did
not come from archaeology. It came from a Jesuit's engravings, made from a prophet's vision
and a set of biblical measurements, in the architectural language of the late sixteenth
century.

When eighteenth-century Freemasons imagined the Temple, this is substantially what they
imagined.

---

### The claim to test

Which brings us to a claim worth taking seriously, because a version of it is made in a
great deal of popular writing: that Freemasonry preserves genuine knowledge of Solomon's
Temple — traditions, measurements, secrets of its construction, handed down through the
craft.

Run the questions.

**Provenance.** Is there any document, of any age, containing Temple information not
derivable from the Bible, Josephus and the Renaissance reconstructions? None has been
produced.

**Chronology.** For a tradition to carry genuine information from the tenth century BCE to
the eighteenth century CE, it must cross the Babylonian destruction, the exile, the Second
Temple period, the Roman destruction, and fifteen centuries of European history in which
the craft of building was repeatedly interrupted. Each of those is a break point.

**Transmission.** Nobody has proposed a route that survives inspection. The Templar version
— that the order excavated the Temple Mount and recovered something — is a modern
speculation with no medieval evidence, and we will deal with it in Chapter 26.

**Intertextuality.** This is where the claim dies. Everything in the Masonic Temple is
traceable to available sources. The dimensions are biblical. The pillars are biblical, with
their names. The craftsman is biblical. The architectural imagination is Villalpando's.
There is no residue — no detail that requires an independent source.

**Institutional continuity.** The masons' craft in medieval Europe is documented, and it is
a European building trade with European techniques and no Levantine connection.

**Anachronism.** The Masonic Temple has features that could not have belonged to the
original: it is furnished with the working tools of the early modern European building
trade, its pillars are described with globes on top — celestial and terrestrial globes,
which are early modern instruments — and its symbolism assumes the Renaissance
architectural orders.

**Verdict: unsupported, comprehensively.** The Masonic Temple is a *literary and
architectural* construction of the early modern period, built from the Bible and from
Renaissance reconstructions, and it contains nothing that requires an ancient channel.

---

### What it is actually for

And having said that, I want to say the other thing, because a book that stopped there
would have missed the point entirely.

The Temple's power in Masonic symbolism has nothing to do with whether the Craft knows
anything about it. It works *because* it is unavailable.

Consider its properties as a symbol.

**It is destroyed.** A building that still stood could be visited, measured, disagreed
about. A destroyed building is perfect, because nothing can contradict it. The Temple is
available only as description, which means it is available only as imagination.

**It was built to a given plan.** So it can stand for any project undertaken according to a
design not of one's own making — which is to say, for a life.

**It was built by a workforce.** The biblical account gives numbers: tens of thousands of
laborers, hewers, bearers of burdens, overseers. It is a *collective* achievement with a
division of labor and a hierarchy of skill — which maps onto a lodge of apprentices,
fellows and masters with no adjustment at all.

**It is sacred without being sectarian.** Jews, Christians and Muslims all recognize it.
Anderson's formula requires exactly this: a religious symbol available to everyone in the
room. There is no other building in the world that qualifies.

**It can be rebuilt inwardly.** Once the physical structure is gone, the injunction to
build the Temple becomes available as metaphor — and Paul had already made the move, in a
letter to Corinth, asking whether his readers did not know that they were the temple of
God. That metaphor was seventeen centuries old when the Craft picked it up. It was not an
esoteric secret. It was in every sermon.

Put those five properties together and you have the ideal symbolic object: universally
recognized, infinitely interpretable, incapable of refutation, and already pre-loaded with
the meaning *you are the building*.

The eighteenth century did not select the Temple because it possessed hidden knowledge of
it. It selected the Temple because the Temple was the best available machine for the job,
and because a craft of builders looking for the noblest building in its own tradition had
only one plausible candidate.

---

### The shape of the lodge

One consequence worth noting before we go on.

Because the Temple is the model, the lodge acquires its shape. The lodge is said to be
situated east to west, as the Temple was. It has pillars. It has a chequered pavement,
after the Temple's floor. It has a sanctuary at the east. The officers' stations correspond
to points in a building that no longer exists.

Which means that every Masonic lodge in the world is, formally, a model of a building known
only from a text — and every member who walks its floor is walking a reconstruction of a
reconstruction of a description.

That is a strange and rather beautiful fact, and it explains why the Craft's imagery proved
so portable. A lodge is not a place. It is a *plan*, and a plan can be laid out in a tavern
in Westminster, a warehouse in Boston, a tent in a colonial garrison, or a room above a
shop in Calcutta. The Temple travels because the Temple was never anywhere.

Now to its furniture — starting with the two instruments that have become, for most of the
world, the only thing anybody can identify about Freemasonry at all.
