# CHAPTER TWENTY
## The Square and the Compasses

It is on gravestones in Ohio and on lodge doors in Ghana. It is on pocket watches, ties,
rings, car bumpers and the sides of buildings. It is one of the most widely recognized
emblems in the world, and the vast majority of people who recognize it could not say what
it means beyond "Freemasons."

Two draftsman's instruments, interlocked. Sometimes a letter G between them. Sometimes,
in American usage, the compasses are described as opened to a particular angle, and the
relative position of square and compasses varies with the degree.

Where did it come from, what did it mean, and when?

---

### The instruments

Both are ancient and both are unremarkable.

A square — two arms fixed at a right angle — is among the oldest tools in the world. It
exists wherever people build with rectangles, which is to say nearly everywhere. Egyptian
tomb paintings show them. Roman examples survive. Medieval European masons used them.

Compasses — two legs joined at a pivot, for striking arcs and transferring distances — are
equally widespread and equally old.

They belong together because they are the two instruments of practical geometry: one tests
and produces the straight and the right-angled, the other the curved and the measured. With
those two tools and a straight edge you can construct essentially the whole of Euclidean
plane geometry, which is why Euclid's constructions are called compass-and-straightedge
constructions.

So the pairing is not symbolic in origin. It is what is in the toolbox.

---

### Before the Craft

Now the part that requires discipline.

The square and compasses appear in European visual culture long before Freemasonry, and
this fact is routinely deployed as evidence of an ancient esoteric pedigree. Let us look at
what the appearances actually are.

**In craft heraldry and mason's marks.** Masons' guilds and companies used tools as
identifying devices, as every trade did — shears for cloth-workers, a last for cobblers.
The London Company of Masons' arms include compasses. Gravestones of masons across medieval
and early modern Europe are carved with the tools of the trade. This is professional
identification and nothing more.

**In images of God as geometer.** A striking medieval iconographic tradition shows the
Creator leaning from the heavens with a pair of compasses, measuring out the world — the
most famous English example being the frontispiece of the thirteenth-century *Bible
moralisée*, and the most famous later one William Blake's *Ancient of Days* of 1794, which
is a knowing reuse of the tradition.

This image is genuinely important for the intellectual background of Chapter 18, because
it visualises exactly the proposition that the universe was made by measure. But note
carefully: it shows *compasses*, used by *God*, in a *Christian devotional* context. It is
not a square-and-compasses emblem, it is not Masonic, and it is not esoteric. It is
scriptural illustration — Proverbs 8:27, "when he set a compass upon the face of the depth."

**In the wider symbolic repertoire.** Squares and compasses appear in Chinese iconography —
the mythological figures Fuxi and Nüwa are conventionally depicted holding a square and
compasses respectively, in imagery going back to the Han dynasty. This is a real and
striking parallel and it is frequently cited as evidence of a universal ancient tradition.

It is also, on inspection, exactly what the Parallel-Is-Not-Descent rule exists for. Two
civilisations that build with right angles and circles will both produce a square and a
pair of compasses, and both may use them to represent the ordering of the world, because
that is what the tools do. There is no transmission route, no chronology of contact
relevant to the emblem's Masonic use, and no textual connection. The resemblance tells us
something about the universality of construction. It tells us nothing about Freemasonry.

---

### When the Masonic meaning appears

The documented history is more modest and much more interesting.

The tools appear in the Craft's own early material as *working tools among others* — square,
level, plumb, gauge, gavel, compasses — each with a moral interpretation attached. The
square is early and prominent: to act "on the square" is idiomatic English by the
seventeenth century, and the phrase's currency shows how natural the moral transfer was.

The compasses acquire their distinctive Masonic meaning in the eighteenth-century ritual
material: to circumscribe desires and keep passions within due bounds. The image is
precise — a pair of compasses defines a limit, and the moral point is limitation rather
than measurement.

The combination of square and compasses with the open book as the "Three Great Lights"
belongs to the eighteenth century and is attested in the exposures and the ritual
literature of that period.[^1] The emblem as a *logo* — square and compasses interlocked,
standing alone, used as an identifying badge on buildings and objects — becomes standard in
the nineteenth century, with the growth of Masonic material culture, printing and
regalia-manufacturing.

The letter G is later still in its prominence, and is discussed in the next chapter.

**So the dating is: tools, always. Moral interpretation, from at least the early
eighteenth century and probably before. The emblem as a public badge, nineteenth
century.**

---

### The later overlay

And then, from the middle of the nineteenth century, something is added that is not in the
eighteenth-century material at all.

In the occult reading — Lévi, and everyone downstream of Lévi — the square and compasses
become a polarity. The square is matter, the compasses spirit; the square is passive, the
compasses active; the square is the terrestrial, the compasses the celestial; and their
interlocking is the union of opposites, the conjunction, the hexagram of ascending and
descending triangles.

This is a genuinely elegant reading. It is also demonstrably a nineteenth-century addition.
The eighteenth-century sources contain nothing of it. The moral interpretation they give is
the one described above — rectitude and restraint — and it is notably unmetaphysical.

The polarity reading arrives with the occult revival and is carried into American Masonry
by Pike, who took a great deal from Lévi. Within two generations it had become, for many
Masons, simply what the emblem meant.

I want to be careful not to sneer at this. Symbols acquire meanings; that is what symbols
are for, and a meaning discovered in 1856 is not a lesser meaning than one recorded in
1730. The error is not interpretation. The error is *dating* — the assumption that because
a reading is profound, it is old.

---

### A note on the relative position

One small point of Masonic practice, included because it illustrates how differently these
things work from the outside.

In many jurisdictions, the position of the compasses relative to the square on the open
volume of sacred law differs by degree: both points hidden, one point revealed, both points
revealed. It is a simple, elegant index of progression, immediately legible to anyone who
knows the system and invisible to anyone who does not.

This is characteristic of how the Craft actually uses its symbolism. The meaning is not
hidden in the sense of being encoded. It is hidden in the sense of being *unremarked*. A
man who has been through the degrees sees a lodge board and reads information from it; a
visitor sees a decorative arrangement. No cipher is involved. The knowledge is entirely
contextual, and that is a very different thing from a secret.

It also varies by jurisdiction, like almost everything else in this book, and any account
that presents one national practice as universal is wrong before it starts.

---

### What the emblem actually tells us

Step back and ask what the square and compasses, taken as historical evidence, establish.

They establish that Freemasonry's self-image is **a craft, not a cult**. The badge is two
tools. Not a symbol of a deity, not a mystical sign, not a letter of a sacred alphabet: the
contents of a workman's bag.

They establish that the Craft's ethics are **verificationist**. The tools test. The moral
system they carry is one of conformity to an external standard that does not care about
your opinion.

And they establish that the Craft was **content to be legible**. A body seriously concerned
with concealment does not put its emblem on the front of its buildings.

That last point is worth holding onto when we reach the conspiracy literature in Chapter
50. The organization accused of running the world by stealth has, for two centuries,
advertised its premises with a sign, published its constitutions, listed its officers in
directories, and marched in public processions in full regalia at the laying of corner
stones.

There is a great deal that is genuinely hidden in the history this book is telling. It is
almost never hidden by the Freemasons.
