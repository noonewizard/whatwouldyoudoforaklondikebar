# CHAPTER TWENTY-TWO
## Hiram

The central legend of Freemasonry has no author, no manuscript, and no datable moment of
composition. It appears, complete, in a hostile pamphlet printed in London in 1730, and
before that there is nothing.

This is the most remarkable fact in Masonic history, and it is remarkable in a way that
cuts against both the parties usually arguing about it. The ancient-wisdom school has to
explain why a legend supposedly carried from antiquity leaves no trace until the reign of
George II. The sceptical school has to explain how a drama of that quality was composed by
persons unknown, adopted across an entire institution within a few years, and left no
draft, no attribution and no record of the decision.

Neither has managed it.

---

### The legend

As given in *Masonry Dissected* and in the developed workings that followed, the story runs
roughly as follows. I summarise rather than quote, and I note again that versions differ
substantially between jurisdictions.

At the building of Solomon's Temple, three grand masters hold the secrets of the master's
degree: Solomon, Hiram King of Tyre, and Hiram Abiff, the chief architect. Fifteen fellows
of the craft, impatient for the master's word before they have earned it, conspire to
obtain it by force; twelve of them repent, three do not. The three accost Hiram Abiff at
the three gates of the Temple as he leaves at high twelve. Each demands the secret; he
refuses; each strikes him, and the third blow kills him.

They bury the body, hastily, and mark the grave. The workmen, finding the master missing,
search. The grave is discovered — in most versions by a sprig of acacia which the murderers
had planted or which had grown there. The body is raised. Because the master's word died
with him, a substitute word is adopted.

And the candidate plays Hiram.

---

### What is and is not biblical

The raw material is scriptural and the plot is not.

Scripture gives a craftsman named Hiram or Huram, sent from Tyre, a widow's son, skilled in
bronze, who makes the pillars and the vessels of the Temple. That is all. He is not called
an architect. He is not murdered. Nothing in the Bible or in Josephus supplies the
conspiracy, the three blows, the burial, the acacia, the search or the raising.

The name *Abiff* is not biblical either in the form the Craft uses; it derives from a
Hebrew phrase in 2 Chronicles rendered variously in different versions — the Geneva Bible's
handling of the passage is generally taken to be the immediate source of the Masonic
form.[^1]

So the legend takes a biblical person, gives him a promotion, and then constructs an
entirely non-biblical drama around him.

---

### The candidates for a source

Four proposals have been made seriously. Let us take them in order.

**One: it descends from the ancient mysteries.** The dying-and-rising figure — Osiris,
Adonis, Attis, Tammuz — is offered as the archetype, with Masonry preserving a mystery-cult
initiation.

This fails on everything except chronology. There is no transmission route, no textual
relation, no institutional continuity, and no evidence that eighteenth-century Englishmen
had access to the content of the ancient mysteries, which were genuinely secret and are
poorly attested even now. The comparison was made *by* eighteenth- and nineteenth-century
writers — it is in the Masonic literature from the 1770s onward, and it is everywhere in
Pike — but it is their interpretation, not their source. They found the parallel, as we
would, by reading about Osiris; they did not inherit it.

**Two: it is alchemical.** Handled in Chapter 11. The parallel is real and the descent is
unsupported.

**Three: it derives from the Templars.** Chronologically impossible in any documented form,
and the Templar material enters Masonry in the 1730s and after — *later* than the Hiramic
legend, not earlier. Chapter 26.

**Four: it was composed in the 1720s, in England, by persons unknown, out of biblical
material, the craft's existing legends, and the dramatic repertoire of the period.**

The fourth is the only one that fits the evidence, and it is worth noticing that it is also
the only one nobody finds satisfying. There is something about "we do not know who wrote
it" that the human mind refuses, and a great deal of Masonic historiography is the record
of that refusal.

---

### Why the 1720s

The case for composition in that decade rests on four things.

The earliest catechisms — Scottish, 1696 to about 1714 — describe *two* degrees and contain
no Hiramic legend. There is a word, and the tradition of a lost or substituted word appears
in embryo, but there is no murder and no raising.

The earliest English exposures — 1723, 1724 — also show two degrees and no legend.

*Masonry Dissected*, 1730, has three degrees and the full legend.

And Grand Lodge's own records show the third degree being worked in London lodges from the
mid-1720s onward, with the practice spreading through that decade.[^2]

A window of roughly six years, in one city, at the exact moment when a new Grand Lodge was
standardising and publishing and expanding — and when, not coincidentally, London theatre
was in one of its most vigorous periods.

---

### What kind of object it is

I want to make an observation about the legend as a piece of construction, because it is
frequently discussed for its supposed sources and rarely for its craftsmanship.

It is extremely well made.

Consider the problem its author faced. The Craft had two degrees, a word, and a great deal
of moral instruction. It needed a third degree — the pressure for one seems to have come
from the ordinary desire for further advancement — and that degree had to do something the
first two did not.

The solution chosen was to give the candidate an *experience of loss*. Not of gain: the
third degree is the only one in which the candidate does not receive the thing he came for.
The master's word is lost. It died with Hiram. What he is given is a substitute, explicitly
described as such.

That is a genuinely unusual design decision, and I think it is the reason the degree works.
A ceremony that promised a secret and delivered one would be a transaction. A ceremony that
promises a secret, kills the only man who has it, and then hands the candidate a
placeholder — while he is lying on the floor playing the corpse — is doing something else
entirely. It is teaching that the thing sought is not available, that the search is the
inheritance, and that what is transmitted is the search.

Whether or not its author intended that, it is what the degree performs. And it is, I
think, the single most interesting idea in Freemasonry.

It also explains a great deal about what happened next. An institution whose central
ceremony declares that the true word is lost has built an open question into its own
foundations. For two hundred years, men would enter that ceremony and come out convinced
that something was missing — because they had been *told* something was missing — and some
of them would go looking for it.

They looked in the high degrees, which promised to restore the word. They looked in the
Royal Arch, whose entire subject is the recovery of the lost name. They looked in Kabbalah,
in Egypt, in the Templars, in Hermeticism, in Theosophy, in the Golden Dawn.

The occult history of Freemasonry is, in a real sense, the history of men taking the third
degree literally.

---

### The acacia

A small closing observation.

The grave is found by a sprig of acacia. The acacia is evergreen, which is the ordinary
symbolism of immortality; it is also, and more interestingly, the shittim wood of the
Tabernacle, the timber God specified for the ark and its poles in Exodus.

But the function in the drama is not symbolic. It is *forensic*. The murderers bury the
body and something growing marks the place, and the searchers find it because of a detail
the murderers did not control.

That is the structure of the entire ceremony in miniature: the thing that was hidden is
found because burial is imperfect, and something always comes up through the ground.

I would not put much weight on that as an interpretation of the legend. I put a great deal
of weight on it as a description of this book's method. Institutions that conceal
themselves leave sprigs. The Scottish lodges kept minutes because an administrator told
them to. Anderson published a constitution. Prichard printed the secrets for sixpence.
Stukeley wrote in his diary. Taxil confessed in a Paris hall. Westcott's German letters
were examined and the German was wrong.

Something always comes up through the ground. That is why this history can be written at
all.
