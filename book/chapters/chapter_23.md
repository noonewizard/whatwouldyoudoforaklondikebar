# CHAPTER TWENTY-THREE
## What the Exposures Reveal

Between 1723 and 1762, in London and Paris, a small industry produced pamphlets telling the
public what happened inside a Masonic lodge. They were written by men who had been inside,
or claimed to have been, and they were sold cheaply to a public that wanted to know.

They are the most valuable documents in early Masonic history, and they are valuable for a
reason their authors did not intend. Because they were published, they are *dated*. And
because they cover four decades, they let us watch the ritual change.

This chapter is the payoff of that. It is short, and it is the evidentiary spine of Part
III.

---

### The sequence

**1723 — *A Mason's Examination*.** Printed in *The Flying Post*, a London newspaper, in
April. Brief. A catechism of questions and answers. Two degrees.[^1]

**1724 — *The Grand Mystery of Free-Masons Discover'd*.** Fuller. Still two degrees.

**1730 — *Masonry Dissected*, by Samuel Prichard.** The watershed. Three degrees, the full
catechism for each, and the Hiramic legend. Three editions in under a fortnight, and
reprinted for the rest of the century.

**1737–1745 — the French wave.** Several exposures appear in Paris, culminating in the
Abbé Pérau's *Le Secret des Francs-Maçons* (1742) and *L'Ordre des Francs-Maçons Trahi*
(1745). These are important because they show the ritual after its transplantation to
France and before the high degrees have taken hold.

**1760 — *Three Distinct Knocks*.** An English exposure of the "Antients" working.

**1762 — *Jachin and Boaz*.** The most commercially successful of all, reprinted for
decades, translated, and pirated.[^2]

Set them in a row and four things become visible that cannot be seen any other way.

---

### Finding one: the third degree is new

The 1723 and 1724 texts describe two degrees. The Scottish catechisms of 1696–1714 describe
two degrees. The 1730 text describes three, with a legend.

There is no earlier witness to the third degree, in any country, in any form.

This is negative evidence, and negative evidence needs care — but it is unusually strong
here, because of *who was writing*. These were commercial publications competing for a
scandal-hungry market. An author who knew of a dramatic ritual involving a murder and a
resurrection and did not print it would have been leaving money on the table. Prichard
printed it the moment it existed, and his sales prove the point.

So: the central ceremony of Freemasonry is a product of the 1720s. Everything built on the
assumption that it is ancient is built on sand.

---

### Finding two: the symbolism grows

Track individual elements across the sequence and the accretion is plain.

The 1723 catechism is spare: the lodge, its form, its supports, the signs, the words. By
1730 there is more furniture. By 1762 there is a great deal more — lesser lights, jewels,
ornaments, the moral interpretations of the tools, the ladder, and the elaborated
descriptions that would become the eighteenth-century lectures.

Nothing is subtracted. Things are added, decade by decade, and the additions are of a
consistent kind: more symbols, more interpretation, more explanation of what things mean.

This tells us something structural about how Masonic ritual develops. It does not evolve by
mutation of an original core. It **accumulates**, and it accumulates *commentary*. The
Craft's history is a history of people explaining it to each other, and the explanations
becoming part of it.

Which is, incidentally, exactly the mechanism by which nineteenth-century occult
interpretations would enter and become traditional. The system was built to absorb
commentary. It had been doing it since the 1720s.

---

### Finding three: what is not there, for forty years

Now the list that matters most for this book's argument.

Across every English and French exposure from 1723 to 1762, the following do not appear:

Kabbalah. The Sephirot. The Tree of Life. Gematria or any operation on Hebrew letters.
Planetary correspondences. Astrological hours. Angelic or spirit names used operatively.
Magic circles. Talismans. Alchemical terminology — no sulphur, mercury, salt, nigredo,
prima materia, philosophers' stone. Egyptian gods or Egyptian mysteries. Any reference to
the Knights Templar. Any reference to Rosicrucians.

None of it. In forty years of hostile and commercially motivated publication across two
countries.

I want to be exact about what this establishes and what it does not.

It establishes that **the Craft degrees, as worked in England and France between 1723 and
1762, contained no esoteric content of the kind later attributed to them.** That is as well
evidenced as anything in this book.

It does not establish that no individual Mason held esoteric views. Plenty did; Europe was
full of people with such views, and some of them were Masons, exactly as some were
clergymen and some were customs officials.

And it does not cover the high degrees, which are beginning to appear in France precisely
during the later part of this window, and which are a different subject entirely — Part IV.

But for the Craft itself, in its formative and its first expansive period, the negative
finding is firm. The furniture was a Bible, a square, a pair of compasses, some builder's
tools and a legend about a murdered architect.

---

### Finding four: publication did nothing

The last finding is the one this book began with in Chapter 3, and it can now be stated
with the evidence in hand.

Every secret was public from 1730. The most complete exposure was the most successful book.
The French translations put the material across Europe. *Jachin and Boaz* was still being
reprinted in the 1790s.

And in the same period, Freemasonry went from a few London lodges to an international
institution with tens of thousands of members, aristocratic and royal patronage, and a
presence on four continents.

Publication did not damage it. If anything, the exposures functioned as advertising: a man
who read *Jachin and Boaz* out of curiosity now knew what the ceremony was, and some
proportion of such men decided they would rather undergo it than read about it.

Which is the correct conclusion, and it took an industry of pamphleteers forty years to
demonstrate it: **you cannot expose an experience.**

---

### A caution about the sources

These are not neutral documents and should not be read as transcripts.

They were written to sell. Some authors padded. Some worked from memory and got things
wrong. Some included material from lodges that did other things, and some may have invented
where their memory failed. Prichard swore an affidavit of accuracy before a magistrate,
which tells us he anticipated the objection, not that he was right.

The method for using them is comparative. Where three independent exposures agree, the
element is probably real. Where one has something the others lack, it may be local
practice, or it may be invention. Where a French text differs from an English one, it may
reflect genuine divergence in practice between the countries — which by the 1740s was
substantial.

Handled that way, they are excellent evidence. Handled as scripture, they produce the
confident errors that fill the popular literature.

---

### What Part III has established

We can now state the position at the close of the Craft's formative period, entirely from
documents.

Freemasonry as an institution derives from a Scottish lodge system given permanent form by
royal statute in 1598–99, combined with an English manuscript tradition of craft legend
running back to about 1390, and reorganised in London in the early 1720s by a circle of
Newtonian and Whig gentlemen into a Grand Lodge with a printed constitution.

Its founding document declares a religious minimum so broad as to be almost empty, which
purchases unprecedented breadth of membership at the cost of doctrinal definition.

Its ritual is a dramatic technology of remarkable effectiveness, using darkness, ordeal,
oath and staged revelation, culminating in a legend of death and raising whose central
declaration is that the true word is lost.

Its symbolism is drawn, item by item and demonstrably, from the Bible, from ordinary
Christian iconography, and from the tools of the building trade.

And it contains, in forty years of published evidence, no Hermeticism, no Kabbalah, no
alchemy, no ceremonial magic, no Egypt, no Templars and no Rosicrucians.

That is the machine. It is now built, it is spreading across Europe at extraordinary speed,
and it has three properties that will determine everything that happens next: it is
*graded*, so it can be extended; it is *secret*, so extensions are hard to audit; and it is
*empty*, so the extensions can contain anything at all.

Within fifteen years of Prichard's pamphlet, Frenchmen will begin adding degrees. Within
thirty, a Bordeaux merchant will be performing angelic invocations inside a Masonic
structure. Within fifty, a Prussian order will require Masonic membership before teaching
alchemy.

None of that came out of the lodge.

All of it went in.
