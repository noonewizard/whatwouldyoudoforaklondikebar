# PART IV — THE GREAT EXPANSION

# CHAPTER TWENTY-FOUR
## The Eighteenth-Century Explosion

In 1730 a Freemason had three degrees available to him.

By 1780 a French or German Mason could, if he had the time, the money and the appetite,
work through Scottish degrees, Elect degrees, Templar degrees, Rose-Croix degrees,
Kadosh degrees, Egyptian degrees, Hermetic degrees, Rectified degrees, philosophical
degrees, chapters, councils, areopagi, consistories and tribunals, ascending through
numbered grades into the twenties, thirties and — in some systems — the nineties.

One estimate puts the number of distinct degrees invented in eighteenth-century France at
well over a thousand. Nobody has ever produced a complete list, and nobody ever will,
because a great many were worked once, in one place, by a dozen men, and left no trace but
a manuscript in a drawer.

This is the most important development in the history of Freemasonry after the
institution's own formation, and it is the least discussed outside specialist literature.
It is also the point at which the subject of this book actually begins — because it is in
these degrees, and not in the Craft, that Western esoteric material enters Masonic
structures.

---

### The mechanism

How does an institution go from three degrees to a thousand in fifty years?

The answer is embarrassingly simple, and it explains the whole phenomenon.

**Nobody owned the copyright.**

Freemasonry had no central authority outside its own jurisdictions, no doctrinal office, no
mechanism for saying *this is not Masonry*. The English Grand Lodge governed its own
lodges. It had no power in France, and by the 1740s it could not have exercised any if it
had. A group of men in Bordeaux or Lyon or Berlin who wished to constitute a new degree
needed nothing but each other's agreement.

And the Craft's own structure invited it. A three-stage system in which the third stage
declares that the true secret is *lost* is a system with an unresolved ending. It is an
invitation, written into the ritual, to ask what was lost and whether it can be found.

The high degrees are, almost without exception, answers to that question. The Royal Arch
recovers the lost word. The Elect degrees avenge the murder. The Templar degrees supply a
continuing order that preserved what was hidden. The Rose-Croix supplies a Christian
fulfillment. Each takes the hole at the center of the third degree and fills it.

---

### The market

That is the structural explanation. There is also a social one, and it is less edifying.

Eighteenth-century France was a society organized by rank and obsessed with it. Precedence,
title, the right to a particular seat or a particular form of address, the distinction
between the noblesse d'épée and the noblesse de robe and the men who were neither — this
was the texture of daily life for the class of people joining lodges.

A system that offers *advancement* to men who had nowhere else to advance is a system with
a market. A merchant who could never be a marquis could become a Knight of the East, a
Prince of Jerusalem, a Sublime Prince of the Royal Secret. He could wear a sash and a
jewel. He could outrank, within the lodge, men who outranked him outside it.

I do not say this cynically, and the mockery it attracted at the time was often
snobbish. But any honest account has to include it, because the degree titles themselves
make it plain. They are not the vocabulary of mysticism. They are the vocabulary of
chivalry and court: Knight, Prince, Sovereign, Grand Elect, Sublime.

There was also, straightforwardly, money. Degrees cost fees. A man who possessed a system
and the authority to confer it had a business. Some of the eighteenth century's more
enterprising degree-mongers traveled Europe selling patents, and at least one — the
Chevalier Ramsay's less scrupulous successors — did very well out of it.

---

### The mythologies

What the degrees actually *contained* varied enormously, and the variety is the point of
Part IV. But the mythological repertoire can be summarised, because it is surprisingly
small.

**The chivalric.** Masonry descends from crusading knights, and the Craft degrees are the
outer court of a knightly order. This is Ramsay's contribution and it is the largest single
source of eighteenth-century high-degree material.

**The Templar.** A specific and more aggressive version of the chivalric: Masonry *is* the
Knights Templar, surviving in concealment since 1314, preserving a secret and awaiting
restoration. This is von Hund's, and it comes with a claim of living authority.

**The vengeance narratives.** The Elect degrees pursue and punish the murderers of Hiram.
These are among the earliest high degrees and their appeal is dramatic rather than
doctrinal.

**The Christian-mystical.** The Rose-Croix degrees reinterpret the Masonic legend in
Christian terms: the lost word is the Word, the Temple is the Church, the raising is the
Resurrection. This is theologically serious and it pulled Masonry back toward a
confessional identity that Anderson had carefully removed.

**The Hermetic and alchemical.** Degrees containing genuine esoteric instruction —
alchemical, Kabbalistic, theurgic. These are the minority, but they are the reason this
book exists.

**The Egyptian.** Cagliostro's rite and its relatives. Late, theatrical, and entirely
eighteenth-century in its Egypt.

---

### What this does to the argument

Here is why Part IV is the hinge of this book.

In Part III we established that the Craft contained no esoteric material between 1723 and
1762, on the evidence of four decades of published exposures. That finding holds, and it is
firm.

But it would be dishonest to stop there and declare that Freemasonry has nothing to do with
Western esotericism, because from the 1740s onward there are Masonic bodies that
unambiguously do. The Élus Coëns performed theurgic operations. The Gold- und Rosenkreuz
taught alchemy. Willermoz's Rectified Rite carried a complete Christian-theosophical
doctrine of the fall and reintegration of souls.

So both statements are true, and the apparent contradiction dissolves once you separate the
Craft from what was built on top of it:

**The Craft supplied a form and no content. The high degrees supplied content — and the
content came from outside.**

That is the carriage thesis in its cleanest form, and the eighteenth century is where it
can be demonstrated, because — and this is the crucial point — **we can usually name the
people who did it.**

This is not a case of tracing influence through the mists. Ramsay gave a speech; we have
the text and the date. Von Hund made a claim; we know what he could not produce. Martinez
de Pasqually founded an order in 1761; we have its documents and its doctrine. The Gold-
und Rosenkreuz reorganised itself in 1777; McIntosh has been through its papers.

These are not survivals from antiquity. They are compositions, by named authors, in known
years, for reasons we can often reconstruct.

---

### A word about quality

One more thing before we start naming names.

There is a tendency, in both Masonic and skeptical writing, to treat the high degrees as an
eighteenth-century absurdity — a proliferation of costumes and titles that sensible people
eventually pruned back. The English response was essentially that: the union of 1813
declared that pure Ancient Masonry consists of three degrees and no more, including the
Royal Arch, and English Freemasonry has been comparatively austere ever since.

That judgment is understandable and it is also a loss. Some of the high-degree material is
junk — repetitive, derivative, invented on Tuesday to be worked on Thursday. Some of it is
not. The Rectified Scottish Rite's doctrine is a coherent and serious piece of Christian
esoteric theology. The Rose-Croix ceremony is, by common consent even among people who
dislike its theology, one of the most powerful rituals ever composed. The Royal Arch is
strange and deep and nobody knows where it came from.

Dismissing the whole phenomenon as costume drama makes the history unintelligible, because
it cannot explain why serious people — including men of real learning and real piety — gave
decades of their lives to it.

They gave those decades because the third degree had told them something was lost, and
because they believed, with varying degrees of evidence and none of it good, that they knew
where to look.

The man who first told them where to look gave a speech in Paris in the winter of 1736, and
he was a Scottish Jacobite tutor with a Catholic conversion, a doctorate from Oxford, and a
gift for saying the thing that a room wanted to hear.
