# CHAPTER TWENTY-FIVE
## Ramsay's Oration

Andrew Michael Ramsay was a Scot from Ayr who had gone to the continent, converted to
Catholicism under the direction of Fénelon, tutored the sons of the exiled Stuart claimant
at Rome, received a doctorate at Oxford, been elected a Fellow of the Royal Society, and
acquired the style of Chevalier. He was a man who had made himself, and who had made
himself out of borrowed materials, and he was good at it.

In 1736 he composed a discourse to be delivered at a Masonic gathering in Paris. Whether he
ever delivered it, and in what form, is disputed; what is not disputed is that it
circulated, in manuscript and then in print, and that it did more to shape continental
Freemasonry than any document except Anderson's *Constitutions*.[^1]

It is about six pages long. It changed everything.

---

### What he said

The oration is a piece of institutional flattery of a very high order, and its argument
proceeds in stages.

Freemasonry, Ramsay says, is not what the vulgar suppose. It is an order dedicated to
virtue, to learning, to the union of nations and the softening of manners. Its members are
bound by a fraternity that transcends country and creed. Its ambition is a universal
republic of the mind — and here Ramsay gestures at a vast project of collaborative
learning, a universal dictionary of the liberal arts, to which the fraternity across Europe
might contribute.

Then the history.

Our ancestors, he says, were the crusaders. In the Holy Land, princes and nobles associated
themselves in a fraternity, took vows, adopted ancient signs and symbols from the
mysteries, and undertook to restore the Temple. They joined themselves with the Knights of
St John of Jerusalem. From that association the order descends, and it was carried into
Europe, and particularly into Scotland, where it was preserved.

And — the sentence that mattered most in that room — the order flourished in Scotland
because a Scottish king, James Lord Steward, was Grand Master, and from Scotland it spread
to France and elsewhere.[^2]

---

### What he was doing

Read that with the six questions in hand and it collapses instantly. There is no evidence
for any of it. Not a document, not a chronicle, not a route. The crusading fraternity Ramsay
describes is unattested; the Scottish preservation is unattested; the Grand Mastership is
unattested.

But asking whether Ramsay's history is true is the less interesting question. The
interesting question is what the speech was *for*, and the answer is visible in its
audience.

Ramsay was addressing French Masons in 1736. Freemasonry had arrived in France within the
previous decade, largely through British — and particularly Jacobite — channels. It was
under suspicion. It had been condemned in some quarters as a foreign import, a Protestant
import, and a political one. In 1737 the French authorities would move against it; in 1738
the Pope would condemn it outright.

A French nobleman considering membership in 1736 faced two objections: that this was an
English trade club unworthy of a gentleman, and that it was religiously suspect.

Ramsay answers both, in a single move. The order is not a trade club — it is *chivalric*.
Its ancestors were not stonecutters but crusading knights, which is to say, the ancestors of
the men in the room. And it is not Protestant — it is crusading Catholic in origin,
associated with the Knights of St John, dedicated to the recovery of the Holy Land.

He also, and not incidentally, made it Scottish. Which was congenial both to his own
identity and to the Jacobite networks in which he had spent his life.

Every element of the history is calibrated to its audience. That is what tells us it was
composed rather than received.

---

### Was he lying?

I do not think the question has a clean answer, and I want to resist making him a villain.

Ramsay was operating inside the intellectual culture described in Chapter 6: the *prisca
theologia*, in which traditions are presumed to descend from a common ancient source, and
in which the job of a learned man is to trace the descent. Within that culture, an argument
from resemblance and plausibility is a legitimate form of scholarship, not a fabrication.
The Old Charges had been doing the same thing since 1390 — tracing masonry to Euclid and
Solomon on no evidence at all — and nobody thought them fraudulent.

Ramsay was also doing something the eighteenth century did constantly: giving an
institution the genealogy it needed in order to be respectable. Hobsbawm and Ranger's
invented traditions are full of exactly this.

What can be said with confidence is that he had no evidence, that the chronology does not
work, that no contemporary produced any corroboration, and that the story was new in 1736.

---

### The consequences

The oration's effects are out of all proportion to its length, and they run in three
directions.

**It made the high degrees possible.** If Masonry descends from crusading knights, then the
three Craft degrees are plainly incomplete — where is the knighthood? Within a few years of
the oration, French Masonry was producing degrees to fill the gap it had identified.
Chivalric grades, Scottish grades, degrees of knighthood, Kadosh degrees, Templar degrees.
Ramsay did not create any of them. He created the space they filled.

**It made Masonry Scottish.** The term *Écossais* — Scottish — became, in French usage, the
label for high-degree Masonry in general, and it has almost nothing to do with Scotland.
The Scottish Rite, which would become the largest appendant body in the world and which
would produce Albert Pike, carries its name because of a paragraph in a speech by a man
from Ayr who was inventing a genealogy for a French audience.

**And it opened the door to antiquity.** Ramsay's crusaders had, he said, adopted ancient
signs and symbols from the mysteries. That clause — offered in passing, almost as
ornament — authorised two centuries of speculation. If the crusaders took it from the
mysteries, then Masonry contains the mysteries, and the search for what the mysteries
contained becomes a Masonic activity.

Blavatsky's Masonry, Pike's Masonry, the Golden Dawn's Masonry and the conspiracy
literature's Masonry are all, ultimately, downstream of that clause.

---

### The pattern to notice

I want to draw out the structure here, because it recurs so exactly that it is worth
naming.

**An institution has a gap.** Freemasonry in France had prestige problems.

**Someone supplies a history that fills it.** Ramsay, 1736.

**The history is plausible, congenial and unevidenced.**

**Nobody checks, because nobody can and nobody wants to.**

**Within a generation the history is the tradition**, and people begin building practices
on it.

**Within a century, the practices are cited as evidence for the history.** The existence of
Templar degrees becomes an argument that Masonry is Templar in origin — which is precisely
backwards, since the degrees were created because of the claim.

That last step is the one to watch for, because it appears everywhere in this subject. It
is circular sourcing in institutional form: a claim generates a practice, and the practice
is then offered as evidence for the claim.

The Rite of Strict Observance is the purest example ever produced, and it ran for nearly
thirty years before its own members put it to a vote.
