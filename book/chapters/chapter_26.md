# CHAPTER TWENTY-SIX
## The Manufacture of Antiquity

On 18 March 1314, on an island in the Seine, Jacques de Molay, the last Grand Master of the
Knights Templar, was burned alive. The order had been arrested in France seven years
earlier, tried on charges of heresy, blasphemy, idolatry and obscenity extracted largely
under torture, and suppressed by papal bull in 1312. Its properties were transferred, its
members dispersed, imprisoned or absorbed, and its name became, for four centuries, a
byword for a spectacular institutional death.

Four hundred and forty years later, a Saxon baron began telling German Freemasons that
their order was the Templars, that the succession had never been broken, and that he had
his authority from superiors whose names he was not permitted to reveal.

The story of the Rite of Strict Observance is the best documented case in this book of a
tradition being manufactured, defended, and finally dismantled by its own adherents. It
deserves a full chapter, because everything that is usually asserted about hidden
transmission can be watched happening here in the open.

---

### What the Templars actually were

Briefly, because the popular literature has made it necessary.

The Poor Fellow-Soldiers of Christ and of the Temple of Solomon were founded around 1119 to
protect pilgrims in the crusader states. They took their name from their quarters on the
Temple Mount in Jerusalem — the al-Aqsa compound, which the crusaders identified with
Solomon's Temple. They became a military order, then an enormously wealthy one, operating
what amounted to an international banking network.

They were not architects. They were not custodians of esoteric knowledge. They did not
excavate anything, so far as any medieval source records. Their charges of heresy were
extracted under torture by a French king who owed them money, and even the Church's own
inquiry — the Chinon parchment, rediscovered and published in the twenty-first century —
indicates that Clement V absolved the leadership of heresy while suppressing the order for
reasons of political expediency.[^1]

Their suppression was total and well documented. There is no medieval evidence of a
surviving Templar organization.

Between 1314 and the 1730s, nothing in Freemasonry refers to them. The Old Charges do not
mention them. The Scottish records do not mention them. The catechisms do not mention them.
The English exposures of 1723 to 1762 do not mention them.

The Templar entry into Masonry happens in the eighteenth century, and it happens after
Ramsay's chivalric opening of 1736.

---

### Von Hund

Karl Gotthelf, Baron von Hund und Altengrotkau, was a Saxon nobleman, a Catholic in a
Protestant region, a man of substantial estates and, by every account including hostile
ones, sincere.

His account of his own initiation ran as follows. In Paris in the early 1740s he was
received into a higher Masonic order, in the presence of persons of great rank — he named,
at various times, Lord Kilmarnock and others associated with the Jacobite cause — and was
given to understand that this order was the continuation of the Knights Templar, preserved
in secret since the suppression. He was invested with authority over a German province. He
was told that Unknown Superiors directed the order, and that he would be contacted.

He was never contacted.

For the rest of his life — he died in 1776 — von Hund waited. He built the Rite of Strict
Observance around the claim: a system of degrees in which every member took a knightly
name, was assigned to a province of the old Templar geography, and swore, as the rite's
name declares, *strict observance* to superiors he could not identify.

And the rite spread extraordinarily. Through the 1760s and 1770s it became the dominant
form of high-degree Masonry in the German lands and beyond, attracting princes, dukes and,
eventually, most of the continent's serious Masonic energy.

---

### The problem

Von Hund's problem was structural and it could not be solved.

The whole rite rested on an authority he could not produce. He had no charter. He could name
no living superior. The men who had allegedly received him in Paris were dead or
unidentifiable, and when pressed he could not give a coherent account. He appears to have
believed his own story — his behavior over thirty years is very hard to explain otherwise,
and he spent his own fortune on the rite — but belief is not evidence, and by the 1770s his
followers were asking increasingly hard questions.

Other claimants appeared, as they always do in such situations, offering rival authorities.
A man named Johnson claimed to be an emissary of the Unknown Superiors and was exposed as
an impostor. Rival systems multiplied. Alchemists arrived offering the secret the order was
supposed to possess. The whole German Masonic world descended into a confusion of competing
warrants.

---

### Wilhelmsbad

So in 1782 they held a convention to settle it.

The Convent of Wilhelmsbad met near Hanau, over several weeks, and it is one of the most
extraordinary meetings in the history of Western esotericism: an assembly of European
Masonic leaders, gathered to determine, by inquiry and vote, whether their order descended
from the Knights Templar.

They had before them the Strict Observance's claims, the absence of documents, the failure
of the Unknown Superiors ever to appear, and thirty years of accumulated doubt. They also
had a rival proposal from Lyon, of which more in the next chapter.

The convention's conclusion was that the Templar descent could not be sustained. The
Strict Observance was effectively wound up; the Templar claim was set aside; and the
convention gave its endorsement to a reformed system in which knighthood was retained as a
*symbolic* rather than a historical inheritance — the Chevaliers Bienfaisants de la Cité
Sainte, the Beneficent Knights of the Holy City, within Jean-Baptiste Willermoz's Rectified
Scottish Rite.[^2]

Let me state what happened there as plainly as I can.

**A body of eighteenth-century European aristocrats, who had every reason to want an
ancient chivalric pedigree, who had built an international organization on one, and who had
staked their honor on it, examined the evidence and concluded that it was not there.**

Then they said so, and reorganised accordingly.

That is a remarkable act of intellectual honesty, and it almost never gets mentioned. The
popular literature on Masonic Templarism is enormous, and virtually none of it tells the
reader that the claim's own believers voted it down in 1782.

---

### Why the claim survived anyway

And yet it did survive. It is arguably stronger now than it was then.

Templar degrees persisted in Masonic systems and persist today — the Knights Templar of the
York Rite in America and the Great Priory in England, both perfectly respectable bodies
which are entirely clear that their Templar reference is commemorative rather than
genealogical. The claim of literal descent migrated out of Masonry into the wider culture,
where it has flourished: the Templars in Scotland, the Templars at Rosslyn, the Templars
and the Grail, the Templars and the bloodline, the Templars and everything else.

Why does a claim survive its own refutation?

Chapter 6 gave part of the answer: a tradition can outlive the destruction of its
credentials if enough people need it. But there is a second mechanism visible here, and it
is worth naming.

**Once a claim generates institutions, the institutions become evidence for the claim.**

By 1782 there were Templar Masonic bodies all over Europe. They had members, regalia,
ceremonies, archives and continuity. A person encountering them in 1850, or in 1950, would
reasonably ask why Masonic Templar orders exist — and the available answer, the one that
requires no research, is that they exist because Masonry is Templar in origin.

The actual answer is the reverse. They exist because a Saxon baron said so in the 1750s,
and enough people believed him for long enough to build things.

This is the circularity identified at the end of the last chapter, operating at full scale
and across centuries. It is the single most common mechanism of false historical belief in
this entire subject, and once you have seen it here you will recognize it in the
Rosicrucian orders, in the Golden Dawn's German warrant, and in a dozen twentieth-century
organizations claiming Egyptian, Essene or Atlantean descent.

### The Rose-Croix, briefly

One other family of degrees belongs in this chapter, and it needs a different verdict.

The Rose-Croix degrees — which appear in France from the 1750s and become the eighteenth
degree of the Scottish Rite — take the Masonic legend and read it Christologically. The
lost word is the Word made flesh. The Temple is rebuilt in the Resurrection. The ceremony
is constructed around the passion and the restoration, and in its developed form it is, by
wide agreement, among the most affecting rituals ever written.

Does it derive from Rosicrucianism?

The name says so. The evidence does not. Chapter 12 established that there was no
Rosicrucian organization in the seventeenth century to descend from, and the Rose-Croix
degrees show no textual dependence on the manifestos. What they show is a *Christian
recovery*: an attempt by French Catholic Masons to take back the confessional content that
Anderson's formula had removed, using a name that carried mystical prestige.

That is not fraud and it is not descent. It is renaming, and it is extremely common.

**Verdict on this chapter's two claims: the Templar descent is unsupported, was manufactured
in the 1750s, and was repudiated by its own adherents in 1782. The Rose-Croix is a genuine
eighteenth-century Christian-mystical development wearing a seventeenth-century name it has
no title to.**

Which leaves the case that is not like either of them. Because while von Hund was waiting
for superiors who never came, a customs official in Bordeaux with an obscure background and
a claimed Iberian descent had founded an order that was doing something no Masonic body had
done before.

It was not claiming ancient authority. It was performing operations.
