# CHAPTER TWENTY-SEVEN
## Theurgy in the Lodge

This is the chapter the book has been building toward since Chapter 1, and its finding can
be stated in a sentence before the evidence.

**The first unambiguous, documented entry of operative magical practice into a Masonic
structure occurs in 1761, in France, and we know who did it.**

Not in Egypt. Not at Solomon's Temple. Not among the Templars. Not in a Scottish lodge in
1598, or a Warrington tavern in 1646, or the Goose and Gridiron in 1717. In 1761, at
Bordeaux, by a man named Martinez de Pasqually, about whom almost nothing is certainly
known.

---

### The founder

Martinez de Pasqually — the spelling varies; so does nearly everything else about him —
appears in the French Masonic record in the 1750s carrying a patent of doubtful provenance
which he claimed had been granted to his father by Charles Edward Stuart. He was probably
born around 1727, possibly in Grenoble; his family background may have been Iberian and may
have been Jewish or of converso descent, which has been argued both ways and settled by
nobody. His French was imperfect. His writing is difficult.

In 1761 he founded, or completed the founding of, an order with an extravagant name: the
*Ordre des Chevaliers Maçons Élus Coëns de l'Univers* — the Order of Knight-Masons, Elect
Priests of the Universe. *Coën* is a rendering of the Hebrew *kohen*, priest.[^1]

Its structure was Masonic. It had degrees; it recruited from lodges; it used Masonic forms
and language. Its content was not Masonic at all.

---

### The doctrine

Martinez's teaching survives principally in a long, unfinished and famously obscure work,
the *Traité de la réintégration des êtres*, and in his correspondence with his students.
The system runs roughly as follows.

Before the material world, spiritual beings emanated from God. Some fell, through pride,
and their fall necessitated the creation of matter as a prison and a remedy. Adam was
created as a being of glory, charged with the reintegration of the fallen; he fell in turn,
and the material body is the consequence. Human history since is the process of
*réintégration* — the return of emanated beings to their source.

And the process can be assisted. Not merely by piety, but by *operation*.

The Élus Coëns performed ceremonial workings, at prescribed times determined
astrologically, in prepared spaces marked with figures and characters, with invocations of
divine and angelic names, aimed at obtaining what the order called *la Chose* — the Thing —
a visible sign of spiritual contact, often described as a luminous appearance or a *passe*.

This is theurgy, in precisely the Iamblichan sense set out in Chapter 7: ritual operation
directed at contact with intelligences above the human, understood by its practitioners as
religious rather than magical, and undertaken for the purpose of the operator's ascent.

Martinez's students recorded whether operations succeeded. Some did, in their judgment;
many did not. The correspondence about this is one of the most curious bodies of material
in eighteenth-century religious history: serious, literate men, writing to each other about
whether a working had produced its sign, in the tones of colleagues comparing experimental
results.

---

### Why this matters so much

Because it is the exception that establishes the rule, and because it does so with dates.

Everything in Part II showed esoteric material available in print and *not* entering the
lodge. Everything in Part III showed forty years of published Masonic ritual containing
nothing esoteric. Chapter 26 showed a claim of ancient descent manufactured and then
repudiated.

Here, at last, is the real thing: genuine operative magic, inside a genuinely Masonic
organizational structure, with doctrine, ritual texts, practitioners and results.

And its date is 1761.

Which means that when someone asserts that Freemasonry has always contained a magical
tradition, the correct response is not denial. It is: *yes, from 1761, in one French order,
founded by a man we can name, whose doctrine is his own and whose sources are traceable to
Christian theosophy, Kabbalah and the Renaissance magical tradition — and which mainstream
Freemasonry neither authorised nor adopted.*

That is a far more interesting answer than either yes or no.

---

### Saint-Martin

Martinez died in 1774, in Saint-Domingue, where he had gone to settle an inheritance,
leaving his order without a head and his treatise unfinished. Two of his students carried
the work forward in opposite directions, and the divergence is instructive.

Louis-Claude de Saint-Martin had been Martinez's secretary. He performed the operations
and, over time, came to believe that they were unnecessary — that the external ceremonial
was a scaffolding, and that the real work was interior. He turned away from theurgy toward
what he called the *voie intérieure*, the inward way, and toward Jakob Böhme, whom he
learned German in order to read and then translated into French.

He published under the name *le Philosophe Inconnu*, the Unknown Philosopher, and his
writing — mystical, Christian, anti-clerical, politically ambiguous — was widely read. The
current called Martinism descends from him, and it has been reconstituted more than once,
most influentially by Papus at the end of the nineteenth century, which is where it
re-enters this book.

Saint-Martin's trajectory is worth marking because it is a permanent option in this
history: the practitioner who goes through the ceremonial and comes out the other side
believing that the ceremonial was the least of it. A. E. Waite would make the same journey
a hundred and thirty years later, in a different order, and arrive at a similar place.

---

### Willermoz

The other student went the opposite way, and had the greater institutional effect.

Jean-Baptiste Willermoz was a Lyon silk merchant, an organizer of formidable ability, and a
man who spent his long life inside Masonic structures. He had been a Coën. He had also
joined the Strict Observance. And he concluded — correctly — that von Hund's rite had a
magnificent chivalric form and no doctrine worth the name.

So he supplied one.

Willermoz took the Strict Observance's structure, removed the Templar-descent claim that
was by then indefensible, and filled the resulting system with a version of Martinez's
doctrine of the fall and reintegration, refined and made more orthodox, and inflected by
Saint-Martin's interiority. The result, worked out at the Convent of Lyon in 1778 and
carried at Wilhelmsbad in 1782, is the *Régime Écossais Rectifié* — the Rectified Scottish
Rite — whose members are the Beneficent Knights of the Holy City.[^2]

It survives. It is worked today in France, Switzerland and elsewhere. It is, so far as I
can judge, the most theologically coherent system ever built inside a Masonic frame.

And look at what its history demonstrates.

At Wilhelmsbad, the European Masonic leadership rejected a false claim of ancient descent
and adopted in its place a system whose doctrine had been composed *within the previous
twenty years* by two Frenchmen and a Lyon merchant.

They were not fooled, and they did not pretend. They chose content over pedigree.

That is the single most encouraging episode in this book, and it is worth setting against
everything in Part VI, where the opposite choice is made repeatedly.

---

### What the case establishes

Four things, and I want them explicit.

**One: magic entered Freemasonry from outside, late, and by an identifiable route.** Not
carried down within it. Poured in, by named men, in the 1760s and 1770s.

**Two: the Masonic form was the container, not the source.** Martinez used Masonic degree
structure because it was the available technology for organizing graded initiatory
instruction in eighteenth-century France. His doctrine owes nothing to Masonic ritual, and
Masonic ritual acquired nothing from him.

**Three: this was a minority phenomenon.** The Élus Coëns were never large. The Rectified
Rite has never been large. The overwhelming majority of eighteenth-century Freemasons
worked three degrees, dined, gave to charity and went home. Any account that presents
theurgic Masonry as typical is wrong by several orders of magnitude.

**Four: mainstream Freemasonry did not adopt it and in most jurisdictions did not know
about it.** An English Mason of 1780 had no idea that anything of the kind existed.

Those four statements are, together, the carriage thesis stated for the eighteenth century.
The Craft supplied a vessel. Someone else supplied the cargo. The vessel was not changed by
what it carried, and the cargo did not originate on board.

The same thing was happening simultaneously in Prussia, on a larger scale, with a different
cargo — and there the order in question made the relationship explicit, by requiring that
every candidate be a Freemason before he could be taught anything at all.
