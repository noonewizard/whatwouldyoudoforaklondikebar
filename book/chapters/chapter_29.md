# CHAPTER TWENTY-NINE
## Women at the Door

In 1744 — fourteen years after Prichard printed the Hiramic legend, seventeen years before
Martinez founded the Élus Coëns — women were being initiated in France into ceremonies
built on Masonic ritual.

They went on being initiated for the next two hundred and seventy years. The rite they
worked has its own texts, its own degrees, its own officers and its own symbolism, and it
was practised across France and beyond, in lodges attached to men's lodges, attended by
duchesses and by the wives and daughters of the bourgeoisie, with published rituals and
documented membership.

It is called the Rite of Adoption, and until very recently it was almost entirely absent
from the history of Freemasonry.

The scholar who recovered it is Jan Snoek, whose study reconstructs the rite's development
from some hundred and thirty manuscripts and printed sources, and whose work makes it no
longer possible to write about eighteenth-century Freemasonry as an exclusively male
phenomenon.[^1]

---

### The exclusion and its basis

Anderson's *Constitutions* of 1723 states that the persons admitted members of a lodge must
be good and true men, free-born, of mature and discreet age, no bondmen, no women, no
immoral or scandalous men.[^2]

That is the rule, and it has governed mainstream Freemasonry ever since. The usual
justification offered is the operative inheritance: the craft of masonry was a male trade,
and the speculative body preserved its qualifications.

The justification does not survive much pressure. By 1723 the qualifications had already
been detached from the trade in every other respect — the members were not masons, the
lodges were not building sites, and "free-born" was retained while "able to cut stone" was
not. The rule excluding women was a social rule, retained because eighteenth-century English
associational life was male, and dressed in a craft rationale.

France, notably, did not accept the position. French Masonic sociability was built around
salons and mixed company in a way the London tavern lodge was not, and within twenty years
of the *Constitutions* the French had invented a solution.

---

### What Adoption was

The Rite of Adoption was worked by a lodge of women *adopted* by, and meeting under the
authority of, a men's lodge. Its ceremonies were conducted by officers of both sexes: the
men's lodge supplied a presiding officer, the women's their own.

Its degrees were its own. The usual four are Apprentice, Companion, Mistress and Perfect
Mistress, with further degrees in some systems.

And its symbolism was *not* a copy of the Craft's. This is Snoek's most striking finding
and the one that most changes the picture. The Adoption rituals draw heavily on the Garden
of Eden and its imagery — the tree, the apple, the serpent, the ladder — and on the Tower of
Babel, and on the Ark. Where the men's Craft is built on Solomon's Temple and the death of
a builder, the women's rite is built on Genesis and on a narrative of fall and restoration.

That is a genuinely different symbolic system, developed in parallel, with its own logic.
It is not a watered-down men's ritual, and describing it as one — which is what the older
literature did when it noticed the rite at all — is simply a failure to read the texts.

The rite attracted serious participation. Its patronesses included women at the summit of
French society; the Duchess of Bourbon presided over a lodge in Paris in the 1770s. It
survived the Revolution, was revived under the Empire, and continued through the nineteenth
century.

---

### Why it was forgotten

Three reasons, and they are worth naming because the mechanism is general.

**It was not "regular."** Adoption lodges were not Craft lodges, and as the nineteenth
century hardened the definitions of regularity, they fell outside them. Bodies outside the
definition do not appear in the histories written by bodies inside it.

**Its records were less well kept, and less well preserved.** Institutional archives
preserve what institutions value.

**And the men who wrote Masonic history were not looking for it.** This is the largest
factor and the least excusable. A century of Masonic historiography treated the Craft as
the subject and everything else as an aberration — and when the aberration involved women,
it was recorded, if at all, as a curiosity.

Snoek's recovery of the rite is a straightforward demonstration of what happens when
someone looks: a hundred and thirty sources, a coherent ritual tradition, two and a half
centuries of practice, hiding in plain sight in archives that had always been open.

---

### What it proves

For this book's argument, the Adoption rite does something no other case does.

Every previous chapter in Part IV has shown Masonic *form* being filled with content from
outside — chivalric, Templar, theurgic, alchemical. Each time the content came from the
Western esoteric tradition, and each time a reader might reasonably suspect that the
affinity was more than structural: that something in Masonic ritual invited esoteric
filling.

The Adoption rite removes that suspicion, because here the same form is filled with content
that is not esoteric at all. The material is Genesis. The purpose is moral and social. The
result is a functioning initiatory system for women in a society that offered them almost
no institutional life of their own.

The form took it without difficulty. It would have taken anything.

That is the strongest possible statement of the carriage thesis, and it comes from the
part of Masonic history that the esoteric literature has never bothered with.

---

### The nineteenth century and after

The story does not end in 1744, and its later stages matter for Part VI.

Adoption Masonry continued in France through the nineteenth century. In 1882 Maria
Deraismes, a writer and campaigner for women's rights, was initiated in a French lodge —
irregularly, from the standpoint of the obediences — and in 1893, with Georges Martin, she
founded *Le Droit Humain*, an order admitting men and women on equal terms in the same
lodges. That is Co-Masonry, and it spread internationally.

In England it arrived in 1902, and its principal promoter was Annie Besant — by then the
president-in-waiting of the Theosophical Society — who made Co-Masonry a vehicle for
theosophical ideas and carried it across the British Empire and to America.

Which produces a fact worth stating plainly, because it cuts against the usual account:
**the most successful transmission of Masonic ritual into the twentieth-century occult
world ran through women's and mixed Masonry, not through the regular male Craft.**

Besant's Co-Masonry, not the United Grand Lodge of England, is the route by which Masonic
initiation reached the Theosophical movement. And Aleister Crowley's earliest Masonic
initiation of any kind was, on his own account, in an irregular or co-Masonic context.

The bodies excluded from Masonic history as irregular turn out to be the ones that did the
most to carry Masonic ritual into the world beyond the Craft.

---

### A note on the present

Women's Freemasonry exists now and is substantial: in England the Order of Women
Freemasons and the Honourable Fraternity of Ancient Freemasons, in France several
obediences, in America a range of bodies including the Eastern Star, which is a different
kind of organisation again. The United Grand Lodge of England has since 2018 recognised
these bodies as regular in their practice while not in amity with them, and has issued
guidance on gender reassignment that would have been unimaginable a generation ago.

None of that is this book's subject. I mention it because the eighteenth-century material
is often presented as though the question were settled and historical, and it is neither.

What the historical record shows is narrower and more useful: that the exclusion of women
from Freemasonry was a social decision of 1723, not a structural property of Masonic
ritual; that the ritual worked perfectly well for women from 1744; and that a great deal of
what happened next in the esoteric world happened because of bodies that mainstream
Freemasonry had declined to recognise.

Part IV is complete. The eighteenth century took a three-degree English moral ceremony and
turned it into the universal structure for initiatory association in the Western world —
capable of holding chivalry, theurgy, alchemy, Christian mysticism, feminism and, before
long, a great deal else.

It also carried it across an ocean, into a society that would use it for purposes nobody in
London had anticipated, and where a free Black man in Boston would obtain a warrant from
the Grand Lodge of England and begin a tradition that is still arguing with its parent
institution two hundred and forty years later.
