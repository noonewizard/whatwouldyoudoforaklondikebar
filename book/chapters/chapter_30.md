# PART V — THE ATLANTIC CRAFT

# CHAPTER THIRTY
## Colonial Brethren

In 1734 a Philadelphia printer reissued a London book. He had no particular reason to
expect it to sell — it was a set of regulations for a fraternal society, with a long
antiquarian history and some drinking songs at the back — but he was a member of the
society, he had recently become master of his lodge, and he was a man who understood that
printing a thing makes it real.

Benjamin Franklin's edition of Anderson's *Constitutions* was the first Masonic book
printed in America.[^1]

It is a small event with a large meaning. Freemasonry did not arrive in the colonies as an
exotic import to be marvelled at. It arrived as a going concern, with a printed rulebook, a
recognised procedure, and a local printer willing to underwrite it — and within forty years
it would be one of the few institutions in British North America that existed
simultaneously in every colony, with a common language and mutual recognition across a
thousand miles.

---

### The arrival

The dates are reasonably clear. Provincial Grand Masters were appointed for the American
colonies from 1730 onward. Lodges are documented in Philadelphia and Boston in the early
1730s — St John's Lodge in Boston from 1733 under a deputation from England — and in the
following decades across the seaboard, in Virginia, the Carolinas, New York, Rhode Island
and the rest.[^2]

Two features of the colonial situation shaped what Freemasonry became there, and both
matter for the rest of this book.

**The first is scarcity of institutions.** A colonial town of two thousand people had a
church, perhaps a court, and very little else. There were no universities to speak of, few
clubs, no professional associations in the modern sense. A body that met regularly, kept
accounts, elected officers, dined, and admitted men by ballot was supplying something for
which there was no competition.

**The second is distance.** A merchant travelling from Charleston to Boston, or from
Philadelphia to London, arrived among strangers and needed a way to establish that he was
respectable, solvent and worth extending credit to. Masonry supplied exactly that: a
recognition system, a claim on hospitality, and a network of men who had sworn to assist
each other.

Steven Bullock's study of the American Craft in this period documents the consequences in
detail, and the picture that emerges is not primarily one of philosophy or symbolism. It is
one of credit, sociability, status and connection.[^3]

---

### Who joined, and why it mattered

In the colonial period, American Freemasonry was an elite institution. Its members were
merchants, officials, planters, professional men, officers — the colonial gentry and the
people who aspired to join them. Membership was a marker of standing and was understood as
one.

That is worth stating because it cuts against two later mythologies at once. It is not the
democratic brotherhood of Revolutionary legend; that comes later. And it is not a
conspiratorial cabal; a body whose membership is a public marker of social standing, whose
processions are attended by the whole town, and whose constitutions are printed by the
local newspaper man is not operating in the dark.

What it was, precisely, was **a technology for making strangers into associates**, in a
society composed largely of strangers. That function — social rather than mystical — is the
principal reason for its colonial success.

---

### The furniture of colonial Masonry

What did they actually do? Largely what English lodges did, with local variation.

They met in taverns. They worked three degrees. They dined, at length and with a great deal
to drink — the Masonic songs at the end of Franklin's *Constitutions* were not decorative.
They processed publicly on St John's Day, in aprons, to a church service, and the sermon
was frequently printed. They buried their members with Masonic honours. They laid corner
stones.

That last practice deserves a note, because it became characteristically American. A Masonic
corner-stone ceremony is a public ritual in which the fraternity's private symbolism —
square, level, plumb, corn, wine and oil — is performed in the open, at a civic occasion, to
consecrate a public building. It is the single most visible thing American Freemasonry has
ever done, and it establishes something important about its self-understanding: this was an
organisation that understood itself as *civic*, with a public role, using its ceremonial to
bless the construction of a shared society.

A body engaged in a secret conspiracy does not do its rituals on the courthouse steps with
the governor watching.

---

### The absence

And now the question this book has to ask of every Masonic environment: what esoteric
content was present?

The answer for colonial America is: essentially none, and the evidence is good.

The ritual worked was the English Craft ritual of the period, which Part III established
contained no Hermetic, Kabbalistic, alchemical or magical material. The high degrees, which
did contain such material, arrive in America late — the Scottish Rite's Supreme Council is
not founded at Charleston until 1801, and the systems that fed it were mostly imported in
the later eighteenth century through the West Indies and French connections.

The colonial Masonic sermons that survive — and a good number were printed — are ordinary
Protestant moral discourses. They talk about brotherly love, charity, order, the duties of
society and the virtues of the Craft. They do not talk about Egypt, or ancient mysteries, or
hidden wisdom.[^4]

The American colonial Mason was, in the overwhelming majority of cases, a Protestant
gentleman who had joined a dining club with an impressive ceremony and useful connections.

This matters enormously for Chapter 50, where we deal with the claim that the United States
was founded as a Masonic project on occult principles. Whatever else may be said about it,
the Freemasonry actually available in colonial America contained no occult principles to
found anything on.

---

### Franklin

A word about Franklin himself, because he is the most cited American Mason after Washington
and the most misrepresented.

He joined St John's Lodge in Philadelphia in 1731, became its master, printed the
*Constitutions* in 1734, and served as Provincial Grand Master. In France in the 1770s and
1780s, as American commissioner, he became a member and eventually Venerable Master of the
Lodge of the Nine Sisters in Paris — a lodge of intellectuals, artists and philosophes,
which had admitted Voltaire shortly before his death and which Franklin presided over for a
period.

That Parisian connection is where the interesting material is, and it is not occult. The
Nine Sisters was a lodge of the Enlightenment: encyclopedists, scientists, writers. Its
politics were reformist. Franklin's Masonry in Paris was a diplomatic and intellectual
asset, and he used it as one — the lodge was a room in which an American agent could meet
influential Frenchmen without an audience.

Franklin was also, in the same city and the same decade, a member of the royal commission
appointed in 1784 to investigate Franz Mesmer's animal magnetism. The commission's report —
which devised blind testing, found that the effects were produced by imagination rather than
by any magnetic fluid, and is a landmark in the history of experimental method — is one of
the more elegant ironies in this book.[^5]

The most famous American Freemason of the eighteenth century spent part of his time in
Paris debunking the fashionable occult science of the day, using controls.

---

### What the colonies added

By 1775 Freemasonry in America had acquired two features it did not have in England, and
both would matter.

**It was more socially mobile.** English Masonry was aristocratic at the top and
respectable below. American Masonry, in a society with no aristocracy, was a mechanism by
which men of moderate origin could associate with men of standing — and increasingly, as
lodges multiplied, a mechanism by which men of moderate origin associated with each other.

**And it was becoming political, without intending to.** Lodges were where men met. In the
1760s and 1770s, what men met to talk about in Boston and Philadelphia was increasingly the
relationship with Britain. Nothing in Masonic ritual addresses that subject, and Anderson's
*Constitutions* explicitly forbids discussing politics in lodge. But an institution that
gathers the politically active in a private room, under obligations of mutual loyalty, at a
moment of national crisis, is going to be *relevant* whether or not it is intentional.

What happened next has been written about more, and more badly, than almost anything else in
this history.
