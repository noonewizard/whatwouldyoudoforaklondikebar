# CHAPTER THIRTY-ONE
## Revolutionary Brotherhood

The list is genuinely impressive, which is part of the problem.

George Washington was made a Mason at Fredericksburg in 1752, at the age of twenty. Benjamin
Franklin, Provincial Grand Master. Paul Revere, Grand Master of Massachusetts. Joseph
Warren, killed at Bunker Hill, Provincial Grand Master. John Hancock. Israel Putnam. Henry
Knox. Nine signers of the Declaration of Independence by the most careful counts, and more
by the enthusiastic ones. A third or more of Washington's general officers.

On 30 April 1789, Washington took the presidential oath on a Bible borrowed from St John's
Lodge No. 1 of New York, administered by Robert Livingston, Grand Master of New York. In
1793 he laid the corner stone of the Capitol wearing a Masonic apron, with a silver trowel
and the ceremonial of the Craft, before a public procession.[^1]

None of this is disputed. All of it is documented. And almost everything built on it is
wrong, in both directions.

---

### The two errors

**The conspiracy error** says: the United States was founded by Freemasons as a Masonic
project, its capital laid out on occult principles, its seal encoded with esoteric
symbolism, its Constitution a Masonic document.

**The apologetic error** says: Masonic membership was a social formality with no
consequences, and the Craft had nothing to do with the Revolution.

Both fail the same test, which is proportion. The question is not whether Freemasons made
the Revolution or whether Masonry was irrelevant. The question is what an institution of
this kind actually does in a society undergoing that kind of crisis — and that question has
a decent, documented, unexciting answer.

---

### What Masonry actually supplied

Bullock's account, which is the standard one, identifies several contributions, and none of
them is doctrinal.

**A pre-existing intercolonial network.** In 1774 there was almost nothing connecting
politically active men in Boston to politically active men in Charleston. There were no
national newspapers, no political parties, no federal institutions. There *were* Masonic
lodges, mutually recognizing, with a common ceremonial and an obligation of assistance.
When men from thirteen colonies had to learn to trust each other very quickly, some of them
already had a framework for doing so.

**A rehearsal of procedure.** Lodges elected officers by ballot. They kept minutes. They had
constitutions and by-laws, points of order, committees, and rules for admitting and
expelling members. For a great many colonial Americans, the lodge was the first place they
ever participated in a constituted deliberative body that governed itself by written rules.
That is not a small training.

**A vocabulary.** Brotherhood. The level. Virtue. The temple. Harmony. Order. The language
of Revolutionary and early national civic rhetoric is saturated with it, and the saturation
is not coincidental — many of the men producing that rhetoric had heard it every month for
years.

**And a model of association across rank.** The lodge's declaration that men meet on the
level was, in a hierarchical society, a genuine anomaly, and one that Americans took more
seriously than the English did.

Those four things are real, documented, and sufficient. They do not require any esoteric
content, and there was none available to require.

---

### What Masonry did not supply

It did not supply a program. Masonic ritual contains no politics. Anderson's
*Constitutions* explicitly instruct members to be peaceable subjects, never to be concerned
in plots against the state, and to submit to the civil powers. The lodge's own rules forbade
political discussion within it.

And the membership was divided. This is the fact that disposes of the conspiracy reading
most efficiently, and it is almost never mentioned.

**There were Loyalist Freemasons.** Many of them. Masonic lodges existed in the British
army; military lodges traveled with regiments and were among the most important vehicles
of Masonic expansion in the eighteenth century. Officers on both sides of the war were
Masons. Some American lodges split; some ceased working; some carried on with members who
would end the war in Nova Scotia.

An institution that supplies both sides of a war with members is not directing it.

---

### The Boston case

The most interesting American Masonic politics is local and it concerns two rival grand
lodges, which tells us more than the national story does.

Boston had, from the 1750s, both a Grand Lodge deriving from England's "Moderns" and a body
deriving from the "Antients" — the rival English grand lodge formed in 1751, whose appeal
was to Irish immigrants and to men outside the established elite. The Antients were, socially,
the less grand of the two, and in Boston that mapped onto the political division with
reasonable fidelity: the Antients' St Andrew's Lodge was the home of Joseph Warren and Paul
Revere, and its members were prominent in the patriot movement.

It was at the Green Dragon Tavern, which St Andrew's Lodge owned a share of, that the
Boston Tea Party was organized — and the lodge's minutes for the evening of 16 December
1773 record that the lodge closed early on account of the small number of members present.
This has been cited for two centuries as evidence of Masonic direction of the Tea Party.
What it is evidence of is that the men who threw the tea into the harbor were also the men
who attended that lodge, which is a different claim, and a true one.[^2]

The distinction is exactly the distinction this book keeps insisting on. Overlapping
membership is not institutional action. A room where the patriots met is not a patriot
organization, any more than a tavern is.

---

### Washington

Washington's own Masonry has been the subject of more invention than any other aspect of
this history, so it is worth stating what the record shows.

He was initiated in 1752 and passed and raised in 1753 at Fredericksburg Lodge. He attended
irregularly. During the war he took part in Masonic observances with military lodges, and
there is good evidence he valued the fraternity's role in the army. He was named charter
master of Alexandria Lodge No. 22 in 1788 and accepted, though he did not preside actively.
He wrote courteous letters to Masonic bodies. He laid the Capitol corner stone in 1793.

He also, in 1798, wrote a letter to a Reverend G. W. Snyder, who had sent him a copy of
Robison's *Proofs of a Conspiracy*, in which he said that he did not believe the lodges of
Freemasons in this country had, as societies, endeavored to propagate the doctrines of the
Illuminati or the principles of Jacobinism, and that he had not been in a lodge more than
once or twice in the last thirty years.[^3]

That letter is routinely quarried by both sides — conspiracists cite the first half, Masons
the second. Read whole, it is the statement of a man who thought the fraternity was
harmless, who was not personally very active in it, and who was faintly irritated at being
asked.

---

### The corner stone

I want to end on the image, because it is the right one for this chapter.

In September 1793 the President of the United States walked in procession to the site of
the Capitol, wearing a Masonic apron, carrying a silver trowel, accompanied by lodges of
Maryland and Virginia in full regalia, before a crowd, with artillery, and laid a stone with
corn, wine and oil.

Everything about that scene is public. The newspapers reported it. The apron survives. The
trowel survives.

And what it means is not that a secret society was founding a nation in code. It means that
in 1793 the Masonic corner-stone ceremony was simply *how Americans consecrated a public
building* — the available civic ritual for a republic that had abolished established
religion and needed something to do at ceremonies. The Craft supplied a non-sectarian
liturgy for a state that could not use a sectarian one.

That is the American story in a sentence, and it is the carriage thesis again in a
different register. Freemasonry gave the new republic a *form* — of association, of
procedure, of public ceremony — and the republic filled it with its own content.

Which is precisely what a group of free Black men in Boston had already understood, nine
years earlier, when they obtained a warrant from London and began to do the same thing for
themselves.
