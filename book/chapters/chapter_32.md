# CHAPTER THIRTY-TWO
## African Lodge No. 459

On 29 September 1784, the Grand Lodge of England issued a warrant constituting African
Lodge No. 459 in Boston, Massachusetts. Its Master was a free Black man named Prince Hall.

The warrant took three years to arrive. When it did, the lodge it authorised was as regular
as any in America — chartered by the same body, under the same constitutions, working the
same degrees. And American Freemasonry spent the next two hundred years declining to
recognise it.

The reasons given changed over time. The reason did not.

---

### Prince Hall

What is known about him is less than one would wish and more than is usually reported.

He was born around 1735 or 1738; the place is uncertain, and the confident accounts of a
Barbadian birth rest on nineteenth-century sources of doubtful reliability. He was enslaved
in the household of a Boston leatherworker named William Hall, and was manumitted in 1770.
He worked as a leatherdresser and caterer. He was a property owner and a voter. He was a
Methodist. He signed petitions.

Those petitions are the best evidence we have of his mind, and they are remarkable. In
1777 he was among the signers of a petition to the Massachusetts legislature arguing that
the natural right to freedom belonged to every person and that enslavement was incompatible
with the principles the colonies were then asserting against Britain. In 1787 he petitioned
for equal access to schools for Black children in Boston, and when refused, helped to
establish a school. In 1788 he petitioned successfully against the kidnapping of free Black
Bostonians into slavery.[^1]

A man petitioning a legislature in 1777 on the incompatibility of slavery with natural
right is not incidentally political. He was building institutions in a city that offered
his community none, and Freemasonry was one of them.

---

### The initiation

In March 1775, Prince Hall and fourteen other free Black men were initiated into
Freemasonry in Boston — not by an American lodge, but by a military lodge attached to a
British regiment then garrisoning the town, on the eve of the Revolution.[^2]

The circumstance is worth pausing on. Irish military lodges travelling with British
regiments were among the most prolific initiators in the eighteenth-century Masonic world,
and they operated with a latitude that settled lodges did not. When the regiment departed,
the fifteen men were left with a permission to meet as a lodge but without a charter to
work degrees.

They met that way for nine years. In 1784 they applied to London. In 1787 the warrant
reached Boston. African Lodge No. 459 was constituted, and in 1791 Prince Hall was
designated Provincial Grand Master.

Everything in that sequence is procedurally regular. The warrant came from the same Grand
Lodge that had chartered lodges across the colonies.

---

### The refusal

American Masonic bodies did not recognise it, then or for a very long time afterwards.

The arguments deployed over two centuries were various: that the warrant lapsed when
English Grand Lodge authority ended in America; that African Lodge had become irregular by
failing to affiliate; that territorial jurisdiction was exclusive and a state Grand Lodge
could not permit a second body in its territory. These are real points of Masonic law and
they were argued with great technical seriousness.

They were also applied to no other case with anything like the same rigour. The territorial
argument, in particular, was applied in states where multiple grand bodies had coexisted
without difficulty, and was maintained in jurisdictions that recognised foreign grand
lodges thousands of miles away while refusing the one in their own city.

The stated reasons were jurisdictional. The operative reason was race, and the historical
scholarship is unanimous on this.[^3]

Recognition came slowly and unevenly. Some northern jurisdictions recognised Prince Hall
Grand Lodges from the late twentieth century; a number of southern ones did not do so until
the twenty-first; at the time of writing, a small number still have not.

---

### What it means for this book

I have placed this chapter at the structural centre of the book's argument, and not for the
reason a reader might expect. It is here because it is *evidence*.

Part IV showed Masonic form being filled with content from outside: chivalric, Templar,
theurgic, alchemical. Chapter 29 showed it filled with Genesis and adapted for women. The
pattern was clear but a sceptic could still object that all of those cases involve
initiatory or religious content, and that there might be something in Masonic ritual that
specifically invites it.

African Lodge removes the objection completely.

Here the same form — identical degrees, identical warrant, identical ritual — is taken up
by a community for whom its meaning is *neither esoteric nor moral but political*: an
institution of self-governance in a society that permitted them none. A lodge is a body
that elects its own officers, keeps its own funds, disciplines its own members, buries its
own dead with honour, and corresponds as an equal with bodies abroad. For free Black
Bostonians in 1787, none of those capacities was available anywhere else.

Chernoh Sesay's work on the social origins of Black Freemasonry makes the case in detail:
the lodge was a mechanism of community formation, leadership, mutual aid and public
standing, emerging precisely in the passage from slavery to freedom.[^4] Stephen Kantrowitz
has traced how the institution carried forward into the nineteenth century as a vehicle of
Black political life.

The ritual did not change. The meaning did, completely, because the users were different.

**That is the carriage thesis proved in a register where no magic is involved at all.** The
Masonic form is a container. What it means is set by who picks it up.

---

### The universalism problem

There is a harder point here and I do not want to slide past it.

Anderson's *Constitutions* required that a candidate be free-born. In eighteenth-century
England that clause was a relic of guild qualification, distinguishing free men from
bondmen in a society that had few of the latter. Transplanted to a slave society, it became
something else, and it was used as something else: a formerly enslaved man was not
free-*born*, and the clause was deployed against Prince Hall Masonry accordingly.

A rule written for one society became an instrument of exclusion in another. Nobody in
London intended that. It happened anyway, and it happened because the universalist language
of the Craft — men meeting on the level, the brotherhood of all mankind — was always being
worked by people with a narrower idea of who counted.

The same tension appears everywhere in this part of the book. Colonial lodges in
slaveholding societies were full of slaveholders. Lodges that declared the equality of all
men under the Great Architect excluded women by rule and Black men by practice. Masonic
imperial networks, as Jessica Harland-Jacobs has shown, carried British power around the
globe while proclaiming universal brotherhood, and their local application of that
brotherhood was calibrated precisely to colonial racial hierarchy.[^5]

I do not think this makes the universalist language hypocritical, exactly. I think it makes
it *unfinished* — a claim made in advance of anybody's willingness to honour it, which
people then held the institution to. Prince Hall's petitions are an example of exactly that
move: taking the Revolution's language of natural right and insisting on its application.
His lodge did the same thing with the Craft's.

An institution that says all men meet on the level, and then refuses, has handed its
critics the standard by which it will be judged. That is not nothing. It is, in fact, a
substantial part of why Masonic language proved so useful to the people it excluded.

---

### The long consequence

Prince Hall Freemasonry became one of the most important institutions in African American
life. Through the nineteenth century and into the twentieth, Prince Hall lodges were
centres of leadership, mutual aid and respectability politics, with a membership that
included a large share of Black professional and civic leadership. W. E. B. Du Bois was a
member. Thurgood Marshall was a member.

And it accumulated its own historiography, its own disputes, and its own claim to
legitimacy — argued, for two centuries, on precisely the documentary grounds this book has
been using throughout. *Where is the warrant? Who issued it? On what date? Under what
authority?*

The answer, in this case, is satisfying in a way that few answers in this book are. The
warrant existed. It is dated. It was issued by the Grand Lodge of England on 29 September
1784.

The documents were never the problem.
