# CHAPTER THIRTY-THREE
## The Morgan Affair

In the summer of 1826, a stonemason and bricklayer in Batavia, New York, told people he was
going to publish the secrets of Freemasonry.

William Morgan was about fifty, a drinker, in debt, and of unreliable reputation. He had
claimed Masonic membership; whether he had been regularly initiated is disputed. He had been
refused admission to the new Royal Arch chapter at Batavia, and he was angry about it. He
made an arrangement with a local printer named David Miller, himself a disaffected Mason,
to publish an exposure.

What happened next destroyed American Freemasonry for a generation, produced the first
third party in United States history, and remains the most consequential single episode in
the Craft's history anywhere in the world.

---

### The events

The sequence, so far as it can be established from the trials and contemporary records, ran
as follows.

Word of the forthcoming book spread among local Masons through the summer of 1826. There
were attempts to stop it: pressure on Miller, an attempt to burn the printing office, and
harassment.

On 11 September, Morgan was arrested on a petty charge — a debt, and an allegation of
stealing a shirt and tie — and taken to Canandaigua. He was released, immediately re-arrested
on the debt, and jailed. On the evening of 12 September, someone paid the debt, and he was
released from jail at night. Outside, he was seized, forced into a carriage, and driven
west.

The carriage route can be traced with reasonable confidence: through a series of villages,
changing horses, to Fort Niagara, an abandoned military post on the American side of the
Niagara River. Morgan was held in the magazine there.

After that, nothing is certain. He was never seen again.[^1]

---

### What happened to him

We do not know, and I want to be careful, because both certainties on offer are unearned.

The Masonic-apologetic position — that Morgan was paid off and sent to Canada, or emigrated,
or was later seen abroad — rests on rumours and sightings of the kind that follow every
disappearance, none corroborated.

The anti-Masonic position — that he was rowed into the river and drowned — rests on a
confession made many years later, of doubtful provenance, and on the general probabilities.

A body recovered from the lake at Oak Orchard in 1827 was identified as Morgan at an
inquest, then re-examined and identified as a Canadian named Timothy Monro, whose widow
described his clothing accurately. The anti-Masonic leader Thurlow Weed is reported to have
remarked that the body was "a good enough Morgan until after the election," a line that has
followed American politics ever since, and which tells you a great deal about the atmosphere
of the moment.[^2]

What is certain: Morgan was abducted, by Freemasons, in an organised operation involving
many people across several counties, and he disappeared. Several men were tried. The
convictions obtained were for conspiracy to abduct, and the sentences were light — the
longest was a matter of years, most were months.

Those light sentences did more damage than the abduction.

---

### The reaction

Because what the public concluded — and it was not an unreasonable conclusion — was that
the courts would not convict Freemasons.

Consider the local situation. In western New York in 1826, a substantial proportion of
sheriffs, judges, jurors, prosecutors and newspaper editors were Masons. A crime had been
committed by Masons against a man who threatened the fraternity. Witnesses were
uncooperative. Investigations stalled. Grand juries returned little. Some officials
obstructed openly.

And the obligation itself was quoted back at them. A Mason had sworn to keep a brother's
secrets. What did that oath require of a juror?

That question — whether a private obligation could override a public duty — is the real
subject of the Morgan affair, and it is the reason the reaction was so enormous. It was not
primarily about a missing bricklayer. It was about whether a republic could tolerate
institutions whose members owed each other prior loyalties.

---

### Anti-Masonry

What followed was one of the strangest and most consequential mass movements in American
history.

Anti-Masonic conventions met. Anti-Masonic newspapers proliferated — dozens of them.
Churches, particularly in the evangelical revival that was sweeping the same region at the
same time, demanded that members renounce the lodge. Ministers preached against it.
Thousands of Masons publicly seceded, and their renunciations were printed.

And in 1828 it became a political party — the first significant third party in United States
history, and the first to hold a national nominating convention, in Baltimore in 1831, a
procedural innovation that both major parties promptly copied. It ran a presidential
candidate in 1832, carried Vermont, and elected governors and congressmen. Among the men
who began their careers in it were Thurlow Weed, William Seward, and Thaddeus Stevens.[^3]

Historians have long debated what Antimasonry was really about, and the debate is worth
noting. Kathleen Smith Kutolowski's work on the social base of the movement found that it
was not a movement of the dispossessed against an elite in any simple sense — the
distinctions between Masons and Antimasons in western New York were subtler than the
rhetoric suggested.[^4] It correlates strongly with evangelical revivalism, with
temperance, and with a broad anxiety about secret and privileged associations in a
democratic society.

Its effect on Freemasonry was devastating. In New York State, lodge numbers collapsed —
from hundreds to a fraction. Grand lodges in several states suspended operations. The total
American membership fell by something like two-thirds over the following decade. Some
lodges surrendered their charters; others simply stopped meeting and hoped.

---

### What it demonstrates

Three things, and each connects to an argument running through this book.

**One: secrecy has costs, and they come due in a crisis.** Chapter 3 described how
concealment manufactures authority and mythology. The Morgan affair is the bill. An
institution that had cultivated an air of privileged knowledge and mutual obligation found,
when something went wrong, that the public had no way to evaluate what had happened and
every reason to assume the worst. The lodge's opacity, which had been an asset for a
century, became the thing that convicted it.

**Two: the conspiracy was real, and it was local and small.** This is the part that neither
side likes. The abduction of William Morgan *was* a Masonic conspiracy: organised,
multi-county, involving officials, and followed by obstruction. It was also completely
unlike the thing the Antimasons believed they had uncovered. It was not directed from
anywhere. There was no national plan, no international body, no secret ruler. It was a
group of provincial men in western New York who panicked about a book and did something
criminal, and then their friends covered for them.

That is what Masonic conspiracies actually look like when they occur, and the gap between
that and the imagined one is instructive. Real institutional wrongdoing tends to be local,
improvised, and stupid. The version the conspiracy literature requires — coordinated,
patient, global, invisible — is a different kind of object, and the Morgan affair is
evidence against it rather than for it, because it shows what happens when Masons actually
do conspire: they get caught, tried, and written about for two centuries.

**Three: the movement against it made American politics.** The national nominating
convention, the modern third-party template, and a cohort of politicians who would go on to
found the Republican Party and drive emancipation — all of it came out of the reaction to a
disappearance in Batavia.

---

### The recovery

Freemasonry in America did not die. It came back from the 1840s, and by the later nineteenth
century it was larger than it had ever been — the age of the great Masonic temples, of the
Scottish Rite's expansion, of the Shrine, of fraternal orders generally, in what historians
of the period call the golden age of fraternity.

But it came back changed. The post-Morgan Craft was more careful, more publicly charitable,
more anxious to be seen as respectable, and considerably more invested in explaining
itself. The enormous American Masonic literature of the nineteenth century — Mackey's
encyclopedias, the ritual monitors, the lecture systems, and eventually Pike's *Morals and
Dogma* — is partly a product of an institution that had learned what happens when the
public cannot tell what you are.

Which is to say: the Craft responded to an accusation of hidden knowledge by producing an
enormous quantity of interpretation.

And the man who produced the most influential interpretation of all was, at the time of the
Morgan affair, a seventeen-year-old schoolteacher in Massachusetts who had not yet gone
west.
