# CHAPTER THIRTY-FOUR
## Empire and the Portable Lodge

A regiment of the British army in the eighteenth century might carry, among its
impedimenta, a warrant.

Irish Grand Lodge in particular issued charters to lodges attached to regiments rather than
to places — traveling lodges, which moved with the battalion and could be opened wherever
it was quartered. There were hundreds of them. They operated in Ireland, in England, in the
Americas, in the Caribbean, in India, in the Mediterranean, in Africa, and wherever else the
British army went, which by the nineteenth century was very nearly everywhere.

They were among the most effective vehicles of Masonic expansion in history, and the reason
is structural. A lodge that is attached to a place needs a building and a settled
membership. A lodge that is attached to a warrant needs neither. It needs a room, a few
objects, and enough members to open.

It was one such lodge that initiated Prince Hall in Boston in 1775.

---

### The infrastructure of empire

Jessica Harland-Jacobs's study of Freemasonry and British imperialism is the essential work
here, and its argument is worth stating carefully because it is easily caricatured.[^1]

Her claim is not that Freemasonry ran the empire. It is that Freemasonry functioned as
imperial *infrastructure* — one of the connective tissues by which a scattered, mobile,
administratively thin empire held itself together.

Consider the problem an imperial system faces. It must move men constantly — soldiers,
officials, merchants, engineers, planters — between places where they know nobody. Each
arrival must be integrated into a local society of expatriates. Each must be assessed for
trustworthiness. Each needs sociability, credit, and, when things go badly, assistance and
a decent burial.

Freemasonry supplied all of it, with a recognition system that worked across the entire
network. A Scottish engineer arriving in Calcutta, a Cornish officer posted to Jamaica, an
Irish clerk sent to Cape Town — each could present himself at a local lodge and be received.

By the mid-nineteenth century the Craft was established across the empire on a scale that
is difficult to overstate: lodges in every substantial colonial settlement, district grand
lodges, imperial dignitaries as grand masters, and a constant traffic of certificates,
warrants and correspondence flowing back to London, Edinburgh and Dublin.

### Universalism and its limits

And here the tension of the last two chapters reappears, sharpened.

Masonic rhetoric was emphatically universal. The Craft declared itself the center of union
among men who would otherwise have remained at a perpetual distance. It admitted men
without regard to nation or creed. In India, lodges admitted Indians — Parsis first and
most readily, then Hindus and Muslims — and Anderson's religious formula was stretched to
accommodate them, with candidates obligated on their own sacred books.

That is a real and significant thing, and it happened earlier and more extensively than the
surrounding colonial society would have predicted.

It was also, everywhere, contested and limited. Admission of non-Europeans was resisted in
many lodges, granted grudgingly in others, and in some colonial contexts effectively
prohibited by social practice regardless of what the constitutions said. In southern Africa
and in the Caribbean, color lines ran through the Craft as they ran through everything
else. And the entire imperial Masonic network operated as an instrument of an imperial
project, whatever its members believed about brotherhood.

The honest formulation is that Freemasonry in the empire was *simultaneously* a genuine site
of cross-cultural association unusual for its time, and a component of a structure of
domination. Both statements are true. Treating either as the whole truth produces bad
history — the first produces the self-congratulatory Masonic version, the second produces a
flattening that cannot explain why Indian, Ottoman and African men joined in substantial
numbers and found it useful.

Thierry Zarcone's work on Freemasonry in the Ottoman and Islamic world documents the latter
case in detail: lodges as sites of reformist politics, of contact between Ottoman elites and
European ideas, and eventually of nationalist organizing — which is to say, Masonic
structures used against the imperial interests that had carried them there.[^2]

The form again. It travels, and it does what its users want.

### Jews and the Craft

A second case, differently instructive.

Jewish men were admitted to English lodges within a few years of the 1723 *Constitutions*,
at a time when Jews in Britain labored under substantial civil disabilities and would not
be admitted to Parliament for another century and a quarter. Anderson's formula — the
religion in which all men agree — made it possible, and lodges took the opportunity.

Jacob Katz's study of Jews and Freemasons in Europe traces the consequences, and they are
not simple.[^3] In Britain and the Netherlands, admission was comparatively easy. In the
German lands it was fought over for a century, with some lodges admitting, others refusing,
and others admitting Jews only as visitors. In France the position shifted with the
Revolution. Everywhere, Jewish membership was both a genuine opening and a recurring
occasion for conflict.

And it produced a long-running libel. From the late nineteenth century, antisemitic
polemic in France, Germany and Russia fused the two — *judéo-maçonnerie*, the Jewish-Masonic
conspiracy — into a single imagined enemy. That fusion was a principal theme of the
*Protocols of the Elders of Zion* and of the propaganda that followed, and it had lethal
consequences. Freemasonry was banned and its members persecuted under fascist regimes
across Europe; Masonic bodies were suppressed in Italy, Germany, Spain and occupied
territories, and Masons were among the groups deported and killed.

That deserves to be stated plainly in a book about Masonic secrecy, because it is the
clearest possible demonstration of what opacity costs. An organization that will not
explain itself supplies its enemies with a blank surface. In the twentieth century, people
wrote things on that surface and then killed for them.

### Class

One more dimension, more mundane and easily lost.

Freemasonry in the eighteenth century was a gentleman's institution. In the nineteenth it
became something broader, and in Britain it became substantially a *lower-middle-class* one
— shopkeepers, clerks, foremen, tradesmen — alongside its grander lodges.

This matters because it is bound up with the enormous nineteenth-century expansion of
friendly societies and fraternal orders generally: the Oddfellows, the Foresters, the Buffs
and dozens more, most of which borrowed Masonic ritual form wholesale. Daniel Weinbren and
others have documented the relationship between Masonic ceremonial and the mutual-aid
movement, and it is a large and under-appreciated part of the Craft's influence.[^4]

Before the welfare state, an ordinary family's protection against sickness, unemployment
and burial costs was a subscription to a society that paid benefits. Those societies needed
trust, discipline, and a mechanism for binding members to obligations — and they reached,
almost without exception, for the Masonic template: degrees, passwords, regalia, officers,
a ceremonial of admission.

So the largest single application of Masonic ritual technology in the nineteenth century
was not esoteric at all. It was insurance.

I find that a useful thing to know when reading the occult literature of the same decades.
While Éliphas Lévi was reconstructing ceremonial magic in Paris and Westcott was assembling
the Golden Dawn in London, several million Europeans were being initiated into
Masonic-derived ceremonies in order to secure sick pay.

---

### Part V's finding

The Atlantic and imperial chapters establish something the esoteric chapters cannot,
precisely because no esotericism is involved.

The Masonic form proved portable to a degree that no other institutional form of its period
matched. It crossed oceans, empires, languages, religions, classes and the color line. It
was used by revolutionaries and loyalists, slaveholders and abolitionists, imperial
administrators and anti-imperial nationalists, aristocrats and clerks, men and women.

In every case it did roughly the same *structural* work — bounded association, graded
admission, mutual obligation, self-government, public ceremony — and in every case its
*meaning* was supplied by the people using it.

Which means that when we turn, in the next part, to a group of Victorian Englishmen who
took the Masonic form and filled it with Kabbalah, Egyptian god-names and ceremonial magic,
we should not be surprised, and we should not treat it as a revelation about Freemasonry.

They were doing what everybody else had been doing for a hundred and fifty years.

They were just doing it with stranger cargo, and they had a French author to supply it.
