# PART VI — THE OCCULT REVIVAL

# CHAPTER THIRTY-FIVE
## Éliphas Lévi

In 1847 a Frenchman of thirty-seven was released from prison for the second time. He had
been convicted, on both occasions, of writing books.

The first, *La Bible de la liberté*, had appeared in 1841 and been seized within days: a
work of revolutionary socialism in prophetic register, declaring that the poor were the true
inheritors and that the existing order was an offence against God. The second, in 1847,
belonged to the same current. Between them he had written on the emancipation of women, on
the Christian communism of Lamennais and Buchez, and in a milieu that included Flora
Tristan, the utopian socialists and the Catholic left.

His name was Alphonse-Louis Constant. He had trained for the priesthood at Saint-Sulpice
and been ordained deacon, and had left before taking final vows, unable — the accounts
vary — to accept celibacy or to accept the Church.

Seven years after his release, in 1854, he published the first volume of a work under a
different name. He had rendered Alphonse-Louis Constant into its Hebrew equivalents: Éliphas
Lévi Zahed. The book was called *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*.

It is the single most influential book on magic written in the last five hundred years, and
almost everything after it in this book descends from it.[^1]

---

### The revision

The received account of Lévi, repeated in occult literature for a century and a half, is
that he recovered a tradition: that he read the old books — Agrippa, the grimoires, the
Kabbalah — and restored to Europe a magical science that had been lost.

The work of Julian Strube has substantially dismantled this, and it is the most important
recent revision affecting the whole field.[^2]

Strube's method is simply to read Constant's earlier writings and trace the continuities.
What emerges is that the magical synthesis of 1854 did not arrive from nowhere after a
period of antiquarian study. It grew directly out of the socialist and Catholic-heterodox
writing of the 1840s, and it carries that inheritance in its bones.

The 1840s Constant had argued that a primitive revelation underlay all religions, that the
Church had corrupted it, that a coming synthesis would reconcile religion and reason, and
that the reconciliation would inaugurate a transformed social order. The 1850s Lévi argues
the same things — with *magic* as the name for the primitive revelation, and the magus as
the figure who holds the synthesis.

The revolutionary content did not disappear. It was transposed.

This matters for a reason that goes beyond biography. It means that *occultism*, as a
movement, is not the resurfacing of an ancient current. It is a nineteenth-century
formation with nineteenth-century concerns — the crisis of religious authority, the
prestige of science, the failure of 1848, the search for a synthesis that would satisfy both
faith and reason.

Wouter Hanegraaff's larger argument points the same way: the category of Western esotericism
is itself a construction, and the tradition it names looks continuous mainly because its
participants kept insisting it was.[^3]

---

### What the book says

*Dogme et rituel* is in two volumes and twenty-two chapters each, one for every letter of
the Hebrew alphabet and — Lévi's own innovation — one for every trump of the tarot.

Its central doctrine can be stated in three propositions.

**One: there is a universal agent.** Lévi calls it the astral light: a subtle medium
pervading everything, the vehicle of life, light and imagination, plastic to the will. Every
magical operation works through it. It is, quite clearly, a synthesis of Mesmer's animal
magnetism with the Renaissance *spiritus* of Chapter 8 and with the physics of an age
excited about imponderable fluids — electricity, magnetism, the luminiferous ether.

**Two: the instrument is the will.** This is Lévi's decisive move and it is what makes him
modern. In the older ceremonial magic, the operator compels or petitions a spirit. In Lévi,
the operator's trained will acts upon the astral light directly. Ceremonies, names, circles
and instruments are not addressed to anyone; they are devices for concentrating the
operator's own imagination and resolve.

**Three: the key is the Kabbalah, and the key to the Kabbalah is the tarot.**

That third proposition deserves its own paragraph, because it is an invention.

---

### The tarot

The tarot is a fifteenth-century Italian card game. It acquired a divinatory and esoteric
reputation in eighteenth-century France, principally through Court de Gébelin, who in the
1780s declared it a survival of Egyptian wisdom, and through the cartomancer Etteilla.
There is no evidence for the Egyptian claim and the documentary history of the cards as a
game is well established.

Lévi's contribution was to connect the tarot to the Kabbalah. Twenty-two trumps,
twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet: therefore, he proposed, a correspondence.
Therefore the trumps encode the letters, the letters encode the paths of the Tree of Life,
and the whole system is a single key.

No Jewish source contains this. No pre-modern source of any kind contains it. It is Lévi's,
published in the 1850s.

And it became, within forty years, the organising structure of the entire Anglo-American
magical tradition. The Golden Dawn built its grade system on it. Crowley elaborated it in
*Liber 777* and in the *Book of Thoth*. Every tarot deck on sale today with Hebrew letters
or Sephirotic attributions is downstream of a Frenchman's analogy from a numerical
coincidence.

I want to be clear that I do not say this to belittle it. It is an extraordinarily
productive idea, and systems do not have to be ancient to work. But the sequence must be
stated correctly, because the reverse is asserted constantly: the tarot-Kabbalah system is
not ancient wisdom that Lévi recovered. It is a nineteenth-century construction that later
practitioners projected backwards.

---

### The Baphomet

And then there is the picture.

In the second volume, Lévi published a drawing of his own: a seated winged figure with the
head of a goat, a torch between its horns, one arm raised and one lowered with the words
*solve* and *coagula* inscribed on them, a caduceus at the belly, breasts, and a goat's
lower body. He called it the Sabbatic Goat, the Baphomet of Mendes.

His explanation is emphatically *not* satanic. The figure is a composite emblem of the
universal equilibrium — the union of opposites, male and female, above and below, dissolution
and coagulation. It is a pantacle, a summary of his system in one image.

That drawing is now the visual definition of the Devil in Western popular culture. It is on
album covers and in films. It is what people picture when they hear the word satanic.

It was drawn in 1856 by a former socialist pamphleteer to illustrate a doctrine of balance.

We will follow its career in Chapter 38, because it is also the image that will be used,
within forty years, to accuse an American Freemason of devil-worship on the strength of a
forged letter.

### The man

A word about Lévi himself, because the temperament matters.

He was poor for most of his life and made his living by writing and by teaching students.
He married a woman much younger than himself who left him. He was, by the accounts of those
who knew him, gentle, learned, talkative and somewhat vain. He performed at least one
recorded ceremonial operation — an evocation of Apollonius of Tyana, conducted in London in
1854, which he described afterwards in careful and rather deflating terms: he saw
something, he was exhausted, he was not certain what had happened, and he advised against
repeating it.[^4]

That report is worth holding up against everything the twentieth century made of him. It is
the account of a cautious man describing an ambiguous experience and refusing to overstate
it.

He remained, throughout, a Catholic — heterodox, argued-with, and sincere. He believed he
was restoring the Church's own lost science, not opposing it.

---

### The transmission

How did a French author reach everyone?

By being read, and the routes are documented.

**Into England.** *Dogme et rituel* circulated among English occultists from the 1850s; Lévi
visited London in 1854 and again in 1861, meeting Kenneth Mackenzie, Edward Bulwer-Lytton
and others in the circles that would produce the SRIA. A. E. Waite translated him into
English, beginning in the 1880s, and the translations carried him to a mass audience.

**Into the Golden Dawn.** Mathers, Westcott and their colleagues read him. The astral light,
the tarot-Kabbalah correspondence, the pentagram and its orientations, the doctrine of the
will — all of it is in the Order's material.

**Into Theosophy.** Blavatsky quotes and argues with him extensively in *Isis Unveiled*.

**And into American Freemasonry.** Albert Pike read him in French, and used him — very
extensively and almost entirely without attribution — in *Morals and Dogma*, which was
published in 1871 and given to Scottish Rite initiates for the following century.

That last route is the one this book has been travelling toward since Chapter 1.

Because it means that when an American Mason opened the standard commentary on his own
degrees and found Kabbalah, the astral light, the pentagram, comparative mythology and the
symbolism of the pillars as Severity and Mercy — he was not reading an ancient Masonic
tradition.

He was reading a Parisian who had been in prison for socialism fifteen years before the
book was written.
