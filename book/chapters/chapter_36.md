# CHAPTER THIRTY-SIX
## The Occult Renaissance

The nineteenth century was, by a wide margin, the most religiously inventive period in
European history since the Reformation, and the inventions came from an unexpected
direction.

Not from the churches, which were losing ground. Not from the universities, which were
professionalising. From a diffuse, commercial, largely middle-class culture of seances,
lectures, periodicals, correspondence courses and small orders — an ecosystem that
historians now call the occult revival, and that produced, within about fifty years, nearly
every institution and idea that constitutes modern Western esotericism.

This chapter maps the ecosystem, because the next seven chapters take place inside it.

---

### Mesmer's afterlife

Begin with a Viennese physician who had been discredited by a French royal commission in
1784, with Benjamin Franklin on it.

Franz Anton Mesmer had proposed a universal magnetic fluid, blockages in which caused
illness, and which a practitioner could redirect. The commission — using what were in
effect blind trials — concluded that the effects were real but were produced by
imagination, not by any fluid.

That finding should have ended it. Instead, mesmerism flourished for another century, and it
did so because the commission had inadvertently identified something more interesting than
what it disproved. If imagination could produce these effects, then imagination was a
physical force, and the investigation of it was a science.

Nineteenth-century mesmerism produced trance states, somnambulism, clairvoyance,
anaesthesia, and a very large body of reported phenomena. It is one of the ancestors of
hypnosis and of dynamic psychiatry. It also supplied the occult revival with three things
it could not have done without: a *medium* through which invisible influence travels, a
*technique* for inducing altered states, and a *vocabulary* that sounded scientific.

Lévi's astral light is mesmeric fluid with a cosmology attached.

---

### Spiritualism

On 31 March 1848, in a farmhouse at Hydesville, New York, two sisters named Fox reported
that rapping noises in the house were answering their questions.

Within five years spiritualism was a mass movement on two continents. Within a decade it
had millions of adherents, its own press, its own lecture circuit and its own celebrity
mediums. It reached the highest levels of society — there were seances at the White House —
and it retained a large following into the twentieth century, boosted by the bereavement of
the First World War.

Spiritualism is not the same thing as occultism, and the distinction was fought over at the
time. Spiritualism was broadly democratic, Christian in tone, and focused on contact with
the dead. Occultism was hierarchical, initiatory, and interested in the operator's
development rather than in messages from Aunt Mary. Blavatsky, who began in spiritualist
circles, spent much of her later career distinguishing herself from it, sometimes rudely.

But spiritualism did something indispensable: it created the audience. It produced, in two
generations, a large public accustomed to the idea that invisible intelligences could be
contacted, that special people could do it, and that this was a matter for investigation
rather than dogma. Every occult order of the later nineteenth century recruited from that
public.

### The publishing machine

The revival ran on print, and this is the least glamorous and most important fact about it.

Cheap steam printing, rising literacy, a postal system, and a market for periodicals
produced an infrastructure that had not existed before. There were occult publishers —
George Redway, who printed Mathers's *Kabbalah Unveiled*; John M. Watkins, still trading a
century later — occult booksellers, occult magazines, and a second-hand trade in old
esoteric books that made Agrippa, the grimoires and the alchemical corpus available to
anyone with a few shillings and an address.

This is the mechanism by which Part II reaches Part VI. The Renaissance magical library did
not survive in a secret order. It survived in *the antiquarian book trade*, and it was
reassembled in the nineteenth century by collectors.

Alison Butler's reconstruction of the two most important English collections makes the
point precisely. Frederick Hockley, a working occultist of the mid-Victorian period, spent
decades assembling and copying magical manuscripts. William Wynn Westcott built a Hermetic
library of two hundred and eighty-six items, weighted heavily toward seventeenth- and
eighteenth-century cabalistic, Rosicrucian, Hermetic and Neoplatonic sources.[^1]

When we ask, in Chapter 41, where the Golden Dawn's material came from, this is the answer.
It came off a shelf, and we know what was on the shelf.

### The French scene

Paris after Lévi produced an institutional occultism with a distinctly French character:
literary, quarrelsome, and connected to both Catholic traditionalism and the political left.

Its central organizer was Gérard Encausse, a physician who wrote as **Papus**. He was
enormously productive — his *Traité méthodique de science occulte* (1891) is a synthesis on
the Lévi model — and enormously energetic as a founder of things. Chief among them was the
revived **Martinist Order**, which took its name and claimed its descent from Louis-Claude
de Saint-Martin, whom we met in Chapter 27.

That claim is worth a sentence of scrutiny. Saint-Martin had no order; he had readers, and
a deliberate suspicion of ceremonial organization. The nineteenth-century Martinist Order is
a reconstruction on the model of what its founders thought Saint-Martin's circle had been,
using materials they had assembled themselves. The continuity is doctrinal and literary,
not institutional.

It is the same pattern as everywhere else in this part of the book: a real inheritance of
*texts*, dressed as an inheritance of *organization*.

The French scene also produced Stanislas de Guaita's Kabbalistic Order of the Rose-Cross,
Joséphin Péladan's aesthetic Rosicrucian salons, and a series of public feuds that provided
excellent copy for the Parisian press — which matters more than it sounds, because that
press coverage is the environment in which Léo Taxil would shortly find his market.

### The English pipeline

And now the institutional route that this book cares about most.

In 1866, Robert Wentworth Little and a circle of English Masons founded the **Societas
Rosicruciana in Anglia**. Its material was drawn substantially from the German Rosicrucian
tradition of Chapter 28 — its grade names are recognizably those of the Gold- und
Rosenkreuz — and its purpose was the study of esoteric subjects.

Its entry requirement was that a candidate be a Master Mason in good standing.[^2]

That single condition is the pipeline. It means that in Victorian England, the route into
organized esoteric study ran *through the Craft*. A man interested in Kabbalah, alchemy or
Hermetic philosophy who wanted to pursue it in company had to be a Freemason first.

The consequences are visible in the membership. Kenneth Mackenzie, compiler of the *Royal
Masonic Cyclopaedia* and a man who had met Lévi. Frederick Hockley. William Robert Woodman,
a physician, who became Supreme Magus. William Wynn Westcott, a coroner. Samuel Liddell
Mathers.

The three men who founded the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn in 1888 were all
Freemasons, and they met each other in a Rosicrucian society that required Masonic
membership to join.

**This is the documented junction between Freemasonry and modern ceremonial magic.** Not
Egypt. Not the Templars. Not a hidden tradition inside the Craft. A London Masonic
Rosicrucian society founded in 1866, whose members had access to a second-hand book trade
and a great deal of leisure.

---

### The shape of the ecosystem

Set it out and the structure is clear.

**Mesmerism** supplies the theory of subtle influence and the technique of trance.
**Spiritualism** supplies the mass audience and the experiential expectation.
**The publishing and antiquarian trade** supplies the primary texts.
**Lévi** supplies the synthesis — a coherent doctrine linking will, imagination,
correspondence, Kabbalah and tarot.
**Freemasonry** supplies the organizational form: grades, initiation, obligation, officers,
temple, secrecy.

Five inputs. And the thing assembled from them — the Victorian magical order — is the
direct ancestor of everything in Western esotericism today.

Note what is *not* in the list. There is no ancient lineage. There is no unbroken
transmission. There is no surviving secret society from an earlier age.

Every component is identifiable, datable and modern, except the printed texts — and those
were bought in shops.

That is the occult revival, and it is one of the most impressive acts of collective
creative synthesis in modern European cultural history. It deserves to be admired for what
it was, rather than for what its makers claimed it was.

Two of them, though, would spend a great deal of effort on the claim. And one of them, in
Charleston, South Carolina, had already put Lévi's system into a Masonic book without
mentioning where he got it.
