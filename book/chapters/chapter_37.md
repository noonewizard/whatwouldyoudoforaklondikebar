# CHAPTER THIRTY-SEVEN
## Pike and the Architecture of Morals

He was a very large man — six feet tall and heavy, with hair to his shoulders and a beard
to his chest, in an era when that was a statement. He had been a schoolteacher in
Massachusetts, a wanderer in the Southwest, a newspaper editor in Arkansas, a lawyer who
argued Indian claims before the Supreme Court, an officer in two wars, and a poet of some
local reputation.

He read Latin, Greek, Hebrew, French, Spanish, and taught himself Sanskrit in his fifties
in order to read the Vedas. His personal library ran to thousands of volumes and he had
read them. He kept a commonplace book. He wrote, over a long life, an enormous quantity of
material, most of it about symbols.

He also commanded a brigade of Confederate Indian troops at the battle of Pea Ridge in
March 1862, after which he was accused — by his own side as well as the other — of failing
to control them and of atrocities committed against Union wounded. He resigned his
commission in bitterness, quarrelled with the Confederate authorities, was briefly arrested,
and spent the rest of the war in retreat. He was indicted for treason after it and
pardoned.[^1]

Albert Pike is not a comfortable figure and I am not going to make him one. But he is the
most important single author in the history of American Freemasonry, and what he actually
wrote is almost entirely unknown to the people who argue about him.

---

### The book

Pike became Sovereign Grand Commander of the Ancient and Accepted Scottish Rite, Southern
Jurisdiction, in 1859, and held the office until his death in 1891. He found a degree system
that was, by his own assessment, in poor condition — a set of rituals of French origin,
variously transmitted, inconsistently worked, with thin content.

He rewrote them. And in 1871 he published a commentary on them: *Morals and Dogma of the
Ancient and Accepted Scottish Rite of Freemasonry*, about eight hundred and sixty pages,
one chapter for each of the thirty-two degrees.

For the next century it was given to every initiate in the Southern Jurisdiction. Most, by
universal report, did not read it. It is long, it is dense, it has no index in the original,
and its method is not obvious.

---

### What is actually in it

*Morals and Dogma* is a work of comparative mythology and moral philosophy organized around
Masonic symbols. Its method is the concordance of Chapter 8 — Pico's method, four hundred
years on.

For any given degree, Pike will take its symbol and pursue it across traditions: through
Egyptian religion, Zoroastrianism, the Vedas, Greek philosophy, the Hebrew scriptures,
Gnosticism, Kabbalah, Christianity, Islam, alchemy and Hermeticism, assembling parallels
and drawing a moral conclusion. The learning is genuine and the range is enormous. So is
the credulity: Pike's sources for ancient religion are the comparative mythology of the
mid-nineteenth century, which was frequently wrong, and he had no way to know it.

The conclusions are almost invariably moral and almost invariably unsurprising: the initiate
should pursue truth, resist tyranny, exercise charity, subdue his passions, tolerate the
beliefs of others, and work for the improvement of mankind. Pike was a nineteenth-century
liberal moralist with an immense library, and the book's destination is nearly always a
Victorian virtue.

He also said, in the preface, something that almost nobody quotes: that the work was
prepared for the use of the Rite, that everyone is free to reject any of its opinions, and
that he did not intend to impose them.[^2]

That disclaimer is not decoration. It is a direct consequence of Anderson's formula of
1723. Pike could not promulgate doctrine — no Masonic body can — so he offered
interpretation and expressly disclaimed authority for it.

His readers, and still more his enemies, ignored the disclaimer entirely.

---

### The sources

Now the thing that changes everything, and it is recent.

For a century it was possible to read *Morals and Dogma* as Pike's own synthesis. It is
written in a single voice. It quotes sparingly. It does not, in the original edition,
identify most of its sources.

In 2011 the Scottish Rite published an annotated edition prepared by Arturo de Hoyos, the
Rite's Grand Archivist, containing several thousand critical notes. De Hoyos had gone
through the book against Pike's library and identified, line by line, where the material
came from.[^3]

A great deal of it is unattributed transcription.

Pike took, often verbatim or nearly so, from a range of nineteenth-century authors. And the
single largest identified source is **Éliphas Lévi**.

The astral light is in Pike because it is in Lévi. The Kabbalistic material, the tarot
associations, the pentagram, the interpretation of the pillars as Severity and Mercy, the
discussion of the Sephiroth, substantial passages of comparative religion — these are
Lévi's, published in Paris between 1854 and 1861, appearing in Charleston in 1871 without
a name attached.

---

### What this means

It means that the esoteric content of American Scottish Rite Masonry — which is to say, the
material that generations of American Masons and their critics have taken to be the
fraternity's ancient inner teaching — was written by a French ex-socialist fifteen years
before it appeared in the Masonic book.

Let me put the chronology in a single line, because it is the spine of this book's
argument.

**1854–56:** Lévi publishes *Dogme et rituel* in Paris.
**1871:** Pike publishes *Morals and Dogma* in Charleston, incorporating Lévi extensively
and without attribution.
**1871–1974:** Every initiate of the Southern Jurisdiction receives a copy.
**Thereafter:** Kabbalah, the astral light, Hermeticism and the pentagram are widely
understood — by Masons and anti-Masons alike — to be ancient Masonic doctrine.

The material entered the tradition within living memory of the men who first read it. It
arrived in a book that its own author described as a collection of opinions the reader was
free to reject.

---

### Was it plagiarism?

The question is anachronistic but worth answering.

Nineteenth-century practice on attribution was looser than ours, especially in works of
popular synthesis, and Pike was compiling rather than claiming originality. He did
acknowledge a general debt to earlier writers in places. He was not passing off a novel
theory as his own; he was assembling a commentary.

Against that: the scale is very large, the borrowing is often close, and the effect —
whether intended or not — was that thousands of readers took Lévi's nineteenth-century
synthesis for the ancient wisdom of the Craft.

De Hoyos's own judgment, from inside the institution, is worth noting: he published the
findings. The Scottish Rite's Grand Archivist produced the edition that demonstrates the
extent of its most famous author's unacknowledged borrowing, and the Rite printed it.

That is what intellectual honesty looks like in an institution, and it is worth recording
in a chapter that will otherwise be quoted by people with other purposes.

---

### Pike's actual project

What was he trying to do?

I think the answer is visible in the book's structure and in his career, and it is neither
sinister nor mystical.

Pike inherited a degree system with thin content, in a country whose Masonry had been
nearly destroyed by the Morgan affair forty years earlier and had rebuilt itself on
respectability. He wanted to give it intellectual weight. He believed — with the whole
*prisca theologia* tradition behind him — that the world's religions descended from a
common primitive truth, that Masonry's symbols were fragments of it, and that a man who
studied them across traditions would arrive at a universal and tolerant morality.

That is a nineteenth-century liberal Protestant-deist project dressed in comparative
mythology. It is the same project as Anderson's religion in which all men agree, pursued
with a much larger library.

And it failed in a specific and instructive way. Pike wanted to fill the Craft's vacancy
with content. But he had no authority to make content binding — the institution's own
constitution forbade it — so what he produced was a vast, learned, unauthorised
interpretation that sat inside the tradition without ever being the tradition.

Which meant that it could be quoted by anybody, for any purpose, as though it were.

Within twenty-three years of its publication, a French journalist would print a letter
Pike never wrote, attribute to him a doctrine he never held, and use *Morals and Dogma* as
corroboration.

The passages that made it credible were about a figure Pike did discuss, at length, in
terms that are strange, genuine, and not at all what the accusation says.
