# CHAPTER THIRTY-EIGHT
## Lucifer and Baphomet

Two words, taken out of two paragraphs, have done more damage to Freemasonry's reputation
than three centuries of anything else.

This chapter reads the paragraphs.

---

### The Latin word

*Lucifer* is Latin and it means light-bearer. In classical usage it is the name of the
morning star, the planet Venus seen before dawn. It has no diabolical content whatever.

It acquires one by a specific route. In Isaiah 14, the prophet mocks the fallen king of
Babylon: *How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning* — in the
Authorised Version's rendering of the Vulgate's rendering of a Hebrew phrase meaning
shining one, or day-star. The passage is about a human king. Early Christian commentators,
reading it alongside a verse in Luke about Satan falling like lightning, applied it to the
devil, and the identification stuck so thoroughly that in English *Lucifer* became simply a
name for Satan.

So the word carries two meanings in the nineteenth century, and educated writers used both.
When a classically trained author writes *Lucifer*, he may mean the devil, or he may mean
the morning star, or he may be deliberately playing on the distance between them.

Pike was a classically trained author.

---

### The passage

The notorious passage occurs in the chapter on the nineteenth degree. Its subject is the
symbolism of light, and it is written in Pike's comparative-mythological manner, discussing
how ancient systems used the morning star and how the meaning of names has shifted.

Its substance — I paraphrase deliberately, and will explain why — is that Lucifer, the
light-bearer, is a strange and mysterious name to give to the spirit of darkness; that it is
the morning star, the bearer of light, whose brightness dazzles feeble souls; and that the
reader should not doubt it, since traditions are full of divine revelations and
inspirations, and inspiration is of no one age and no one creed.

I have paraphrased rather than quoted for a specific reason, and the reader should know
it. This passage exists in the wild in a dozen mutilated forms, some of them produced by
splicing Pike's sentences with sentences from the Taxil forgery. Quoting it accurately
requires the 1871 edition or de Hoyos's annotated text, and the wording should be verified
against those before it appears in print in a book of this kind. I have marked it
accordingly in the notes.[^1]

What can be said with confidence about its content is this.

It is a passage about **the history of a name**. Pike's point is that a word meaning
light-bearer came to designate the spirit of darkness, and that this is odd. He is doing
philology and comparative religion, in a chapter about the symbolism of light, in a book
whose method is precisely the tracing of symbols across traditions.

It is not devotional. Nothing in it asks the reader to venerate anything. Read in its
paragraph and its chapter, it is a scholarly observation of exactly the kind Pike makes
several hundred times in that book about several hundred other symbols.

And its source is substantially Lévi, who had discussed the same material in the 1850s.

---

### Why it was vulnerable

That said, I want to be fair to the passage's critics, because a purely defensive reading
misses something real.

Pike wrote a passage using the word *Lucifer* in a positive register, in a book given to
tens of thousands of Masons, in a nineteenth-century Christian society, without apparently
anticipating how it would look if extracted.

That was a serious misjudgement of his audience, and it was not the only one. The same
chapter and its neighbours contain a great deal of material about dualism, about the
Gnostics, about the equal antiquity of light and darkness, and about the limitations of
dogmatic religion, all written in a tone of Olympian comparison that assumes a reader with
Pike's own detachment.

Most readers did not have it. And the passage was extracted, within twenty-three years, by
somebody who knew exactly what he was doing.

---

### Baphomet: the name

The second word has a stranger history and it is worth doing properly, because it is a
model case of the book's method.

**Stage one, 1307–1312.** In the interrogations of the Knights Templar, conducted under
torture by French royal officials, some Templars confessed to venerating an idol or a head.
The descriptions are wildly inconsistent — a bearded head, a cat, a figure with multiple
faces, a skull. The name recorded in some depositions is *Baphomet*, or variants of it.

The most plausible scholarly explanation of the name is that it is a corruption of
*Mahomet*, Muhammad — a standard medieval slander in which any heresy could be described as
Islamic. It appears in Provençal sources in exactly that sense.

Whether the Templars venerated anything at all is doubtful; confessions under torture
establish what the torturers wanted. No idol was ever produced.

**Stage two, the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries.** The name survives in
antiquarian discussion of the Templar trials, and is picked up by writers seeking to prove
that the Templars were Gnostics, or Ophites, or the survivors of some mystery tradition.
Joseph von Hammer-Purgstall's writings in the 1810s are the most influential of these, and
were contested at the time.

**Stage three, 1856.** Éliphas Lévi draws his goat-headed figure and captions it the
Sabbatic Goat, the Baphomet of Mendes. It is his own composition, assembled from the
Templar name, a passage in Herodotus about goat-worship at Mendes in Egypt, the iconography
of the witches' sabbath, the alchemical formula *solve et coagula*, and a doctrine of
equilibrium. It is a pantacle summarising his system.

**Stage four, 1871.** Pike discusses Baphomet in the Templar material of the Scottish Rite
degrees, reproducing much of the nineteenth-century antiquarian discussion, including Lévi's.

**Stage five, 1894.** Someone puts stages one, three and four together and tells the world
that Freemasons worship the goat-headed figure.

**Stage six, the twentieth century onward.** Lévi's drawing becomes the standard visual
representation of Satan in Western popular culture, appearing in films, on records, and
eventually as a bronze statue erected by a modern religious organisation making a First
Amendment point.

---

### The lesson of the chain

Look at the sequence again.

A name extracted under torture in 1307, probably a corruption of Muhammad, attached to an
idol that was never produced. Antiquarian speculation five centuries later. A drawing by a
French occultist in 1856 illustrating a doctrine of balance. A comparative discussion by an
American Freemason in 1871. A forged accusation in 1894.

At no point does anyone worship anything.

And yet the modern popular understanding — that Baphomet is an ancient demon venerated by
Templars and inherited by Freemasons — has all the appearance of a continuous tradition. It
has a name that recurs across seven centuries. It has documents. It has an image.

Run the six questions on it and it dissolves at every step. The name has no established
referent. The transmission between stages is transmission of *a word and a picture*, not of
a practice. The image is 1856. The accusation is 1894 and was confessed as a fabrication
three years later.

This is what a manufactured tradition looks like from inside. Not a lie told once, but a
series of people each handing on something slightly transformed, most of them acting in good
faith, until the accumulated object has a solidity that none of its components ever had.

It is the same process that produced the Templar descent in Chapter 26, the Rosicrucian
ancestry in Chapter 12, and the Egyptian origins in Chapter 6. And it is the process that
produced the single most successful piece of anti-Masonic propaganda ever written, which
was published in Paris in the 1890s by a man who had already confessed to inventing
everything he wrote, and which is still believed.
