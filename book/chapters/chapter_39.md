# CHAPTER THIRTY-NINE
## Taxil's Devil

On 19 April 1897, in a hall in Paris, a crowd assembled to meet a woman who did not exist.

For eleven years, French Catholic opinion had been following the most sensational religious
story of the age. A converted anti-Masonic writer had been publishing revelations of a
satanic cult operating at the summit of Freemasonry — a body called the Palladium, directed
from Charleston, South Carolina, by the American Sovereign Grand Commander Albert Pike, at
which demons were invoked and the Eucharist desecrated. His principal witness was a former
high priestess of the cult named Diana Vaughan, who had converted to Catholicism, gone into
hiding for fear of Masonic assassination, and been publishing her memoirs.

She had been praised by cardinals. A book of Eucharistic devotions published under her name
had reportedly received papal commendation. An international anti-Masonic congress at
Trent in 1896 had debated her existence.

The meeting of 19 April was announced as the occasion on which she would appear.

The man who took the platform was Léo Taxil, and what he delivered instead was a
confession.[^1]

---

### The hoax

Gabriel Jogand-Pagès had been, in the 1870s and early 1880s, an aggressive anticlerical
journalist and pornographer, publishing scurrilous attacks on the Church under the name Léo
Taxil. He had been prosecuted. He had a taste for elaborate practical jokes — an earlier
one involved convincing the press that the bottom of Lake Geneva was full of sunken Roman
ships.

In 1885 he announced his conversion to Catholicism, renounced his former work, and offered
his services against the Church's enemies. He had also, briefly, been initiated into
Freemasonry and expelled.

The timing was excellent. In 1884 Leo XIII had issued *Humanum genus*, the most substantial
papal condemnation of Freemasonry, calling for the faithful to expose it. Taxil supplied
the exposure.

Over the following twelve years he produced, under his own name and several pseudonyms, a
library of anti-Masonic revelation: *Les Mystères de la franc-maçonnerie*, *Les Sœurs
maçonnes*, and — through a collaborator writing as Dr Bataille — an enormous serial called
*Le Diable au XIXe siècle*, full of demons, electrical devices, crocodile-riding and
mechanical marvels, which is very obviously a joke to a modern reader and was not taken as
one.

Then Diana Vaughan. She was, on the evidence of the confession, a real person — an employee
of an American typewriter company with whom Taxil was in contact — whose name was used for
a fictional character. She produced memoirs, devotional works, and letters. She never
appeared in public.

---

### The Pike letter

The element that outlived everything else was a document.

In 1894, Abel Clarin de la Rive published *La Femme et l'enfant dans la franc-maçonnerie
universelle*, drawing on the Taxil material, and printed what purported to be an extract
from instructions issued by Albert Pike to the twenty-three Supreme Councils of the world on
14 July 1889.

The extract has Pike saying that the Masonic religion should be maintained in the purity of
the Luciferian doctrine; that Lucifer is God, and unfortunately Adonay is also God; that
the true and pure philosophic religion is the belief in Lucifer, the equal of Adonay.[^2]

It is a forgery. It has no source, no original, no archival existence. Pike died in 1891 and
the Supreme Councils of the world received no such instruction. De la Rive himself, after
Taxil's confession, publicly acknowledged that he had been deceived.

And it has been quoted continuously ever since — in anti-Masonic tracts, in fundamentalist
publications, in the twentieth-century conspiracy literature, in books that sold in the
millions in the 1980s and 1990s, and now, endlessly, online. It is frequently spliced with
genuine Pike sentences to produce composite quotations that are harder to check.

---

### The confession

Taxil advertised the meeting of 19 April 1897 as the unveiling of Diana Vaughan. Journalists
came. Clergy came. Freemasons came, curious. He had arranged for a stenographer.

He announced that there was no Diana Vaughan of the kind described, no Palladium, no
satanic Masonry, and no Luciferian doctrine. He had invented all of it. He had done it, he
said, to make fools of his opponents and to demonstrate the credulity of the clergy who had
believed him, and he thanked them for their assistance. He had, he noted, been received and
praised at the highest levels on the strength of documents he had written himself.

The confession was printed in full in *Le Frondeur* on 25 April 1897 under the title
"Twelve Years under the Banner of the Church."[^3]

Accounts of the meeting describe uproar. Taxil is said to have left under police protection.

---

### Why it did not die

Here is the part that matters for this book, and it is the reason the Taxil affair earns a
chapter rather than a footnote.

**The confession did not kill the hoax.**

The Palladium continued to be cited. The Pike letter continued to be printed. Anti-Masonic
writers in the twentieth century reproduced the material, sometimes acknowledging Taxil's
confession and arguing that the confession was itself the lie, sometimes not mentioning it
at all. The material passed into English-language fundamentalist publishing in the
mid-twentieth century and from there into the general conspiracy literature, where it
remains.

A fabrication, publicly confessed by its author in front of the press, survived its own
confession by more than a hundred and twenty years.

Why?

**Because the confession is harder to transmit than the claim.** A claim about devil-worship
is memorable, transmissible in one sentence, and emotionally charged. Its refutation
requires context, dates and a name that means nothing to most readers. In any competition
between the two, the claim wins on structure alone.

**Because the claim served needs that the refutation did not.** People who wanted
Freemasonry to be satanic in 1897 still wanted it in 1930 and 1980. The evidence was never
what was doing the work.

**Because corroboration was available, of a sort.** Pike had written about Lucifer. The
passage existed. A reader who checked found something that looked like confirmation, and
the distance between Pike's philological aside and Taxil's invented doctrine required more
attention than most readers gave it. This is the most instructive feature of the whole
affair: **the forgery worked because there was a real text near it.** Fabrications attach
themselves to genuine material, and the genuine material lends them weight.

**And because it entered the record.** Once a quotation is printed, it can be cited. Once it
is cited, the citation can be cited. Within a few iterations the original is unreachable and
the claim is supported by a wall of references, all of which lead back to a book by a man
who confessed he made it up.

That is circular sourcing, and Taxil is the purest specimen of it in the history of this
subject.

---

### What the affair actually demonstrates

Three things, and none of them is about Freemasonry.

**One: about the Church.** Taxil's real target was clerical credulity, and he hit it. The
willingness of substantial parts of French Catholic opinion to accept, without verification,
an increasingly baroque account of satanic ritual from a recent convert with a history of
fraud is the actual scandal, and the Church's more careful minds said so at the time — there
were sceptics throughout, and some of them were vocal.

**Two: about the anti-Masonic tradition.** The most famous evidence in the entire history of
anti-Masonry is a confessed fabrication. Any body of argument whose central document is
Taxil's has a problem that no amount of subsequent material repairs.

**Three: about how false history propagates.** This is the general lesson and it is why the
chapter sits where it does. The Taxil affair is a controlled experiment in the manufacture
of belief, conducted in public, with a full confession at the end — and it still shows a
positive result a century later.

Everything in Chapter 50 about the Illuminati, the Priory of Sion and the rest follows the
same mechanics. Taxil is simply the case where we have the confession.

---

### A closing note on Pike

Albert Pike died on 2 April 1891, in Washington, three years before de la Rive printed the
forged letter and six years before Taxil confessed.

He never saw the accusation. He had no opportunity to answer it. And his reputation in the
general public, for more than a century since, has consisted almost entirely of a doctrine
he did not hold, attributed to him by a man who admitted inventing it, quoting a letter that
does not exist.

He was a difficult and in some respects an unattractive man, and the worst things that can
truthfully be said about him concern a battlefield in Arkansas in 1862, not a devil.

The second most famous thing about him is that he plagiarised Éliphas Lévi.

The most famous thing about him is not true.
