# CHAPTER FORTY
## Theosophy and the Co-Masons

In November 1875, in New York, a small group founded a society for the study of unexplained
laws of nature and the powers latent in man. Its principal founders were a retired Union
army colonel and lawyer named Henry Steel Olcott, an Irish-American lawyer named William
Quan Judge, and a Russian émigrée of formidable presence and uncertain biography named
Helena Petrovna Blavatsky.

Within twenty years the Theosophical Society had branches on every continent, had relocated
its headquarters to India, had produced two enormous and enormously influential books, had
contributed materially to Indian and Sinhalese nationalist revival, and had become the
single most important channel by which Asian religious ideas entered Western popular
culture.

It had also acquired a relationship with Freemasonry that is more consequential, and much
less discussed, than anything in the Golden Dawn.

---

### Blavatsky's project

*Isis Unveiled* appeared in 1877 in two volumes; *The Secret Doctrine* in 1888, in two
more. Their argument, stated at its simplest, is the *prisca theologia* of Chapter 6 on a
global scale.

There exists, Blavatsky maintained, a single ancient wisdom-religion, of immense antiquity,
from which all the world's religions and philosophies descend as fragments and corruptions.
It has been preserved by a brotherhood of adepts — the Masters, or Mahatmas — who live in
seclusion, principally in Tibet, and who direct the spiritual evolution of humanity from
behind the scenes. She was, she said, their agent, and portions of her books were dictated
or impressed by them.

The structure will be familiar by now. It is the Rosicrucian template of Chapter 12 —
hidden superiors, ancient wisdom, a coming transformation — scaled up and relocated from
Germany to the Himalayas.

The scholarly assessment of her sources has been carried out and is not in serious dispute.
The Asian material derives substantially from the Orientalist scholarship available in her
day, and her presentation of Buddhism and Hinduism is filtered through Victorian
interpretation to a degree that modern scholars of those traditions find striking. The
Western esoteric material comes from the nineteenth-century occult library: Lévi
prominently, along with the standard Hermetic and Kabbalistic sources.[^1]

Some of the Mahatma letters were investigated by the Society for Psychical Research in 1885,
which concluded they were produced by Blavatsky — a finding the SPR substantially revisited
a century later, in a report noting the deficiencies of the original investigation. The
question of what Blavatsky was doing, and how much she believed, remains genuinely open and
I do not propose to settle it here.

---

### What Theosophy took from Masonry

Blavatsky wrote about Freemasonry frequently, and her position was consistent and
characteristic: that the Craft preserved genuine fragments of the ancient wisdom in its
symbols, that its members had almost entirely forgotten what they meant, and that Theosophy
could supply the interpretation.

That formula — *you have the symbols, we have the meaning* — is exactly the vacancy of
Chapter 16 being filled from outside, and it is the same move that Pike had made in
Charleston four years before *Isis Unveiled*.

But Theosophy went further than commentary. It acquired Masonic ritual, and the route by
which it did so is the interesting part.

---

### Co-Masonry

Recall Chapter 29. In 1882 Maria Deraismes was initiated in a French lodge; in 1893, with
Georges Martin, she founded *Le Droit Humain*, which admitted men and women equally in the
same lodges, working a full Masonic ritual.

In 1902 Co-Masonry came to England, and its principal promoter was **Annie Besant**.

Besant was already one of the most remarkable public figures of her generation: a former
clergyman's wife who had lost her faith, a secularist lecturer, a birth-control campaigner
prosecuted for obscenity, a Fabian socialist, the organizer of the 1888 London matchgirls'
strike, and — after reading *The Secret Doctrine* for review — a Theosophist. She became
president of the Theosophical Society in 1907 and, in the following decades, a significant
figure in Indian politics, president of the Indian National Congress in 1917.

Besant took Co-Masonry and made it an instrument. Lodges were established across Britain,
India, Australia, America and elsewhere, frequently in association with Theosophical
branches, and a body of theosophical interpretation was supplied to the ritual — most
influentially by Charles Webster Leadbeater, whose books on Masonic symbolism read the
degrees in terms of chakras, subtle bodies, Masters and rays.[^2]

---

### The point that gets missed

Now the observation this chapter exists for, and it is one that neither Masonic nor occult
historiography likes.

**The most successful transmission of Masonic ritual into the twentieth-century esoteric
world did not run through regular male Freemasonry. It ran through women's and mixed
Masonry, which regular Freemasonry did not recognize.**

Consider what Co-Masonry achieved. It carried full Masonic ritual — not a derivative, not an
imitation, but the working degrees — into an international spiritual movement with tens of
thousands of members on five continents, with a developed esoteric interpretation attached,
during exactly the decades when Western esotericism was taking its modern shape.

Nothing the regular Craft did in the same period had a comparable effect on the esoteric
world, for the straightforward reason that the regular Craft was not trying to have one.

And the irony is precise. The bodies excluded from Masonic history as irregular are the ones
that did the most to spread Masonic ritual beyond the fraternity. The exclusion did not
prevent the transmission. It merely ensured that the transmission happened somewhere the
Craft could not influence it.

---

### Krishnamurti

One episode deserves recording, because it is the best illustration in this book of what
happens when an esoteric organization's own machinery is turned against it.

In 1909 Leadbeater identified a fourteen-year-old Indian boy, Jiddu Krishnamurti, as the
vehicle for a coming World Teacher. The Theosophical Society organized around this: an
Order of the Star in the East was founded, with tens of thousands of members, property, and
an apparatus of expectation. Krishnamurti was educated in England, groomed for the role, and
presented internationally.

In 1929, at a gathering in the Netherlands, he dissolved the Order and resigned from it. His
statement is one of the more remarkable documents of twentieth-century religion. Truth, he
said, is a pathless land, and cannot be approached by any path whatsoever, by any religion,
by any sect. He did not want followers. Organizations, he argued, become crutches, and the
moment you organize a truth you kill it.[^3]

He spent the following sixty years lecturing, to large audiences, against the idea that
anyone should listen to a teacher.

I include this because it is the only case in this book of a person at the center of an
initiatory system looking at the apparatus and declining it on principle — and because his
objection is, in essence, the objection that Saint-Martin had reached in the 1780s and that
Waite would reach in the 1900s, stated more radically than either managed.

---

### Theosophy's legacy

Whatever one concludes about Blavatsky, the Society's effects are enormous and mostly
unacknowledged.

It introduced karma, reincarnation, and a vocabulary of spiritual evolution into Western
popular culture on a scale nothing else approached. It contributed to the revival of
Buddhism in Ceylon and to Indian cultural nationalism, in ways Indian and Sri Lankan
historians have documented. It shaped modern astrology, the New Age movement, and a great
deal of twentieth-century alternative spirituality. Through Rudolf Steiner, who broke from
it, it produced anthroposophy and Waldorf education. Through Besant, it carried Masonic
ritual around the world.

And it did all of this on the strength of a claim — an ancient brotherhood of Masters
directing human evolution from Tibet — for which there has never been a scrap of evidence of
the kind Chapter 5 requires.

Which is, by now, the most familiar finding in this book. The claim is unsupported. The
consequences are real, large, and still with us.

The Masters are the Unknown Superiors again. Everybody in this story wants somebody above
them, and when nobody is there, they are supplied.

Nowhere more elaborately than in a London flat in 1887, where a coroner produced
correspondence with a German adept who did not exist, and founded the most influential
magical order in history on it.
