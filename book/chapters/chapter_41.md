# CHAPTER FORTY-ONE
## The Golden Dawn

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn existed, in its original form, for about twelve
years. At its peak it had perhaps three hundred members. It fell apart in a series of
quarrels so undignified that one of them ended in a London police court.

It is the most influential magical organization in history, and essentially everything in
modern Western ceremonial magic descends from it.

---

### The founding

The story the Order told about itself ran as follows.

In 1887 a manuscript came into the hands of William Wynn Westcott, a London coroner, Master
Mason, and Secretary General of the Societas Rosicruciana in Anglia. It was written in
cipher — a substitution alphabet taken from a sixteenth-century work by the abbot
Trithemius — and, decoded, contained the outlines of five initiation rituals and a body of
Kabbalistic and symbolic material.

Among the pages was the address of a German adept, Fräulein Anna Sprengel of Nuremberg.
Westcott wrote to her. She replied, granting him authority to found a temple in England, and
conferring honorary grades on him and on his colleagues. In 1888 the Isis-Urania Temple of
the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn was constituted in London, with three Chiefs: Westcott;
Samuel Liddell Mathers, a translator and ritualist of unstable temperament and immense
industry; and William Robert Woodman, physician and Supreme Magus of the SRIA.

In 1890 Fräulein Sprengel died, and the German connection lapsed.[^1]

---

### The warrant

Ellic Howe's documentary study of the Order, published in 1972, examined the Sprengel
correspondence and concluded that it is spurious.

The grounds are straightforward. The German is defective in ways characteristic of an
English speaker writing German rather than of a German writing German. The addresses do not
check out. No such person has been traced. No German order of the kind described has been
identified. And the correspondence conveniently terminated, by death, at the point at which
continued contact would have become difficult to sustain.

Westcott appears to have written the letters himself.[^2]

The Cipher Manuscripts are a harder question. They are real documents; their contents are
substantial; and their origin is genuinely obscure. Proposals have included Kenneth
Mackenzie — a Masonic and occult writer of the previous generation, connected to Lévi, who
died in 1886 — as the compiler, and Frederick Hockley's circle as the milieu. The material
in them is drawn from identifiable printed sources: Agrippa, the Kabbalistic literature,
Lévi, the Masonic and Rosicrucian grade systems of the SRIA, and the tarot.

Alison Butler's contribution was to stop asking where the manuscripts came from and start
asking what the founders had available. Her reconstruction of Hockley's collection and of
Westcott's Hermetic library — two hundred and eighty-six items, weighted toward
seventeenth- and eighteenth-century cabalistic, Rosicrucian, Hermetic and Neoplatonic
sources — shows that the material in the Ciphers was, in its entirety, within reach of the
men who produced them.[^3]

**No German order is required to explain the Golden Dawn. A good library and considerable
talent will do it.**

---

### The audit

So: what did the Golden Dawn take from Freemasonry?

This is the question the whole book has been traveling toward, and it deserves a proper
answer rather than a slogan. Here it is, item by item.

**Borrowed from Masonry.**

*The graded initiatory structure.* A series of degrees, conferred ceremonially, each
admitting to further teaching, with advancement controlled by superiors. The specific
sequence — Neophyte, Zelator, Theoricus, Practicus, Philosophus, then the Second Order —
comes via the SRIA from the Gold- und Rosenkreuz of Chapter 28, which is itself a Masonic-
requiring body.

*The temple.* A room arranged with officers at stations, an altar at the center, cardinal
orientation, and movement between points. Anyone who has read Chapter 17 will recognize the
blocking immediately.

*The officers.* A presiding chief, subordinate officers with defined stations and functions,
a guardian of the door. The Masonic pattern exactly.

*The obligation.* A sworn undertaking, taken kneeling at the altar, with penalties.

*Signs, grips, passwords and the examination of visitors.*

*Secrecy and staged revelation*, including the practice of withholding material until the
appropriate grade.

*Regalia*, sashes and collars, worn to indicate rank.

**Parallel and adapted.**

*The Vault of the Adepti.* The Second Order's central ceremony, the Adeptus Minor grade,
takes place in a seven-sided vault containing a pastos with the body of the founder — which
is, as Chapter 12 showed, a direct construction from the *Fama Fraternitatis* of 1614. The
*form* of a ritual drama in which the candidate encounters a dead founder is Masonic; the
*content* is Rosicrucian and taken from a printed pamphlet.

**New, and not Masonic at all.**

*The Kabbalistic grade structure.* Each grade is assigned to a Sephirah on the Tree of
Life. There is nothing of this in Masonry.

*The system of correspondences.* Elements, planets, zodiacal signs, Hebrew letters, tarot
trumps, colors, gods, plants and perfumes, all mapped onto each other and onto the Tree.
This is the Agrippan tradition of Chapter 9 fused with Lévi's tarot-Kabbalah equation of
Chapter 35 — and it is the Order's greatest single intellectual achievement.

*The practical work.* Ritual invocation and banishing; the Lesser and Greater Rituals of the
Pentagram and Hexagram; scrying; astral travel; talismanic magic; alchemy; geomancy;
divination. **None of this has any Masonic precedent whatever.** The Craft has no operative
magic; the Golden Dawn is almost entirely operative magic.

*The Enochian system.* The angelic language and tables recorded by John Dee and Edward
Kelley in the 1580s, which survived in manuscript in the British Museum and were
elaborated by the Order into a complete magical system. Egil Asprem's study traces the route
precisely — from Dee's papers to the reading room to the Order's curriculum.[^4]

*The Egyptian dress.* God-forms, god-names, the imagery of the Book of the Dead. This comes
from nineteenth-century Egyptology — real Egyptology, newly available, which the Order's
members read with enthusiasm.

*The admission of women on equal terms.* From the beginning, and pointedly. Constance Wilde,
Maud Gonne, Florence Farr, Annie Horniman and Moina Bergson Mathers were members; Farr ran
the London temple.

---

### The formulation

Stated as compactly as I can manage:

**The Golden Dawn is a Masonic machine running non-Masonic software.**

Every structural component — the grades, the temple, the officers, the oath, the staged
revelation, the regalia — is Masonic in derivation, arriving through the SRIA and the
German Rosicrucian tradition. Every operative component — the Kabbalah, the
correspondences, the invocations, the Enochian work, the Egyptian god-forms — comes from
printed books in a private library.

The founders did not find magic hidden in Freemasonry. They took the Masonic apparatus,
which they knew from the inside, because it was the best available technology for
organizing graded initiatory instruction — and they loaded it with material they had
assembled themselves from the antiquarian book trade.

That is the carriage thesis, in its most important single instance, with the components
itemised.

---

### The collapse

The Order fell apart between 1900 and 1903, and the manner of it matters.

Westcott withdrew in 1897 after his occult activities became known to his official
superiors. Mathers, increasingly autocratic and living in Paris, claimed authority from the
Secret Chiefs — invisible superiors with whom he alone was in contact, who authorised his
decisions and could not be consulted.

The London members asked for evidence. Mathers, cornered, told them that the Sprengel
letters had been forged by Westcott.

That admission — from the man with the most to lose by it — is the strongest evidence for
the forgery, and it destroyed the Order's warrant at a stroke. If the German authority was
fabricated, then the Order's claim to Rosicrucian descent rested on nothing, and the Chiefs'
authority rested on nothing.

What followed was a revolt, a schism, a series of splinter orders, and — in April 1900 — an
episode in which a young member named Aleister Crowley attempted to seize the London temple
premises on Mathers's behalf, reportedly in Highland dress and a mask, and the matter ended
in court.

The Order broke into fragments: the Stella Matutina, the Alpha et Omega, A. E. Waite's
Independent and Rectified Rite, and others.

And the mechanism of its destruction is the same mechanism that destroyed the Rite of Strict
Observance a hundred and twenty years earlier. **An organization whose authority rests on
superiors who cannot be produced will eventually be asked to produce them.** Von Hund could
not. Westcott could not. Mathers could not. In each case the demand came from within, and in
each case the organization did not survive it.

---

### Why it mattered anyway

And here is the thing that ought to be surprising and is not, by this point in the book.

The Golden Dawn's foundation was fraudulent, its leadership quarrelsome, its lifespan
twelve years and its membership tiny.

Its *system* is the basis of modern Western magic.

Israel Regardie published the rituals in four volumes between 1937 and 1940, on the
explicit grounds that the material was too valuable to be lost with the dying orders that
held it. Crowley had already published a great deal of it in *The Equinox* from 1909.
Everything that followed — Thelema, Wicca, the whole twentieth-century magical revival, the
contents of any occult bookshop today — descends from those publications.

A tradition can be enormously productive and have a fake pedigree. Those are independent
facts. The Golden Dawn's system works, in the sense that people have used it for a hundred
and thirty years and report that it does something, and none of that depends on Fräulein
Sprengel having existed.

Which raises a question the last part of this book has to answer: if the pedigree does not
matter, why did they all insist on one?

Before that, though, there is a component of the Golden Dawn's system that needs a chapter
of its own — because it is the single most successful piece of invented tradition in modern
esotericism, and its inventors were quite open about assembling it, and within a generation
everyone had forgotten.
