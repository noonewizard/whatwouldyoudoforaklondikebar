# CHAPTER FORTY-THREE
## Crowley

He was born in 1875 into a family of Plymouth Brethren — a small, rigorous, biblically
literalist sect — in Leamington Spa. His father was a preacher and a former brewer, and by
all accounts the boy adored him. His father died when he was eleven. His mother, dealing
with an increasingly difficult son, reportedly called him the Beast of Revelation.

He took the name and kept it for sixty years.

Edward Alexander Crowley — Aleister, later, because he liked the rhythm — inherited money,
went to Cambridge, climbed mountains at a genuinely world-class level, wrote a great deal of
poetry of which some is good, and joined the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn in November
1898.

He is the terminus of the line this book has been tracing, and the reason to include him is
not sensation. It is that he is the first person in this history to state plainly what the
machinery was for.

---

### What he inherited

Crowley's Golden Dawn membership lasted about eighteen months before the Order tore itself
apart, and he was in the middle of the tearing — Mathers's agent in the London affair of
April 1900 described in Chapter 41.

What he took from it was the entire system: the grade structure on the Tree of Life, the
correspondences, the pentagram and hexagram rituals, the Enochian material, the tarot
attributions, the scrying, the Egyptian god-forms. He spent the rest of his life
elaborating and publishing it.

Two acts of publication matter most. In *The Equinox*, from 1909, he printed Golden Dawn
ritual material that the surviving fragments of the Order regarded as secret — an act
Mathers tried and failed to stop by injunction. And in *Liber 777*, also 1909, he published
the tables of correspondence in extended form, adding Eastern material to the Order's
Western scheme.

Those publications are the reason the Golden Dawn system survived. Regardie's four volumes
of the 1930s completed the job, but Crowley opened it, and he did so on the explicit ground
that a secret which dies with an order is worth nothing.

---

### A∴A∴ and the problem of authority

In 1907 he founded his own order, the A∴A∴, with George Cecil Jones. Its structure is the
Golden Dawn's, rationalized: a sequence of grades ascending the Tree, with a curriculum, a
reading list, and — the significant innovation — a rule that each member knows only the
person who initiated him and the people he initiates.

That rule is a direct response to the failure of Chapter 41. If there is no collective
body, there is no collective revolt. If nobody knows who the Chiefs are, nobody can demand
that they be produced.

It is an elegant solution to a structural problem, and it is worth noting that Crowley,
having watched the Golden Dawn destroy itself over the authority question, designed his own
order specifically so that it could not happen the same way.

His own answer to the authority question was different again, and we will come to it.

---

### The O.T.O.

In 1912 Crowley was contacted by Theodor Reuss.

Reuss was a German with a long involvement in fringe-Masonic bodies: rites of Memphis and
Misraim, Scottish Rite bodies of irregular provenance, and a network of high-degree
patents that circulated in the murkier reaches of continental Masonry. He had assembled
these into the **Ordo Templi Orientis**, an order with a ladder of degrees built on
Masonic models, whose higher grades taught a secret which Reuss held to be the key to all
magic: a technique of sexual magic.

Reuss's account — which Crowley enjoyed repeating — was that he confronted Crowley over a
passage in *The Book of Lies*, published that year, which he believed disclosed the order's
highest secret. Crowley claimed to have written it without knowing. Reuss made him head of
the O.T.O. for Great Britain and Ireland.[^1]

This is Crowley's Masonic connection, and it needs stating precisely because it is the
single most-abused fact in the anti-Masonic literature.

The O.T.O. is built on a Masonic degree template. Its early rituals derive from irregular
continental high-degree systems. Its terminology is Masonic. Reuss possessed patents of
various provenance for rites that most regular Grand Lodges regarded as clandestine.

The O.T.O. is not, and never was, recognized by any regular Grand Lodge. Crowley's Masonic
initiations — there were several, of different kinds, including one in Mexico in 1900 and
later contacts with Co-Masonry and with irregular bodies — did not give him a standing that
regular Freemasonry acknowledged, and he knew it. He wrote about the distinction himself,
sometimes with irritation and sometimes with amusement.[^2]

So the correct statement is: **Crowley was deeply involved with Masonic *forms*, through
irregular and co-Masonic channels, and had no recognized standing in regular
Freemasonry.**

Both halves of that sentence are load-bearing. Anti-Masonic writing drops the second half.
Masonic writing sometimes drops the first, which is equally misleading, because the O.T.O.'s
Masonic derivation is real and documented.

### Thelema

In April 1904, in a flat in Cairo, Crowley wrote out — or, on his account, took dictation
of — a short text in three chapters, delivered by a discarnate intelligence called Aiwass
over three days.

*Liber AL vel Legis*, the Book of the Law, announces a new aeon. Its central formulations
are: *Do what thou wilt shall be the whole of the Law*, and *Love is the law, love under
will*, and *Every man and every woman is a star*.

The first of these is the most misunderstood sentence in modern esotericism. It is not
permission. In Crowley's elaboration, the True Will is not what a person happens to want; it
is the deepest orientation of their nature, the thing they exist to do, and discovering it
is the work of a lifetime. Most of what people want, on this account, is precisely what
obstructs it. The formula is closer to *find your nature and follow it without deviation*
than to *do as you please*, and Crowley was explicit on the point.

This is also, structurally, his answer to the authority problem of the last chapter. He did
not claim a German adept or a Tibetan master. He claimed that the source had spoken to him
directly, that he was its prophet, and that the authority therefore rested in the book and
in his relation to it.

It is not a solution anybody else can borrow. It is, at least, not a forgery.

---

### The definition

Crowley's contribution to this book's central question is a single sentence, and it is worth
taking seriously rather than rhetorically.

He defined Magick — with the *k*, to distinguish it from conjuring — as *the Science and Art
of causing Change to occur in conformity with Will*.

And he immediately noted that this definition includes an enormous range of ordinary
activity. Writing a book is magick: the writer conceives a change and effects it by
arranging causes. So is baking a cake. He made the point deliberately, and it is the key to
what he was actually proposing.

Because notice what the definition removes. It contains no spirits. It contains no
supernatural mechanism. It contains no claim about non-physical entities, and although
Crowley worked constantly with such entities in practice, he repeatedly offered a
psychological account of them as a legitimate alternative — the spirits of the grimoires, he
suggested in one well-known passage, may be portions of the operator's own brain.[^3]

What is left, when the metaphysics is bracketed, is a technology of the will: ritual,
symbol, concentration, imagination and discipline, applied to the transformation of the
operator.

Which is precisely where this book's argument has been going since Chapter 2.

---

### The end of the line

He spent his later decades in decline: heroin addiction that began with a doctor's
prescription for asthma, bankruptcy, expulsion from Italy, the tabloid campaigns that made
him the Wickedest Man in the World, and a long tail of quarrels. He died in Hastings in
1947, in a boarding house, aged seventy-two.

He was, by most accounts including those of people who liked him, frequently cruel, a poor
custodian of the people who depended on him, and the author of a good deal of racial and
sexual invective that is genuinely unpleasant to read. I do not want to launder that.

He was also a serious scholar of the material, a ritualist of real ability, a translator, a
formidable mountaineer, and — the thing that matters here — the person who took the whole
apparatus this book has been tracing and said out loud what it was.

---

### The audit

What did Crowley take from Freemasonry?

**The O.T.O.'s degree structure**, via Reuss, via irregular continental high degrees, via
the eighteenth-century explosion of Chapter 24. Documented.

**The A∴A∴'s graded ascent**, via the Golden Dawn, via the SRIA, via the Gold- und
Rosenkreuz. Documented.

**The ritual grammar** — temple, officers, obligation, staged revelation, initiation as
transformation. Documented, and by this point in the book unremarkable.

**Nothing doctrinal.** There is not a single element of Thelemic doctrine that derives from
Masonic ritual. The Craft has no cosmology to lend.

Which is the same finding as Chapter 41, the same finding as Chapter 28, the same finding as
Chapter 27, and the same finding as Chapter 29.

**Freemasonry supplied the form. Every time. To everybody. And never the content.**

Part VI is complete. We have followed the Masonic apparatus from a Charleston degree system
to a Parisian magus to a London coroner's library to a Cairo hotel room, and at every stage
the transmission has been the same: the structure travels, and whoever receives it fills it
with what they have.

What remains is to ask why the structure works — which is the subject of Part VII — and then
to put the whole thing on trial.
