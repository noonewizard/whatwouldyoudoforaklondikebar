# PART VII — THE HIDDEN ENGINE

# CHAPTER FORTY-FOUR
## Ritual as Technology

We have followed the apparatus for three hundred years and six countries. It is time to ask
what it does.

I want to be careful about the register of this part of the book. Parts I to VI were
history: documents, dates, transmission. Part VII is analysis, and analysis of a different
kind — it uses modern theoretical frameworks to describe a mechanism, and those frameworks
were not available to the people being described.

The rule established in Chapter 2 governs everything that follows. I will make historical
claims, sociological claims and carefully hedged psychological claims. I will not make
metaphysical claims, and I will not diagnose any dead person's inner life. Where I use a
theory, I will say whose it is and what it was built for.

---

### The word "technology"

I have used it throughout and it deserves defending, because it sounds like a metaphor and
I mean it more literally than that.

A technology is a reliable arrangement of means to produce an effect. It does not have to
be mechanical. A legal contract is a technology; so is double-entry bookkeeping; so is the
sonnet form. What makes something a technology rather than a practice is that it can be
*transmitted* — written down, taught, reproduced in a different place by different people,
and expected to work again.

By that standard, the Masonic initiation is unambiguously a technology. It was specified in
detail. It was taught. It was carried to four continents by men who had learned it in one
room and reproduced it in another. It was adopted wholesale by organisations with entirely
different aims — friendly societies, Rosicrucian orders, magical societies, women's orders,
Black fraternal bodies — on the evident assumption that it would go on working.

And it did. That is the interesting part. A ritual designed in eighteenth-century London for
English gentlemen produced recognisably similar effects on free Black Bostonians in 1787,
Parisian duchesses in 1775, Bengali clerks in 1860, Prussian alchemists in 1780, and
Victorian magicians in 1890.

Something in the arrangement is doing work independent of what anybody believes it means.

---

### The components

Strip the initiation of Chapter 4 to its mechanism and it has seven parts. Every one is
present in the eighteenth-century sources; every one recurs in every derivative system in
this book.

**Separation.** The candidate is removed from ordinary company and placed alone. He waits.

**Divestment.** Money and metals removed; clothing disarranged; the ordinary markers of
status suspended.

**Sensory restriction.** The blindfold. He cannot see, which means the room must be
constructed from sound, touch and movement.

**Ordeal.** Not injury, but controlled stress: the point at the breast, the rope, the
questions he cannot answer, the awareness that he does not know what happens next.

**Commitment.** The oath, spoken aloud, in his own voice, kneeling, before witnesses, with
consequences named.

**Revelation.** The blindfold removed at a prepared instant, on a prepared image.

**Reincorporation.** He is given a place, a title, a badge, and a body of people who now
address him differently.

Van Gennep's tripartite scheme of 1909 — separation, liminality, incorporation — maps onto
this cleanly, and Victor Turner's elaboration of the middle phase supplies the vocabulary
for what happens in it: the stripping of status, the equality of initiands, the openness of
the person in transition.[^1]

I offer those as descriptive tools. Van Gennep was generalising from ethnographic reports
of other societies; Turner was working with Ndembu material. Neither had a London tavern in
mind, and the fit — while genuinely good — is a fit between a general pattern and an
instance, not evidence of any historical relationship.

Catherine Bell's caution is the right one to hold alongside them: "ritual" is not a natural
kind, and treating it as a universal category with universal properties smuggles in more
than it explains.[^2]

---

### What the components do

Taking them one at a time, in the most deflationary terms available.

**Waiting** raises arousal. The candidate's attention is fully engaged before anything has
happened, which means the ceremony begins with a subject already primed.

**Divestment** removes the markers by which he ordinarily knows his position relative to
others. Whatever else he is in that room, he is not what his coat says he is.

**Sensory restriction** does two things. It forces him to build the space from non-visual
information, which is effortful and therefore memorable. And it removes his capacity to
anticipate, which means every event arrives without warning.

**Ordeal** produces physiological arousal, and arousal is the best-established modulator of
memory encoding we have. This is not a mystical claim; it is the reason people remember
where they were during disasters and forget entire ordinary Tuesdays.

**Commitment** engages a mechanism social psychology has documented extensively: a public,
effortful, voluntary declaration changes subsequent behaviour and self-description far more
than a private or costless one. The candidate has spoken the words himself, in front of
people, having gone to trouble to get there.

**Revelation** places the content at the point of maximum receptivity. And the content is
deliberately modest — a book and two tools — which matters, because an extravagant
revelation would invite evaluation, and a modest one invites the recipient to supply the
significance himself. What a person constructs is held more firmly than what he is given.

**Reincorporation** converts the whole event into a durable social fact. He now has a status
that other people confirm every time they use it.

Seven components, each doing an identifiable job, assembled into a sequence in which each
prepares the next.

I do not think anyone designed this. There is no memorandum, no committee, no theorist. It
appears to have been arrived at by accretion and practical refinement over a long period —
which is how most technologies are arrived at, and which is entirely compatible with its
being extremely well made.

---

### What it cannot do

Here is where the honesty of the last part of this book has to start.

The mechanism above describes how an initiation produces **memory, commitment and status
change**. Those are well-evidenced effects and the sociological ones leave records: we can
see, in membership rolls and correspondence and charitable accounts and business
partnerships, that Masonic obligation had practical consequences for how people behaved.

It does not describe **moral transformation**, which is what the ritual claims to produce.

And on that, the evidence is simply not there. We have no way to determine whether men who
went through Masonic initiation became more upright, more charitable or more temperate than
comparable men who did not. There is no control group, no pre-registration, no measurement.
There is a great deal of testimony, and testimony from people who have undergone an
expensive commitment ceremony is precisely the testimony one should treat most carefully.

What we can say is narrower and still substantial: **the ritual reliably produces people who
report having been changed, who behave as members of a bounded group, and who remember the
event for the rest of their lives.**

Whether it made them better men is not a historian's question, and I am not going to pretend
to answer it.

### Bogdan's argument

The scholar who has done most with this material for the study of Western esotericism is
Henrik Bogdan, and his argument deserves setting out because it is the theoretical spine of
this book's thesis.[^3]

Bogdan's contention is that rituals of initiation are the principal *transmission vehicle*
of Western esotericism in the modern period — that the reason esoteric currents survived
from the Renaissance into the twentieth century is not a chain of secret teachers but the
existence of initiatory organisations capable of carrying them.

Put that beside the finding of Parts III to VI and the picture completes itself.

The esoteric currents of Part II — Hermetic, Kabbalistic, alchemical, Rosicrucian — had no
institutional carrier. They existed in books. Books are a poor transmission medium for a
tradition: they preserve content but not practice, community, authority or continuity. A
book can sit unread for two centuries.

Freemasonry supplied what the books could not: an *organisational form* for graded
initiatory instruction, reproducible anywhere, requiring no central authority, capable of
holding any content.

And from the moment it existed, the currents began using it. The Élus Coëns in 1761. The
Gold- und Rosenkreuz in the 1770s. The SRIA in 1866. The Golden Dawn in 1888. The O.T.O. in
1912. Wicca in the 1950s.

**The Western esoteric tradition survived into modernity because Freemasonry gave it a body
to travel in.**

That is the thesis, and this is the chapter where its mechanism is stated. The rest of Part
VII examines the three components that make the body work: symbol, secrecy, and the
initiate's own physical presence in a dark room.
