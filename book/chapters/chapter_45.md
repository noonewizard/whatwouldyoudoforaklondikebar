# CHAPTER FORTY-FIVE
## Symbol as Operating System

The strangest thing about Freemasonry is not what it teaches. It is that it does not teach
anything.

Three hundred years of ritual, a vast literature, an enormous symbolic vocabulary, and no
doctrine. There is no Masonic theology. There is no Masonic philosophy. There is no
authorised interpretation of the square, the compasses, the Temple, the pillars, or the
raising of Hiram. There is not, and has never been, any body empowered to supply one.

Every organisation in this book that acquired a doctrine — the Élus Coëns, the Rectified
Rite, the Gold- und Rosenkreuz, Theosophy, the Golden Dawn, Thelema — acquired it from
outside, and in almost every case the doctrine outlived its organisation by less time than
the organisation's borrowed Masonic structure did.

This chapter is about why the emptiness is the point.

---

### What a symbol without a definition does

Consider two ways of transmitting the proposition *conduct yourself with integrity*.

**The first: state it.** Tell the candidate that he should be honest, and explain why. This
is efficient, unambiguous, and forgettable. It is also *evaluable*: the recipient can
consider the proposition, weigh it, and agree or disagree. And if he disagrees, he is now in
opposition to the institution that told him.

**The second: give him a square.** Put it in his hand, tell him it is the working tool of a
craft, tell him that Masons act by it, and stop. Do not define the transfer. Do not say
which aspects of his conduct it governs. Let him work it out.

The second method is less efficient and enormously more durable, for four reasons.

**It cannot be refuted.** A proposition can be false. A square cannot. There is nothing to
disagree with, and therefore no possibility of schism.

**It is completed by the recipient.** He supplies the application, from his own life. What a
person constructs for himself is held more firmly than what he is handed, and it is
personalised to his circumstances without any effort by the institution.

**It grows with him.** What the square means to a man of twenty-two joining his father's
lodge is not what it means to him at fifty-five after a business failure. A defined
proposition stays the size it was issued. An undefined symbol accommodates a whole life.

**And it is portable across everything.** A moral proposition is embedded in a moral
framework, which is embedded in a religion, which divides people. A tool is not. The square
travelled to Catholic France, Lutheran Prussia, Jewish London, Hindu and Parsi Bengal,
Ottoman Istanbul and Black Boston, and it worked in every one, because it never said
anything that any of them could object to.

Anderson's formula of 1723 — the religion in which all men agree — is usually read as a
statement about religion. It is better read as a statement about *method*. The institution
discovered, or stumbled into, a way of transmitting moral seriousness without transmitting
doctrine, and the vehicle was the undefined symbol.

---

### The operating-system analogy

I have called this an operating system and the analogy is worth unpacking, since it is doing
real work in the book's argument.

An operating system does not do anything a user wants. It cannot write a letter or play
music. What it provides is a *managed environment* in which programs that do those things
can run: memory, a file system, a way of getting input and putting output somewhere,
conventions that applications can rely on.

Its value is precisely its indifference to content. An operating system that only ran
accounting software would be a worse operating system.

Masonic symbolism functions the same way. It provides an environment — a spatial
vocabulary (east and west, above and below, within and without), a temporal one (the
degrees, the progress from darkness to light), a moral one (the tools, the standard, the
test), and a narrative one (the Temple, the loss, the search) — within which almost any
content can be run.

Load Christian theology and you get the Rose-Croix. Load Martinez's angelology and you get
the Élus Coëns. Load alchemy and you get the Gold and Rosy Cross. Load Genesis and women's
association and you get the Adoption Rite. Load Black political self-organisation and you
get African Lodge. Load mutual insurance and you get the Oddfellows. Load Hermetic Qabalah
and you get the Golden Dawn. Load Thelema and you get the O.T.O.

Nine applications. One platform. The platform was not altered by any of them.

### The cost

I have made this sound like an unalloyed strength, and Chapter 16 already flagged that it is
not. The point bears restating here in its full form, because it is the book's principal
criticism of its subject.

**An institution that will not say what it means cannot say what it does not mean.**

Freemasonry has spent three centuries unable to correct any interpretation of itself,
because correction requires an authority to define, and defining is the one thing it has
constitutionally refused to do.

The consequences are visible throughout Part VI. When Pike's comparative mythology was taken
for Masonic doctrine, no one could say otherwise. When Blavatsky announced that Masons had
forgotten the meaning of their own symbols, the Craft had no counter-claim available,
because it had never asserted a meaning to forget. When Taxil manufactured a Luciferian
Masonry, the fraternity's denial carried less weight than it should have, because the
fraternity had never specified what it *did* believe.

And the same property that made the Craft hospitable to a Jewish merchant in 1730 made it
hospitable to a Prussian reactionary in 1780 and to a British imperial administrator in
1860. A vessel does not select its cargo. That is what makes it a vessel.

### Why the symbols were these symbols

One last question, and it returns to the historical register.

If the content is arbitrary, why a square and a Temple? Why not any other set?

The answer, established in Part III, is that they were not chosen for their meaning. They
were *inherited from a trade*. The square, the level, the plumb and the compasses were in
the bag. The Temple was in the craft legend. The pillars were in 1 Kings. Nobody selected a
symbolic system; a building trade acquired an allegorical habit, and the allegory
outlived the building.

But there is a further observation, and I think it is not merely aesthetic.

The building trade's tools are, distinctively, **instruments of verification**. They do not
make anything. A square does not cut stone; it tells you whether the stone is true. A level
does not raise a wall; it tells you whether the wall is upright. A plumb-line tells you the
truth about a vertical and has no opinion about anything else.

Of all the trades that might have produced an initiatory system — weaving, smithing,
carpentry, brewing — this one had a toolkit whose entire function is the comparison of a
thing against an external, indifferent, non-negotiable standard.

Transfer that to conduct and you have an ethics with no doctrine in it. *There is a
standard. It does not care who you are. You may test yourself against it.*

That is the whole moral content of Craft Masonry, and it required no philosophy to generate.
It was implicit in a mason's bag, and the eighteenth century noticed.

I do not think that is why Freemasonry emerged from masonry rather than from some other
trade — Chapter 18 gave the historical reasons, and they are about geometry's status as a
liberal science. But it is, I think, why the symbolism proved so durable once it existed.
A system built on measurement rather than on assertion is a system that cannot be argued
with, and therefore cannot be broken by argument.

Which brings us to the other thing that cannot be argued with, because it refuses to enter
the argument at all.
