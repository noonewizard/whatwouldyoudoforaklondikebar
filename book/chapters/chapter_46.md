# CHAPTER FORTY-SIX
## Secrecy and Power

Chapter 3 asked what was secret in a Masonic lodge and answered: not much, and it was
published in 1730 anyway. This chapter asks the harder question. If the content was thin
and its publication changed nothing, what was the secrecy *for*?

The answer has four parts, and each of them turns out to be a form of power.

---

### Secrecy makes a boundary

Georg Simmel's 1906 essay remains the best short account of this, and its central move is
to treat the secret society's secret as a *structural* rather than an informational object.

A group needs a boundary in order to exist. The boundary can be drawn by many things —
birth, geography, trade, belief — but each of those imports content, and content divides. A
group defined by belief will fracture over belief; a group defined by trade cannot admit
anyone outside it.

A group defined by *having been admitted* imports nothing. The line is drawn by the act of
crossing it. And the secret is what makes crossing detectable: there is something the inside
has and the outside does not, and possession of it is the definition of being inside.

This is why the *content* of the secret is nearly irrelevant, and why publication does not
dissolve the group. A man who reads *Masonry Dissected* knows the words. He has not crossed
the line, because the line is not made of words. It is made of the ceremony, the witnesses,
and the institutional recognition that follows.

Simmel also noticed the corollary, which is that secret societies tend to develop elaborate
internal hierarchies — because once you have discovered that a boundary can be made by
admission, you can make as many as you like. The eighteenth-century degree explosion of
Chapter 24 is that insight applied by people who had not read Simmel.

---

### Secrecy makes value

A thing withheld acquires worth proportional to the difficulty of obtaining it, and a person
who has undergone difficulty to obtain it will not easily conclude that it was worthless.

This is not manipulation, and it is not peculiar to esoteric organisations. It operates in
medical training, in military selection, in the apprenticeship of any craft, and in every
institution that has ever made entry costly. It is also not simply irrational: costly entry
genuinely does select for commitment, and a group whose members have all paid a price has
information about each other that a group with open doors does not.

But it has a consequence that matters for this book's subject. **It makes the content
unfalsifiable from inside.**

A man who has spent an evening blindfolded, swearing an oath, in order to be shown a square
and a pair of compasses, has two available interpretations. Either the ceremony conveyed
something significant, or he has just been through an elaborate procedure for nothing. The
first is more comfortable and it is also, on the evidence of Chapter 44, substantially true —
something did happen to him. But the same mechanism will support an interpretation that is
not true, with equal force.

That is how a man reaches the thirty-second degree of a system and concludes that its
teaching descends from Egypt. He has paid too much for it to be a nineteenth-century
compilation.

---

### Secrecy makes authority

If the route to what is withheld runs through a person, that person has power requiring no
coercion.

Every system in this book runs on this, and every one of them eventually breaks on it. The
sequence is so regular that it can be written as a life cycle.

**Phase one.** A body is founded with graded access to withheld material. Authority flows
from the top, and the arrangement works.

**Phase two.** The organisation grows. Members ascend. Some reach the top and find that the
material runs out — that there is nothing above the highest grade except the people who
issued it.

**Phase three.** The question is asked: *by what authority?*

**Phase four.** The leadership answers by pointing above itself — to Unknown Superiors, to
a German adept, to Mahatmas, to Secret Chiefs.

**Phase five.** The superiors cannot be produced.

**Phase six.** Schism.

Von Hund: phases one through six, 1750s to 1782. Westcott and Mathers: 1888 to 1900.
Blavatsky's Society survived longer but fought the same battle repeatedly. Crowley, having
watched one collapse, designed the A∴A∴ specifically so that phase three could not be
organised.

**The manufactured superior is not a moral failing peculiar to charlatans. It is a
structural requirement of graded secrecy, and the demand for it arises from below.**

---

### Secrecy makes mythology

And here is the cost, which the institution has never been able to escape.

An opaque body is a screen. People project onto screens, and what they project is determined
entirely by what they already fear.

Run the projections chronologically and the pattern is exact.

**1738.** The papacy, at a moment of anxiety about oath-bound associations outside
ecclesiastical control, condemns Freemasonry. The stated concerns are the oath, the secrecy,
and the mixing of confessions.

**1797–98.** Europe, having just watched a revolution destroy a monarchy, is offered by
Barruel and Robison an explanation involving secret societies. The projection is
*revolution*.

**1826–40.** America, in the middle of an evangelical revival and a democratic expansion,
looks at the Morgan affair and sees *privilege* — an aristocracy of oath-bound men above the
law.

**1884–97.** French Catholicism, losing a culture war against an anticlerical republic,
looks at the lodges and sees *the devil*. Taxil supplies the details.

**1900–1945.** Antisemitic movements across Europe fuse Jews and Masons into a single
enemy, and the fusion has lethal consequences.

**1960s onward.** A media culture suspicious of institutions looks at Freemasonry and sees
*the global elite*.

Five projections in three centuries. The screen did not change. The projections track the
anxieties of the projecting societies with almost embarrassing precision, and the historical
Freemasonry underneath — charity dinners, corner stones, the square and the compasses, a
membership of shopkeepers and clerks — is visible in none of them.

---

### The historian's problem

Which leaves the difficulty this book has had to work around throughout.

Secrecy produces gaps in the record, and the gaps are the same shape as the thing everyone
is looking for. If an organisation concealed its practices, then the absence of evidence for
a practice is not evidence of absence.

I set out the three-part answer in Chapter 3 and it has been vindicated by everything since.

**Secrecy leaks.** Every organisation in this book left a record. The Illuminati's papers
were seized and printed in 1787. The Golden Dawn's rituals were published by Crowley in 1909
and Regardie in 1937. The Masonic secrets were in the newspapers by 1723. Westcott's German
letters were examined and found wanting. Taxil confessed in a hall full of journalists.
Organisations that actually exist leave paper, because they have to.

**The claim of secrecy is testable even when the secret is not.** We cannot examine an
undisclosed transmission. We can examine when the claim of it was first made, by whom, with
what motive, and whether it contains anachronisms. That test has been applied about forty
times in this book and the claims have failed it at an extraordinary rate.

**And the burden does not shift.** "You cannot prove it did not happen in secret" licenses
anything whatever and is therefore not an argument.

---

### What was actually hidden

I want to end this chapter with an observation that I think is the truest thing in it.

There is a great deal of genuine concealment in this history. Almost none of it is Masonic.

The Craft published its constitutions, printed its membership, advertised its buildings,
processed in the street, laid corner stones before crowds, and had its secrets exposed in
cheap pamphlets for four decades without complaint that anyone acted on.

What was actually hidden was this: that Westcott wrote the Sprengel letters. That Pike took
his Kabbalah from a Frenchman he did not name. That von Hund had no warrant. That Diana
Vaughan did not exist. That the Cipher Manuscripts were compiled in the nineteenth century.
That the tarot-Kabbalah system was invented in the 1850s.

Every one of those concealments was practised by someone claiming *to reveal* Masonic
secrets, or claiming authority *derived* from Masonic structures — and every one was
eventually exposed by ordinary documentary research.

The organisation accused of hiding everything hid very little. The people accusing it, and
the people claiming to inherit from it, hid a great deal.

That asymmetry is, I think, the single most useful thing a reader can take from this book
into any other subject.
