# CHAPTER FORTY-SEVEN
## The Body in the Dark

Everything this book has described happened to somebody's body.

That sounds obvious and it is routinely forgotten. The literature on Freemasonry and the
occult is overwhelmingly a literature about *meanings*: what the square signifies, what the
Temple represents, whether the legend derives from Egypt. It is written by people sitting
down, about people who were standing up.

But a man in an eighteenth-century lodge was not principally engaged in interpretation. He
was barefoot on boards. He had a blindfold on. He was cold, or too warm; he was aware of his
own breathing; his knee was on a hard surface; someone had a hand on his arm and was moving
him in a direction he could not predict.

This chapter is about that, and about what a century of work on embodiment and memory
suggests it does — offered, as always in Part VII, as a lens rather than as a finding, with
the prohibition on retroactive diagnosis fully in force.

---

### The inventory

Take the components of Chapter 4 again, but now as physical facts rather than as ritual
stages.

**Bare skin.** A knee, a breast, a foot. The removal of clothing in specific places rather
than generally — which is far more effective, because partial exposure is felt continuously
in a way that complete undress is not.

**Bare feet, or one of them.** Walking on boards in a room full of shod men. Every step
supplies information about the floor and about the asymmetry of one's own condition.

**The blindfold.** Total visual deprivation, in a strange room, among people who can see.

**The cable-tow.** A rope at the neck, held by someone else, so that one's own movement is
not entirely one's own.

**Posture.** Kneeling. Standing in a specified attitude. Placing hands in a specified
position on an object one cannot see.

**Touch.** Being led by the arm. Being positioned by another person's hands. The point at
the breast. The grip.

**Sound.** Voices from fixed positions, addressing and not addressing him. Knocks. The
movement of feet. Silence, which in a room full of people is a physical sensation.

**Duration.** Waiting. The ceremony is slow, and slowness in a state of heightened arousal
is itself an experience.

---

### What the literature suggests

Three bodies of work bear on this, and I will name what each was built for.

**Embodied cognition.** The research program arguing that thought is not confined to
symbolic manipulation in the head but is constituted in part by bodily state, posture and
action. Its relevance here is straightforward: a proposition learned while kneeling, in the
dark, under stress, is not the same cognitive object as the same proposition read in a
chair. The physical circumstances are part of what is encoded.

**Memory and arousal.** The well-established finding that emotionally arousing events are
encoded more durably than neutral ones. This is the best-evidenced element in the chapter
and it is the most banal: the initiation is memorable because it is alarming.

**Whitehouse's modes of religiosity.** Harvey Whitehouse's framework distinguishes two
patterns of religious transmission: a *doctrinal* mode, characterized by frequent,
low-arousal, verbally transmitted teaching, and an *imagistic* mode, characterized by
infrequent, high-arousal, sensorially rich ritual that produces intense personal memory and
strong small-group bonds.[^1]

The Masonic initiation is imagistic in that sense — rare, arousing, sensory, and productive
of a memory the participant can recount fifty years later. And Whitehouse's framework
predicts exactly what Freemasonry exhibits: strong local cohesion, transmission through
participation rather than through doctrine, and — crucially — *interpretive diversity*,
because imagistic ritual does not carry a specified meaning and participants generate their
own.

Which is the finding of Chapter 45, arrived at from an entirely different direction.

I want to be careful. Whitehouse built his framework on Melanesian material and it has been
argued about extensively. I am not claiming it explains Freemasonry. I am noting that a
theory built to explain something else predicts several of the features we have documented,
which is the kind of convergence worth recording and not worth over-interpreting.

---

### The prohibition

And now the thing this chapter must not do.

I cannot tell you what Elias Ashmole felt at Warrington in 1646. I cannot tell you whether
the Duchess of Bourbon was moved by the Adoption ritual, or what passed through Prince
Hall's mind in 1775, or what a Bengali clerk experienced in a Calcutta lodge in 1860.

Not because the evidence is thin. Because there is *no* evidence, and because the kind of
claim involved is not one that historical evidence can support.

This matters more than it may seem, because the temptation is enormous and the literature is
full of people who have not resisted it. Books on this subject routinely tell you what
initiates experienced, on the basis of what the ritual was designed to produce and what a
modern author imagines he would have felt. That is not history. It is the author's
introspection projected onto the dead.

What we have instead is three legitimate categories of statement.

**Design claims.** *The ritual was arranged so as to produce heightened attention.* This is
supported by the ritual texts themselves.

**Report claims.** *Some participants wrote that they were moved.* Supported where the
testimony exists — and it is thinner than one would expect, and heavily formulaic where it
exists, because people describing an initiation tend to use the vocabulary the initiation
gave them.

**Theoretical claims.** *Ritual of this kind is, on current understanding, likely to produce
durable memory and group attachment.* True as a general statement about human beings;
carrying no information whatever about any individual.

Nothing else is available. A book that offers more is selling something.

---

### The one thing the body proves

There is, however, one conclusion that the physical dimension does establish, and it is
important enough to close the chapter on.

**It explains why the ritual survived translation.**

A doctrine must be translated to travel. It must be rendered into a new language, reconciled
with local beliefs, defended against local objections. It arrives diminished and contested.

A body in a dark room does not require translation.

Kneeling means the same thing in Boston and Calcutta. So does being led by the arm, being
unable to see, being asked a question you cannot answer, and having light restored. These
are not culturally specific propositions; they are conditions of being a person with a body
in a room with other people.

That is why the Masonic apparatus crossed every boundary that stopped everything else it
traveled with. Anderson's deism did not survive the journey to Catholic France intact — the
high degrees Christianised it within twenty years. The English moral commentary did not
survive the journey to Prussia — the Rosicrucians replaced it with alchemy. The Craft's
whole interpretive content was stripped and replaced, repeatedly, everywhere it went.

The *ceremony* went everywhere unchanged, because the ceremony is made of the human body and
the human body is the one thing every candidate had in common.

That is the deepest reason for this book's thesis. Freemasonry carried the Western esoteric
tradition into the modern world not because its ideas were compelling but because its
*method* was translatable — and its method was translatable because it was physical.

The content was always local. The darkness was universal.
