# CHAPTER FORTY-EIGHT
## When Symbol Becomes Real

In 1856 a poor Frenchman drew a goat.

He drew it to illustrate a doctrine of equilibrium — the reconciliation of opposites, male
and female, above and below, dissolution and coagulation — and he gave it a name taken from
a fourteenth-century torture chamber. It was one illustration among several in a book about
magic.

A hundred and seventy years later, that drawing is what several billion people picture when
they think of the Devil. It is on album covers and film posters. It has been erected as a
nine-foot bronze statue on the steps of American state capitols as a legal argument about
religious establishment. It has been the subject of court cases, protests, and moral panics.

Éliphas Lévi made a picture of a philosophical principle, and the picture got up and walked
around.

This chapter is about that — about the point at which things made by people begin to act on
history as though they had been there all along. It is the strangest phenomenon in this
book and the one I understand least.

---

### Four cases

**The Baphomet.** Described above. A composite drawn in 1856 from a name of doubtful
meaning, a passage in Herodotus, and an alchemical formula, illustrating a doctrine of
balance. It became the standard iconography of evil, entirely against its maker's intention,
and it did so through reproduction rather than through anyone's argument.

**Fräulein Sprengel.** She did not exist. Westcott, on the evidence, wrote her letters. And
yet she *did* things. She authorised a temple. She conferred grades. Her death in 1890
changed the Order's constitutional position. Real people made real decisions on the basis of
her instructions, and the most influential magical organization in history was founded on
her authority.

**Diana Vaughan.** She did not exist either. She published books. She received the praise of
cardinals. A devotional work under her name was, on the usual accounts, commended in Rome.
An international congress at Trent in 1896 debated her. Thousands of Catholics prayed for
her safety, believing her to be in hiding from Masonic assassins. She had a correspondence.
She had, in every sense that a historian can measure, a career.

**The Palladium.** An organization that never existed, whose supposed activities have shaped
anti-Masonic belief for a hundred and thirty years, and which is still described in print as
though it had a membership.

---

### The mechanism

What is going on here is not, I think, mysterious once stated, though it is unsettling.

A symbol, a person or an institution that exists only as a representation can nonetheless
occupy a *position in a network of real actions*. Once it does, its non-existence stops
mattering.

Fräulein Sprengel's function was to be the source of authority. That function was performed:
Westcott produced letters, the other Chiefs accepted them, a temple was warranted, grades
were conferred, and men and women underwent ceremonies that changed their lives. Every one
of those events happened. The only thing that did not happen was the German adept.

The historian's category for this is not "fiction," because fiction does not warrant
temples. It is closer to what an economist would recognize in a currency: an object whose
value consists entirely in being treated as valuable, and which therefore works exactly as
well as a real one right up until the moment enough people stop.

That is precisely the shape of the Golden Dawn's collapse. Sprengel functioned perfectly for
twelve years and then, when Mathers said she was a forgery, ceased to function instantly —
not because new evidence emerged, but because the collective treatment stopped.

---

### The three-generation rule

There is a pattern in the cases this book has assembled and it is regular enough to state.

**Generation one makes the thing and knows it made it.** Lévi drew the Baphomet and
explained what it meant. Westcott wrote the letters. Taxil invented Diana Vaughan. Mathers
and Westcott assembled the Cipher Manuscripts from their libraries. Ramsay composed a
genealogy. Von Hund may be the exception — he may have been deceived rather than deceiving —
and his is the case where the evidence is genuinely ambiguous.

**Generation two receives it as given and elaborates.** Pike takes Lévi's material as the
tradition and builds on it. Crowley receives the Golden Dawn system as an inheritance and
extends it. Second-generation Strict Observance Masons take the Templar descent as
established and construct further degrees on it.

**Generation three cannot tell the difference, and defends it.** By the twentieth century,
the tarot-Kabbalah correspondence is simply what the tarot is. The Baphomet is simply what
Satan looks like. Masonic Templarism is simply a fact about Masonry. And anyone pointing at
the documentary record is met with genuine, uncomprehending resistance, because the claim
being made — *this was constructed in a specific year by a specific person* — no longer
matches anybody's experience of the object.

Three generations is roughly sixty to eighty years, which is about how long it takes for
everyone who watched something being built to die.

---

### Why it matters that they are made

A reader of a certain temperament will ask, at this point, whether it matters. If the Golden
Dawn's system works, if the Baphomet means something, if Masonic ritual changes people — why
should anyone care that the pedigrees are false?

Three answers, in increasing order of seriousness.

**Because the claim is part of the thing.** The Golden Dawn did not merely have a system; it
had a system it said was ancient, and members joined on that understanding. A person who
undertakes an initiation because it descends from Rosicrucian adepts has been told something
false about what they are doing. Whether they would have joined anyway is not the point; they
were not given the choice.

**Because false history has victims.** The Baphomet's career would be merely curious if it
had stayed in the art world. It did not. It fed the satanic-ritual-abuse panic of the 1980s
and 1990s, in which people went to prison for crimes that did not occur. The Taxil
fabrication fed a European anti-Masonic tradition that fused with antisemitism and
contributed to persecutions with a body count. The conspiracy imagination is not a harmless
hobby; Chapter 50 will make the case in detail.

**And because the truth is better.** This is the argument I care about most.

The story in which Freemasonry preserves the Egyptian mysteries is, considered as a story,
not very good. It is inert. Nothing happens in it; a thing is passed along unchanged for
three thousand years by people with no names.

The documented story is far stranger. A Scottish administrator writes a regulation about
record-keeping in 1598 and inadvertently invents the lodge. A trade legend about Euclid gets
printed in 1723 and becomes a genealogy for half of Europe. A Lutheran writes a joke in 1616
and it becomes the template for every secret society for four centuries. A Bordeaux merchant
starts invoking angels inside a Masonic structure in 1761. A French socialist comes out of
prison and invents modern magic. An American general copies him without attribution and
hands the result to a hundred thousand Masons. A London coroner forges a German
correspondence and founds the order that shapes a century of Western magic. A free Black
leatherworker in Boston gets a warrant from London and builds an institution that outlasts
the country that refused to recognize it.

Those are the actual events. They have people in them, and dates, and motives, and
consequences. Every one of them can be checked.

I do not think that story needs improving, and I have never understood the impulse to
replace it with a worse one.

---

### The honest residue

And yet.

Something in this history genuinely does not reduce, and Part VII would be dishonest if it
closed without saying so.

Lévi's Baphomet *works*. It is a more compelling image of the union of opposites than any
of the treatises it illustrated, and it has outlived them. The Golden Dawn's system of
correspondences *works*, in the sense that people build practices on it and find them
coherent. The Masonic third degree *works* — it has been performed for three hundred years
on men of every description, and they come out saying something happened.

None of that requires antiquity. None of it requires a supernatural mechanism, and this book
has been careful never to assert one. But it does require an explanation, and "it is all
made up" is not one, because plenty of made-up things do not work at all.

What the evidence supports is this: **these are well-made objects.** They were constructed by
people with real skill, working with materials that had been refined over centuries, aimed
at effects they understood well enough to reproduce. The Baphomet is a good design. The
third degree is a good ceremony. The Tree of Life with the tarot on it is a good
architecture.

That is not a small claim, and it is the one that survives everything.

We now have the mechanism and we have the history. What remains is the verdict.
