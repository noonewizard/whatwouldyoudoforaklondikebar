# CHAPTER FIFTY
## The Conspiracy Problem

In 1797 a professor of natural philosophy at Edinburgh published a book arguing that the
French Revolution had been engineered by a conspiracy operating through Masonic lodges. In
the following year a French Jesuit in exile in London published four volumes arguing the
same thing at much greater length and with much greater documentation.

John Robison's *Proofs of a Conspiracy* and Augustin Barruel's *Mémoires pour servir à
l'histoire du Jacobinisme* are the founding documents of the modern conspiracy tradition.
Everything that follows in this genre — and there has been a great deal — descends from
them, usually without knowing it.[^1]

They are worth examining properly, because they are not stupid books, and understanding why
intelligent men wrote them is the only way to understand the phenomenon.

---

### What they had

Barruel and Robison were not inventing from nothing. They had material.

In 1784 and 1785 the Bavarian government suppressed the Order of the Illuminati, founded in
1776 by Adam Weishaupt, a professor of canon law at Ingolstadt. The order was real. Its
programme was real: it aimed at the propagation of rationalist, anticlerical and reformist
ideas, and its method was real too — systematic infiltration of Masonic lodges, in order to
use their structure and recruit from their membership.

And when the Bavarian authorities raided the houses of its members, they found correspondence
and internal documents, which they published in 1787.[^2]

So in the 1790s a European reader could hold in his hands the internal papers of a secret
society that had explicitly set out to infiltrate Freemasonry and to influence politics
through it — published by a government, authenticated, and available.

Five years later the French monarchy fell.

Barruel and Robison put those two facts together.

---

### The mistake

The inference is understandable and it is wrong, and the specific way in which it is wrong is
worth stating because it recurs in every subsequent iteration of the genre.

**The order was destroyed before the Revolution.** The Illuminati were suppressed in
1784–87. The Bastille fell in 1789. For the order to have directed the Revolution, it would
have to have done so after being raided, banned, exposed, and having its papers printed by
the state.

**Its membership was small and its reach limited.** The order existed for about a decade in
a few German territories with a few thousand members at most.

**Its programme was not revolutionary in the French sense.** Weishaupt's aims were
Enlightenment-reformist. The order's methods were conspiratorial; its objectives were the
ordinary objectives of the German Aufklärung.

**And the Revolution has an explanation.** France in 1789 had a fiscal crisis, a failed
harvest, a collapsing tax system, a political stalemate between crown and parlements, an
ideological transformation two generations deep, and a monarchy that had bankrupted itself
supporting the American war. A historian does not need a secret society to account for it,
and adding one explains nothing that the fiscal record does not already explain better.

What Barruel and Robison did was take a real, documented, small, failed conspiracy and
expand it to the scale of the event they wanted explained.

That operation — **the real small conspiracy inflated to explain the large event** — is the
basic move of the entire genre, and it is present in every case this chapter examines.

---

### Why 1797

The timing is not accidental and it tells us what the genre is for.

Both men were writing in the immediate aftermath of a catastrophe, from the losing side.
Barruel was a Jesuit whose order had been suppressed and whose Church had been
disestablished, robbed and persecuted; he was writing in exile. Robison was a conservative
Scottish academic watching the European order he valued come apart.

J. M. Roberts's study of the mythology of secret societies makes the essential point: the
conspiracy explanation is a *consolation*.[^3] If the Revolution happened because a small
group of wicked men planned it, then the society that was overthrown was not defective, the
ideas that overthrew it have no merit, and the catastrophe is reversible — identify the
plotters, remove them, and the world returns.

The alternative explanation is that the old order fell because large numbers of people, for
reasons connected to how they actually lived, ceased to support it.

The first is bearable. The second is not.

That is the emotional engine of conspiracy theory, and it has not changed in two hundred
and thirty years.

---

### The later cases, briefly

Each of these deserves the full six-question treatment and each has received it elsewhere in
this book or in the scholarship. Here they are in summary.

**The Priory of Sion.** A French organisation of that name was registered under the law of
associations on 7 May 1956 by Pierre Plantard and three associates. Forged documents — the
*dossiers secrets*, purporting to establish a Merovingian bloodline and a grand-mastership
running through Leonardo, Newton and Victor Hugo — were deposited in the Bibliothèque
nationale in the 1960s. Plantard's fabrications were exposed by French journalists in the
1980s and he admitted substantial parts of them. The material was popularised in English by
*Holy Blood, Holy Grail* in 1982 and then, enormously, by a novel in 2003.

**Verdict: a documented modern fabrication with a registration certificate.** It is the
cleanest case in this book, because we have the paperwork of the hoax itself.

**The geometry of Washington, D.C.** The claim that the street plan encodes pentagrams and
Masonic symbols. Pierre Charles L'Enfant's 1791 plan follows Baroque planning convention —
radial avenues, diagonal vistas, circles at intersections — which is what capital cities
looked like in the period; compare Versailles and Karlsruhe. The alleged figures require
selecting some streets and ignoring others, and change if different streets are chosen.
L'Enfant's Masonic membership has never been established. **Verdict: unsupported; the
pattern is planning convention, and the reading is twentieth century.**

**The Great Seal and the dollar bill.** Treated in Chapter 21. The Eye of Providence is
standard Christian iconography; the final design was by Charles Thomson, who was not a
Freemason; the reverse was not printed on currency until 1935. **Verdict: unsupported.**

**Masonic control of governments.** The documented fact is that Masonic membership among
eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Anglo-American elites was genuinely high and genuinely
consequential — as a network, a source of connection, and a form of sociability. Bullock and
Harland-Jacobs have established this in detail, and it matters. What is not documented, and
what the records positively exclude, is direction: an institution whose members were on both
sides of the American Revolution, both sides of the American Civil War, and both sides of
most European political divisions was not issuing instructions.

---

### The discipline

Three principles follow from this material, and they are the ones this book has tried to
apply throughout.

**Take the claims seriously enough to test them.** Dismissal is not refutation, and it
persuades nobody. The claims in this chapter are historical claims with dates and sources,
and they can be handled by historical methods. The Priory of Sion is not too silly to
investigate; it is *interesting* to investigate, and the registration document is more
damning than any amount of scorn.

**Distinguish the real from the inflated.** There was an Illuminati order. There was a
Masonic conspiracy in western New York in 1826 that abducted a man and covered it up. There
was a genuine occult network at the summit of the Prussian state in the 1780s. Conceding
these strengthens the position rather than weakening it, because it shows what the evidence
for such things actually looks like — local, documented, prosecuted, and written about for
two centuries.

**And identify the need.** Every wave of anti-Masonic conspiracy mapped precisely onto a
social anxiety: revolution in 1797, privilege in 1826, the devil in 1884, race and nation in
1900, institutional distrust after 1960. The content of the accusation is information about
the accuser. That is a historical finding, not a rhetorical put-down, and it is the most
reliable predictor of what the next wave will allege.

---

### The cost

I want to end with the reason this is not an academic exercise.

The Taxil material, the antisemitic fusion of Jew and Mason, and the conspiracy tradition
generally are not harmless. Freemasonry was banned and its members persecuted, imprisoned and
killed under fascist and Nazi regimes across Europe, on the basis of a literature descended
from Barruel by way of Taxil and the *Protocols*. The satanic-panic material of the 1980s
and 1990s, which drew on the same iconography, sent people to prison for crimes that did not
happen.

Conspiracy theory is not a colourful margin of this subject. It is the part of it with a
body count.

Which is the best argument I know for the method this book has used. The six questions are
not pedantry. They are the only reliable protection against a kind of belief that has, on
the historical record, repeatedly got people killed.
