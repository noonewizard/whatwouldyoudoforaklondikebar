# CHAPTER FIFTY-TWO
## What Remains Unexplained

A book that closes every question it opens is lying about at least one of them.

What follows are the questions this investigation could not answer. Some are unanswered
because the documents have not been examined; those may yield. Some are unanswered because
the documents do not exist and never did; those will not. I have tried to say which is
which, because the difference matters and is usually obscured.

---

### Questions that may still be answered

**1. Why was Elias Ashmole made a Freemason at Warrington in 1646?**

We have the entry, the hour, the names of seven men, and nothing else. Ashmole wrote
millions of words about himself and not one about his reason. Further work on the Warrington
men, on the local gentry networks, and on the Mainwaring connection might produce a context;
it will not produce a motive unless a letter turns up.

**2. Who composed the Hiramic legend, and when exactly?**

The central ritual of the world's largest fraternal organisation has no known author. It
appears complete in a hostile pamphlet in 1730 and there is nothing before it. Lodge minutes
of the 1720s may still yield the earliest minuted working of a third degree; the composition
itself is probably lost.

**3. Where did the Royal Arch come from?**

A degree centred on the recovery of a lost divine name, appearing in England and Ireland in
the middle of the eighteenth century, with no clear parentage in the Craft degrees and no
clear continental source. The question of whether its concern with the Name reflects any
genuine contact with Jewish or Christian-Kabbalistic material, or is an independent
development from the third degree's declaration that the word is lost, is open. I incline to
the second and cannot demonstrate it.

**4. When does the All-Seeing Eye first appear in Masonic material culture?**

Not in commentary — in objects. Dated aprons, certificates, tracing boards and jewels. The
work has not been done systematically, and until it is, statements about the symbol's
Masonic dating are approximations. I have given one and flagged it.

**5. Who compiled the Cipher Manuscripts?**

Kenneth Mackenzie is the usual candidate and the case is circumstantial. The manuscripts
exist; their contents are accounted for by the founders' libraries; their compiler is not
established. Paper and ink analysis, if it has been done to modern standards, has not been
published in a form I could consult.

**6. Did Pike read Lévi in French, and when did he acquire him?**

De Hoyos has established *that* Pike used Lévi extensively. The acquisition history — which
editions, purchased when, annotated how — would be recoverable from Pike's library, which
survives, and would tell us a good deal about how the transmission actually happened.

**7. What was Crowley's Masonic status, initiation by initiation?**

The claims and the records conflict, the bodies involved were of varying regularity, and the
literature tends to resolve the confusion in whichever direction its author prefers. A
careful inventory is possible and has not been made.

**8. What did the Adoption Rite look like in practice, lodge by lodge?**

Snoek has reconstructed the texts from some hundred and thirty sources. What actually
happened in a room in Paris in 1775 — attendance, atmosphere, who spoke, what it meant to
the women present — is a different question, and the sources for it are thinner.

**9. What did Prince Hall and his companions understand themselves to be joining in 1775?**

We have his petitions and his public life, which tell us a great deal about his purposes. We
do not have his account of the lodge. Archival work on African Lodge's early records may
help; the interior question may be permanently out of reach.

---

### Questions the evidence probably cannot settle

**10. Was Stevenson right about the art of memory?**

The 1599 statute requires Scottish masons to be tested in the art of memory. Whether that
means the ordinary mnemonic technique of every grammar school or the Renaissance
cosmological art is not determinable from the text, and no other evidence bears on it
directly. Stevenson's case is the best anyone has made for an esoteric element in
seventeenth-century Scottish Masonry, and it remains suggestive rather than proved. I do not
expect this to be resolved.

**11. Did von Hund believe his own story?**

He spent thirty years and a fortune waiting for superiors who never came. That is consistent
with sincerity and consistent with a very long deception. The evidence does not distinguish,
and the man's interior state is not recoverable.

**12. Why did the exposures fail to damage Freemasonry?**

I have offered an answer in Chapters 3 and 23 — that the secret was performative and
boundary-making rather than informational, and that you cannot expose an experience. That is
an interpretation. It is not a finding, and an alternative account of the same evidence is
perfectly possible.

**13. Does Masonic initiation produce measurable psychological change?**

There is no historical data and there can be none. Modern data on modern initiates would
tell us about modern initiates, in a different culture, undergoing a modified ceremony with
full knowledge of what it is. It would not be evidence about the eighteenth century, and
treating it as such would violate every principle this book has been built on.

**14. Why did this symbolic system prove so much more durable than its rivals?**

The eighteenth century produced hundreds of initiatory systems. Nearly all are gone.
Freemasonry is still here. I have suggested reasons — the emptiness, the portability, the
physicality of the ceremony, the fact that the symbols are instruments of verification — and
I am not confident any of them is sufficient. It may simply be that one system had to
survive and this was the one, and that explanations of survival after the fact are mostly
storytelling.

**15. Is "Western esotericism" a real historical object?**

Hanegraaff's argument is that the category was built by its opponents — that what looks like
a tradition is a container assembled out of what successive orthodoxies rejected. If he is
right, then this book has been tracing the history of a container rather than of a thing.
I have taken the challenge seriously throughout and I have not answered it, because I do not
know how to.

---

### One question I did not expect

There is a sixteenth item on the list and it is the one that has stayed with me.

**Why did none of them simply say what they had done?**

Westcott could have said: I have assembled a system of extraordinary power from the books in
my library, and I offer it on its merits. Mathers could have said it. Pike could have named
Lévi. Ramsay could have said that he was proposing a dignity for the Craft rather than a
descent. Blavatsky could have published her synthesis as her own.

Every one of them would have lost something by it — authority, credibility, the thing that
made members join. I have given the structural reasons and I believe them.

But I notice that I do not entirely believe they are sufficient. There is something in the
material itself, some quality of the experience of assembling a symbolic system, that seems
to make its makers feel they are *receiving* rather than composing. Lévi believed he was
restoring the Church's lost science. Blavatsky believed, at least sometimes, in her Masters.
Westcott went on being a coroner and a Freemason and a decent man while maintaining a
correspondence he had written himself.

I do not have an explanation for that, and I am suspicious of the ones available. It is not
simply fraud; the fraudsters in this book are identifiable and few. It is not simply
self-deception, because these were frequently careful people who were rigorous about other
things.

It may be that the making of a symbolic system is an activity that does not feel like
making, in the way that dreaming does not feel like composition. If that is right, it would
explain a great deal in this book, and I have no way to test it.

---

### The residue

So the honest position at the end of three hundred pages is this.

We know what Freemasonry was, how it formed, what its ritual contained, when its symbols
appear, how it spread, what was added and by whom, and how it carried the Western esoteric
tradition into the modern world. That is a great deal, and almost all of it can be checked.

We do not know why a Lancashire antiquary wanted in, who wrote the legend at the centre of
it all, what a candidate actually felt in the dark, or why the people who built these systems
so consistently insisted they had found them.

The last of those is the one I would most like answered, and I suspect it is not a question
about Freemasonry at all.
