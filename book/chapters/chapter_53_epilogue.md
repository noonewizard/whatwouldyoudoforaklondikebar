# EPILOGUE
## The Temple Within

Somewhere tonight, a man is sitting alone in a small room with the door shut.

He can hear them on the other side. Chairs. Voices. Something that sounds like an order
being given. He is not wearing his own jacket. He has been asked to take off his watch and
empty his pockets, and he has done it, feeling slightly ridiculous, and the ridiculousness
has already begun to wear off.

He is in Lagos, or Lima, or a market town in Shropshire, or a suburb of Detroit. He is
twenty-three, or fifty-eight. He was proposed by a man he works with, or by his wife's
father, or by a stranger he got talking to. He is not sure, now that he is here, why he
agreed.

The ceremony he is about to undergo was worked out in London, in taverns, by men whose names
are lost, some time in the 1720s. Almost every element of it was in print by 1730 and any
one of us could read it in ten seconds. He has probably not looked. That is part of it too —
a decision not to know, which is itself a kind of participation.

In an hour he will remember the rest of his life.

---

We have taken the building apart.

There is no Egyptian survival in it. The Hermetica was written in Greek in the second
century and Casaubon proved it in 1614. There is no Templar succession; the men who claimed
one voted it down in 1782. There is no Rosicrucian inheritance; the brotherhood was a
pamphlet, and its author called it a jest. The Kabbalah arrived at four removes through a
Latin anthology and an English translation of it, and the tarot was hung on the Tree by a
Frenchman in the 1850s. The Lucifer letter is a forgery whose author confessed in front of
journalists on the nineteenth of April, 1897.

The square is a builder's tool. The Temple is a Renaissance engraving of a biblical
description. The letter G stands for two English words.

And the man in the small room is about to be changed by it anyway.

That is the thing that survives, and I have come to think it is the only thing in this book
that was never manufactured.

---

I have tried, over these chapters, not to take sides in an argument I think is badly
constructed. The question has never been whether Freemasonry is secretly magical or
harmlessly dull. The question is what it *did*, and the answer is that it built something.

Not a doctrine. The Craft has no doctrine and has been criticised for three hundred years by
people who wanted one. Not a philosophy; the philosophies were all imported and most of them
have gone.

It built a **method**: a reliable, transmissible arrangement for taking an ordinary person,
removing his position and his sight, moving him through the dark, extracting a promise in
his own voice, and then giving him light on a prepared image — and it built it so well that
it could be carried in a sea chest to any place on earth and set up in a rented room by men
who had learned it by heart.

That method is the reason there is a Western esoteric tradition in the modern world at all.
The books would not have done it. Books preserve content and lose practice; they sit unread
for centuries; they cannot confer membership or authority or continuity. What the Hermetic
and Kabbalistic and alchemical currents needed, in order to survive the Enlightenment, was a
*body to travel in*, and in the eighteenth century exactly one was available.

Freemasonry gave them one. It did so without intending to, without approving of most of what
was done with it, and in most jurisdictions without noticing.

---

The men who used it wanted it to be old. All of them, without exception, and I have spent a
long time trying to understand why.

The structural reasons are real: antiquity was the only currency of authority in that
culture, and a graded hierarchy requires a top, and if there is no real one it will be
supplied. I have made those arguments and I stand by them.

But I think there is something else, and the last chapter admitted I could not demonstrate
it.

When you assemble a symbolic system — when the twenty-two cards match the twenty-two paths,
when the pillars turn out to stand for the two poles of a philosophy, when the ritual you
have written produces in a stranger an effect you did not quite predict — it does not feel
like invention. It feels like *recognition*. As though the pieces had been fitted before you
arrived and you had merely walked into the room where they were kept.

I have felt it myself, in a small way, writing this book: the moment when three unconnected
things turn out to be one thing, and the sensation is not of having made an argument but of
having found one.

That feeling is, I suspect, what the whole apparatus of ancient wisdom was built to explain.
Not a lie told to recruit members, but an honest attempt to account for an experience — the
experience of making something that feels given.

If that is right, then the men in this book were not primarily deceivers. They were people
who had built something real, felt it working, and reached for the only vocabulary their
century offered to describe a thing that seemed to exceed its maker.

They were wrong about where it came from. They were not wrong that something was there.

---

The door opens.

There is a question, and someone answers it on his behalf, and he is led forward into a room
he cannot see. There is a point at his chest and a question he must answer himself, and he
answers it. He walks in the dark, turning at intervals, learning a space with his feet. He
kneels. He puts his hands on a book he cannot see and says words after another man, and
means them more than he expected to.

And then the light.

It is a square and a pair of compasses and an open book on a table, in an ordinary room,
with ordinary men standing round it in their aprons — and it is the most significant thing
he has ever been shown, and he will be able to describe this moment when he is eighty.

Nothing supernatural has occurred. Nothing ancient has been transmitted. A small number of
people have arranged a room and a sequence of events with great care, using a design refined
over three centuries, and it has worked exactly as designed.

That is not less than the myth. I have come to think it is considerably more.

The Temple was never in Jerusalem, and it was never in Egypt, and it was never under the
Templar preceptory at Rosslyn. It is a plan — a set of proportions, an orientation, a floor
of black and white squares, and a door that you go through in the dark.

It can be laid out anywhere. It has been laid out nearly everywhere.

And it is being laid out tonight, in a room above a shop, for a man who does not yet know
what is about to happen to him, by people who cannot tell him what it means.

---

ROBERT E. LEE RINGLER
