## A NOTE ON METHOD AND OBLIGATION

Three things should be said before the first chapter, because they govern everything
that follows.

**On evidence.** This book makes a great many historical claims, and each of them is
meant to be checkable. Where the evidence is strong, I say so plainly. Where it is
contested, I say who contests it. Where it does not exist, I say that too, and I have
tried never to let a resemblance do the work of a document. The reader will encounter,
repeatedly, sentences that begin *there is no surviving evidence that* — and will, I
hope, come to find them more interesting than the sentences that begin *it has long
been said*.

**On ritual.** Freemasonry is a body of men and women under obligation. I have not
reproduced private ritual material, and I have not invented any. Everything quoted or
described in these pages comes from sources that have been public for between sixty and
three hundred years: printed constitutions, the eighteenth-century exposures, published
catechisms, and the twentieth-century publications of orders that no longer exist. Where
I describe a ceremony, I am describing a documented historical text, not a current
working — and Masonic ritual varies enormously by jurisdiction, by century, and by
degree. No single system described here should be taken as universal. There is no such
thing as *the* Masonic ritual, and one of the pleasures of the subject is discovering
how thoroughly that is so.

**On sympathy.** I am not a debunker. The men and women in this book were, with a few
memorable exceptions, sincere. They believed they had found something; many of them were
changed by it; some of them were changed by things they had built themselves without
knowing it. Treating their claims as historical propositions — which is to say, testing
them — is not a form of contempt. It is the only way I know to take a person seriously
enough to find out what actually happened to them.
