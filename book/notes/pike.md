# NOTE — ALBERT PIKE

**The man.** 1809–1891. Boston-born, Arkansas lawyer, poet, Confederate brigadier
(with a contested war record that the book must state, not skip), Sovereign Grand
Commander of the Scottish Rite Southern Jurisdiction from 1859 to his death. A
formidable autodidact and a compulsive syncretist.

**The book.** *Morals and Dogma* (Charleston, 1871): a vast commentary on the 32
degrees, given to initiates for over a century. Not a ritual. Not doctrine — Pike said
so himself, disclaiming that every Mason must accept his interpretations. Comparative
mythology written by a man reading everything at once.

**The decisive scholarly instrument.** Arturo de Hoyos's annotated edition (2nd
annotated edn., Supreme Council 33° S.J., 2011; 4,000+ critical notes) traces Pike's
borrowings line by line. Large portions are unattributed transcription — **substantially
from Éliphas Lévi**, whose *Dogme et rituel* appeared only fifteen years before. This
single fact restructures the book's Part VI: Pike is not a conduit of ancient wisdom
into America, he is a conduit of *1856 Paris* into America.

**Lucifer.** Pike's genuine passages use the name in its Latin sense — light-bearer,
the morning star — within a comparative discussion of dualism and the history of
religious symbolism. Read in paragraph, they are anti-dogmatic and syncretic. Read in
fragment, they are damning. The book quotes them at length, in context, and lets the
reader see the difference.

**Baphomet.** Pike discusses the figure in the Templar material. The *image* everyone
pictures is Lévi's 1856 drawing, not a Templar artefact, and not Pike's.

**The accusation.** Taxil's forgery (via de la Rive, 1894), confessed 1897 — see
`notes/occult_revival.md` and `research/controversial_claims.md` no. 5.

**The honest verdict.** Pike was not a Luciferian. He was something the accusation
cannot see and the defence rarely admits: a nineteenth-century comparative mythologist
who filled a Masonic structure with everything he had read, including a great deal of
contemporary French occultism, and who thereby made the Scottish Rite the largest
single distributor of esoteric material in American history.
