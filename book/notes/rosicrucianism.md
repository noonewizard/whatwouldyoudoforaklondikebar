# NOTE — ROSICRUCIANISM

**Five distinct things:**
1. The manifestos — *Fama* (1614), *Confessio* (1615), *Chymische Hochzeit* (1616).
2. The seventeenth-century furore: hundreds of responses, pamphlets, and seekers; no
   members ever found. Andreae later called the *Chymical Wedding* a *ludibrium*.
3. The eighteenth-century Gold- und Rosenkreuz: a **real** order, alchemical, politically
   influential in Prussia, requiring Masonic membership (McIntosh, 1992).
4. Nineteenth-century revivals: SRIA (1866), Masonic entry required; Golden Dawn's
   Rosicrucian self-presentation.
5. Twentieth-century bodies claiming ancient descent.

**The inversion at the heart of the subject.** The popular story runs: Rosicrucianism →
Freemasonry. The documented sequence runs the other way. The manifestos describe a
fiction; the first *institutional* Rosicrucianism postdates Grand Lodge and uses
Masonry as its recruitment base. **Freemasonry did not descend from Rosicrucianism; it
carried Rosicrucianism into the modern world.**

**Why the manifestos mattered anyway.** They established a template — an invisible
brotherhood, hidden superiors, a secret founder's tomb, a coming reformation — that
every later order would reuse. Von Hund's Unknown Superiors, Blavatsky's Masters,
Westcott's Fräulein Sprengel, and the Secret Chiefs of the Golden Dawn are all children
of a text whose author called it a joke.
