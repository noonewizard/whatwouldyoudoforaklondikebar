# APPENDIX B
## A Short History of the Major Masonic Rites

A rite is a system of degrees. This appendix summarises the principal ones, with attention
to a single question: at what point, if any, did each acquire content that can properly be
called esoteric, and on whose authority?

Dates given are dates of documentary attestation, not of claimed origin. Where a body's own
traditional account differs from the documentary record, both are noted.

---

## B.1 — THE CRAFT (BLUE LODGE)

**Three degrees: Entered Apprentice, Fellow Craft, Master Mason.**

The documentary record begins with the Scottish lodge minutes of the late sixteenth century
(Aitchison's Haven, 1599; Mary's Chapel, Edinburgh, 1599) and the Schaw Statutes of 1598–99.
The English record of non-operative admission begins with Elias Ashmole's diary entry for 16
October 1646. The first Grand Lodge was constituted in London; the traditional date of 1717
rests on Anderson's 1738 account, and the dating has been reopened by recent scholarship
favoring 1721 — a dispute set out in the preceding volume and summarised in Chapter 3.

The third degree is not attested before the 1720s. This is the single most important
chronological fact in the book: the Hiramic legend, on which almost every esoteric
interpretation of Masonry depends, appears roughly a century after the earliest lodge
minutes and cannot therefore be evidence of what those lodges were doing.

The Antients/Moderns schism (1751–1813) divided English Masonry over ritual practice and the
status of the Royal Arch. The Union of 1813 produced the formula discussed in Chapter 50.

**Esoteric content at formation: none demonstrable.** Moral instruction through architectural
metaphor, with scriptural framing. See Chapters 28–30.

---

## B.2 — THE HOLY ROYAL ARCH

Attested from the 1740s. Its early history is contested and its relation to the Craft differs
by constitution: in England it completes the third degree; in Ireland and Scotland and in
American practice the arrangements differ materially.

It supplies what the Craft withholds — a recovery of the lost word. Chapter 50 treats the
structural significance of this.

**Esoteric content:** more than the Craft. The vault, the recovery, and the divine-name
material give later commentators considerably more to work with, and they have worked with
it. Whether the eighteenth-century compilers intended any of it is not established.

---

## B.3 — THE SCOTTISH RITE (ANCIENT AND ACCEPTED SCOTTISH RITE)

Thirty-three degrees, of which the Craft degrees are the first three.

Its lineage runs through the Rite of Perfection (twenty-five degrees, mid-eighteenth-century
France) to the Supreme Council at Charleston, South Carolina, in **1801** — the origin of
the number thirty-three, which has no ancient warrant whatever and is the by-product of a
degree count fixed two years into the nineteenth century.

Albert Pike's revision of the rituals and his *Morals and Dogma* (1871) imported a very large
quantity of comparative-religion and esoteric material, drawn substantially from Éliphas Lévi
and from the nineteenth-century Orientalist literature. This is the single largest injection
of occult content into any Masonic body, it is datable, it is attributable to a named
individual, and it is nineteenth-century. Chapter 41 treats it in detail.

**Esoteric content: substantial, and demonstrably added after 1850.**

---

## B.4 — THE YORK RITE (AMERICAN)

A grouping rather than a single rite: Chapter (Royal Arch), Council (Cryptic degrees), and
Commandery (orders of chivalry, including the Knights Templar). Consolidated in the United
States in the nineteenth century.

The Templar orders are a matter of frequent confusion. They are eighteenth- and
nineteenth-century chivalric *creations*; they are not, and do not by their own official
accounts claim to be, an organizational continuation of the medieval order suppressed in
1312. The lineage claims belong to the fringe literature, not to the bodies themselves.

---

## B.5 — THE RITE OF STRICT OBSERVANCE

Germany, 1750s–1780s, associated with Karl Gotthelf von Hund. Built on the assertion of
Templar descent through "Unknown Superiors" whom Hund could never produce. Investigated and
effectively dissolved at the Convent of Wilhelmsbad (1782), which rejected the Templar
claim.

Its importance is as a case study: a Masonic body organized entirely around an unverifiable
transmission claim, examined by its own members using something close to historical method,
and abandoned when the claim failed. Chapter 42 uses it as a control on how Masonic bodies
have actually handled origin claims.

---

## B.6 — THE RECTIFIED SCOTTISH RITE AND THE ÉLUS COHENS

Jean-Baptiste Willermoz reorganised the Strict Observance material into the Rectified
Scottish Rite after Wilhelmsbad, retaining a Christian-mystical orientation. Martinez de
Pasqually's Ordre des Chevaliers Maçons Élus Coëns de l'Univers (from the 1760s) was
genuinely theurgic — it practiced invocation — and is therefore one of the very few bodies in
this appendix where operative magical practice is documented rather than alleged.

This matters for the argument of the book. When occult practice enters Masonic-adjacent
organizations, it does so visibly, with named founders, dated documents, and explicit
instructions. Its absence from the Craft degrees is not an absence of evidence; it is
evidence of absence of that kind of material. See Chapter 30.

---

## B.7 — LATER AND ADJACENT BODIES

| Body | Date | Relation to Craft Masonry | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Societas Rosicruciana in Anglia (SRIA) | 1866–67 | Membership restricted to Master Masons; not a Masonic body | Supplied personnel and material to the Golden Dawn |
| Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn | 1887–88 | **Not Masonic** | Founders were Masons; borrowed grade structure and ritual form. Its founding warrant was forged — established in the preceding volume |
| Ordo Templi Orientis | c. 1902 onward | **Not Masonic**; regular bodies do not recognize it | Adopted degree structure; Crowley's reworking from 1912 |
| Co-Masonry (Le Droit Humain, 1893) | 1893 | Initiates women; not recognized by most regular grand lodges | Theosophical connections through Besant; Chapter 43 |
| Memphis-Misraïm | 19th c. | Irregular in most jurisdictions | Very high degree counts; heavy Egyptological framing |

The distinction drawn throughout this book is between bodies that *are* Masonic, bodies whose
members were Masons, and bodies that borrowed Masonic form. Conflating the three is the most
common single error in the popular literature, and it is the mechanism by which occult
practice that Masonry does not contain is routinely attributed to it.

---

**On jurisdictional variation:** none of the above should be read as describing a universal
practice. Rites differ between constitutions, working differs between lodges within a
constitution, and recognition differs between grand lodges. Where this book has needed a
specific ritual detail it has said which tradition it is describing.
