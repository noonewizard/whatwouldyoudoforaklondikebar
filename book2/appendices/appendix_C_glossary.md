# APPENDIX C
## Glossary of Esoteric and Technical Terms

Terms are defined as this book uses them. Where a term is contested among scholars, the
contest is noted rather than resolved by fiat. Where a term carries a popular meaning
different from its technical one, both are given.

---

### Esoteric and historical terms

**Alchemy.** A body of practice concerned with the transformation of substances, pursued
from antiquity through the early modern period, with a parallel tradition treating the
operations as figures for inner transformation. The two strands are not separable in the
primary sources as cleanly as later commentators assume. See Chapter 29.

**Apophenia.** The perception of meaningful patterns in random or unrelated material. Not a
pathology in itself; a normal function of a pattern-detecting cognitive system operating
without a control. The methodological problem it creates is the subject of Chapter 38.

**Correspondences.** The doctrine that items in different orders of being (planets, metals,
plants, organs, letters) stand in fixed relation. Central to Renaissance magic and to
nineteenth-century occultism. Its presence in a system is diagnostic: the Craft degrees do
not use it, and the later rites do. See Chapters 29, 41.

**Esotericism (Western).** As used by Faivre, Hanegraaff and the academic field that has
grown since the 1990s: a family of currents in European thought — Hermetism, Christian
Kabbalah, alchemy, Paracelsianism, Rosicrucianism, Naturphilosophie, modern occultism —
studied historically rather than doctrinally. The field's own boundaries are disputed; see
Chapter 27 and the historiography file.

**Gematria.** Assignment of numerical values to letters, and the interpretation of words
sharing a value as related. A real technique within Jewish exegesis with its own rules and
constraints. Its uncontrolled application to English words is treated in Chapter 38 and
Appendix F.

**Hermetism / Hermeticism.** *Hermetism* properly denotes the Greek Hermetic texts of the
second and third centuries CE and their tradition. *Hermeticism* is often used more loosely
for the broader early-modern current descending from their 1463 translation by Ficino. This
book keeps the distinction; much of the literature does not.

**Kabbalah / Cabala / Qabalah.** Jewish mystical tradition (Kabbalah); its Christian
adaptation from Pico della Mirandola onward (Cabala); and the Hermetic system constructed by
nineteenth- and twentieth-century occultists, chiefly through Lévi and the Golden Dawn
(Qabalah). Three distinguishable things. Conflating them is how a four-link transmission
chain to Freemasonry is manufactured; see Chapter 41.

**Notarikon.** Interpretation of a word as an acronym of a phrase, or the reverse. A
technique of Jewish exegesis.

**Occultism.** Specifically the movement beginning with Éliphas Lévi in the 1850s and
running through the Theosophical Society, the Golden Dawn, and their successors. Not a
synonym for esotericism, and not a description of anything demonstrably present in
eighteenth-century Masonry. Chapter 40.

**Rosicrucianism.** The furore following the *Fama Fraternitatis* (1614), *Confessio* (1615)
and *Chymical Wedding* (1616), and the currents that grew from it. The original order
described in the manifestos has never been shown to have existed. Later bodies using the
name are eighteenth-century and later.

**Temurah.** Systematic letter-substitution (e.g. Atbash) within Jewish exegesis.

**Theurgy.** Ritual operation intended to produce an effect through the action or presence
of a divine power, as distinct from *thaumaturgy* (wonder-working) and from moral allegory.
Documented in the Élus Coëns; not documented in the Craft degrees. Chapter 30.

### Ritual-studies and cognitive terms

**Communitas.** Victor Turner's term for the undifferentiated bond among those passing
through a liminal phase together. Contested: critics note that it is easier to assert than to
measure. Chapter 10.

**Doctrinal mode / imagistic mode.** Harvey Whitehouse's two attractors in religious
transmission: frequent, low-arousal, routinised practice versus rare, high-arousal,
sensorily vivid practice. Masonic initiation sits awkwardly between them, which is analysed
rather than smoothed over in Chapter 34.

**Flashbulb memory.** Vivid, confidently held memory of a surprising event. Note: high
confidence does not track accuracy, and this matters for autobiographical testimony about
initiation. Chapter 35.

**Liminality.** Van Gennep's middle phase — separation, transition, incorporation — as
developed by Turner. Chapter 9.

**Ritualization.** Catherine Bell's term, and her argument that "ritual" is an analytic
category imposed by scholars rather than a natural kind. Adopted in this book as a
discipline: it forbids the move from *this resembles ritual X* to *this descends from ritual
X*. Chapter 6.

**Ritual criticism.** Ronald Grimes's programme for evaluating rites as performances that can
succeed or fail. Chapter 47.

**Symbolic compression.** Used in this book for the property of a symbol that carries more
meaning than any paraphrase of it recovers, and that can be re-read at successive stages of a
member's career. Defined and defended in Chapter 32.

**Tacit knowledge.** Polanyi's category for what is known but cannot be fully stated. Relevant
because ritual instruction is largely of this kind. Chapter 32.

### Terms of art in this book

**Anchored / unanchored symbol.** A meaning the ritual itself supplies, versus one imported
by a later commentator. Appendix A applies the distinction throughout.

**The career, not the ceremony.** The book's central methodological claim: that the unit of
analysis for initiatory effect is the decades of membership, not the evening of initiation.
Chapters 6, 46.

**Over-determination.** The diagnostic condition in which a symbolic reading explains
everything and therefore excludes nothing. Treated as a warning sign rather than a strength.
Chapter 38.

**Parallel is not descent.** The rule that structural resemblance between two systems is not
evidence that one derives from the other, absent a documented transmission. Applied
throughout; stated formally in Chapter 27 and tested in Chapter 45.

**The structural secret.** The finding that Masonry's central secret is not a withheld
content but the discovery that there is no withheld content of the kind sought — and that
this discovery is itself the instruction. Chapters 36, 50.
