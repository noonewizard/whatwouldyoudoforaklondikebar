## F.1 — THE GEMATRIA CONTROL TEST: COMPLETE FIGURES

**Procedure, stated before the calculation was run.** Simple English gematria, A = 1 through
Z = 26. Sum the letter values of each word. Two sets of twenty words: one drawn from Masonic
symbolism, one from household objects and root vegetables. Both sets fixed before any value
was computed. Two questions asked of each set: how many pairs within the set share a value,
and how many words land on a number conventionally treated as significant (3, 5, 7, 33, 47,
72, 93).

The calculation was performed by machine and the output is transcribed here unaltered.

### Result

The yield is identical: three internal pairs and one significant-number hit on each side.
The Masonic set produced no more coincidence than the root vegetables did.

### Two further coincidences, offered as a second demonstration

Running the two sets *against each other* — which is exactly the move a cross-traditional
correspondence argument makes — yields two more matches:

- MASON = 62 = TESCO
- TUBALCAIN = 83 = BISCUIT

Neither means anything. Both would look impressive in a book that reported only the hits.
That is the entire point of the exercise: the procedure generates matches at a predictable
rate from any sufficiently long list of words, and a finding is only a finding if the same
procedure applied to material that cannot contain the pattern comes back empty. Here it does
not come back empty. It comes back with the same catch.

### The arithmetic of why

Twenty words produce 190 possible pairs. English gematria values for common words cluster
roughly between 30 and 110 — a range of some eighty numbers. On a crude uniform assumption
one expects on the order of two matching pairs per set by chance alone, and three is
unremarkable. Against eight "significant" numbers and twenty words, one hit is likewise
close to expectation. Nothing in either column requires explanation.

The reader who wishes to test this further should take any twenty words to hand — the
contents of a kitchen drawer, the names of railway stations — and repeat the procedure. The
result will be the same, which is the only reliable finding gematria of this kind produces.

## F.2 — THE FOUR QUESTIONS FOR ANY DECIPHERMENT CLAIM

These are the questions Chapter 38 sets out. They are listed here in a form the reader can
apply to material this book does not cover.

1. **Was the procedure specified in advance?** A decipherment method chosen after the
   answer is known is not a method; it is a description of the answer.
2. **How large is the search space?** How many readings were available, and how many were
   tried and discarded? A claim that reports one hit and no misses has reported nothing.
3. **Does it survive a control?** Run the identical procedure on material that cannot
   contain the message. If the control yields comparable results, the procedure is
   generating, not detecting.
4. **Does it predict?** Does the reading entail something not already assumed, which can be
   checked independently? A decipherment that explains only what prompted it is unfalsifiable.

A claim failing question 1 or 3 should be set aside regardless of how striking its output is.

Evidence levels are those of Appendix E (E1 strongest, E6 weakest).

Its existence is not in dispute; its significance is routinely overstated. |
| "Letter and halve it" and similar ritual instructions constitute a concealed cipher system | **Misread** | E4 | These are mnemonic and ritual-delivery conventions for communicating a word in a controlled manner. No evidence that they encode a second message. |

| Numerological significance of 3, 5, 7, 33 in Masonic structure | **Partly historical, partly projected** | E3 | Three, five and seven are explicit in ritual and require no numerological reading. The prominence of 33 derives from the Scottish Rite's degree count (1801), not from antiquity. |

## F.4 — WHAT A GOOD CIPHER CLAIM WOULD LOOK LIKE

For completeness, and because this appendix has been almost entirely negative, it is worth
stating what would change the picture.

A persuasive claim would identify a Masonic text of known date and provenance; state a
decipherment procedure in advance and in full; apply it to a control corpus of comparable
material from the same period and show that the control yields nothing; and produce a reading
that entails a checkable fact — a name, a date, a location — not already contained in the
material that prompted the search.

No claim examined for this book meets that standard. That is a statement about the claims
examined, not a prediction that none could ever be made.
