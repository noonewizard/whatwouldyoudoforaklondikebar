# APPENDIX G
## Primary Sources: What Each One Can and Cannot Establish

Full citations are in the bibliography. This appendix gives the working notes — what each
body of material is good for, and what it cannot be used to prove. Readers who intend to
check the book's arguments should read this before the bibliography, because the constraints
listed here are what most of the book's caution derives from.

---

## G.1 — TIER ONE: WHAT THE CEREMONY CONSISTED OF

**The Scottish catechisms (Edinburgh Register House MS 1696; Chetwode Crawley MS c. 1700;
Kevan MS c. 1714).**
*Establish:* the earliest recoverable ritual — two degrees, a word, a grip, a
question-and-answer sequence learned by heart. The dialogic form is already in place in 1696
and precedes everything else in this book.
*Do not establish:* any drama, any third degree, any interpretation.

**The exposures (1723–1762, English and French): Prichard, *Masonry Dissected* (1730);
*Three Distinct Knocks* (1760); *Jachin and Boaz* (1762); the French *Réception* literature.**
*Establish:* ritual content at datable moments; the emergence of the third degree between
1724 and 1730; the decade-by-decade accretion of symbolism; the physical conditions —
hoodwink, cable-tow, divestment, circumambulation, obligation, restoration of light.
*Caution:* commercial, sometimes hostile, occasionally padded. Use comparatively. Where three
independent exposures agree, the element is probably real.
*Also establish, powerfully:* that publication did nothing to the institution. Masonry's
secrets have been in print for three centuries and the lodges continued. This is the central
fact of Chapter 36.

**Anderson, *Constitutions* (1723; 1738).**
*Establish:* official self-presentation; the religious formula; the traditional history; the
attribution of the 47th Proposition **[PAGE VERIFICATION REQUIRED]**.
*Do not establish:* what happened in lodges.

**Lodge minutes, membership registers, and disciplinary records.**
*Establish:* attendance, finance, charity, exclusion, lapse. **The only Tier-One evidence
bearing on whether the ritual had behavioural consequences**, and therefore the most
important material in the book for Chapters 44 and 46.
*Limitation, stated frankly:* this study worked from published transactions and secondary
summaries of lodge records rather than from archival originals. The argument of Chapter 44
would be substantially strengthened, or corrected, by systematic archival work. See §G.5.

## G.2 — TIER TWO: WHAT PARTICIPANTS SAID IT DID

This is the book's distinctive corpus and its thinnest.

**William Preston, *Illustrations of Masonry* (1772, and later editions to 1812).**
The single richest source here: a participant attempting to describe the working of an
apparatus a century before anyone had a vocabulary for it.
*Establish:* what an informed eighteenth-century Mason believed the ceremony conveyed.
*Do not establish:* that it conveyed it.
*Caution:* the editions differ substantially; cite the edition.

**Masonic sermons and orations, eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.**
*Establish:* the public account of the Craft's purpose — moral improvement, charity, civil
order. Their very unremarkableness is evidence: a body engaged in occult transformation does
not preach like this.

**Monitors and lectures (Webb and after).**
*Establish:* the American interpretive tradition and its divergence from Preston's.

**Oliver, Mackey, Pike, Waite, Leadbeater.**
*Establish:* successive interpretive layers, each datable.
**The rule of direction applies** (§G.4). Leadbeater (1926) is a special case: an explicit
theory of ritual efficacy stated in metaphysical terms — this book's question, answered by
someone who did not think it needed evidence.

**What is conspicuously absent.** No eighteenth- or nineteenth-century Masonic source known
to this study describes trance, ecstasy, dissociation, or visionary experience in initiation.
The testimony is sober throughout. That absence is itself a finding, reported as one in
Chapter 33.

## G.3 — TIER THREE: COMPARATIVE AND ESOTERIC

**Esoteric primary texts** (the *Hermetica*; Agrippa; Knorr von Rosenroth; Lévi; Mathers;
Crowley).
*Establish:* what was available to read, when, and in what language; and what later
practitioners believed Masonic ritual was doing.
*Do not establish:* that any Mason read them. **Availability is not transmission.**

**Ancient mystery sources.**
*Establish:* considerably less than the comparative literature assumes. The fundamental
limit is that the Eleusinian secret was kept: there is no participant's account of what
happened inside the Telesterion. Every reconstruction of what initiates *experienced* is
extrapolation, and Chapter 31 says so in the body text rather than in a footnote.

**Cipher material.**
*Establish:* that the pigpen substitution was used in Masonic record-keeping; that Atbash,
gematria, notarikon and temurah are genuine techniques within their own traditions.
*Do not establish:* that any of them carried Masonic doctrine. See Appendix F.

## G.4 — THE RULE OF DIRECTION

**A source is primary for its own period and secondary — often unreliably — for any earlier
one.**

| Source | On its own period | On an earlier period |
|---|---|---|
| Preston (1772) | Primary | Level D speculation |
| Pike (1871) on American Masonry | Primary | Level D on Egypt |
| Leadbeater (1926) on Co-Masonic belief | Primary | Type E, reported not endorsed |

This rule disposes of a large fraction of the literature at a stroke, and it is worth stating
why that is legitimate rather than convenient. A nineteenth-century writer describing what
Masonry meant to nineteenth-century Masons is a witness. The same writer describing what it
meant in 1730 is a reader of the same documents available to us, with fewer of them and no
critical apparatus. His conclusions deserve consideration; his authority does not transfer.

---

## G.5 — THE EVIDENTIARY ASYMMETRY

Stated plainly, because it shapes everything.

**Excellent evidence for what the ceremony was.** Published, dated, cross-checkable, in two
languages, across four decades.

**Moderate evidence for what participants believed it did.** Preston, the sermons, the
monitors, the later interpreters.

**Almost no evidence for what it actually did to anyone.** No self-reports of the kind a
psychologist would want. No measurements. No controls. No comparison group. No longitudinal
data of any sort.

The honesty of this book depends on never letting the richness of the first category
disguise the poverty of the third. Where a conclusion in Part XI looks thinner than the
reader expected, that is why.
