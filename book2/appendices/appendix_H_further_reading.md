# APPENDIX H
## Recommended Further Reading

Annotated, and organized by what a reader might want to do next. Entries marked **†** are
where to start in that category. Full citations are in the bibliography; this appendix gives
the reasons.

A note on selection: several widely read books are listed here with warnings rather than
omitted. A reader who goes looking will find them anyway, and it is more useful to say what
is wrong with them than to pretend they do not exist.

---

## H.1 — IF YOU WANT THE HISTORY OF FREEMASONRY DONE PROPERLY

**† David Stevenson, *The Origins of Freemasonry: Scotland's Century, 1590–1710* (Cambridge,
1988).** The book that moved the argument onto documentary ground. Stevenson's Scottish
thesis is contested in its details and has reshaped the field regardless. Read it before
anything else.

**Margaret Jacob, *Living the Enlightenment* (Oxford, 1991).** Masonry as sociability and
civil society — the best account of what lodges were *for* in the terms their members would
have recognized.

**Andrew Prescott and Susan Mitchell Sommers**, on the dating of the first Grand Lodge. Their
reopening of the 1717/1721 question is the most consequential recent revision.
**[SOURCE VERIFICATION REQUIRED]** — this study could not access the *Ars Quatuor Coronatorum*
printing directly; the open-access journal *REHMLAC+* carries related work.

***Ars Quatuor Coronatorum*, the transactions of Quatuor Coronati Lodge No. 2076 (1886– ).**
The "authentic school" of Masonic history. Uneven across a century and a half, and the only
sustained body of critical scholarship produced from inside the institution.

**Jan Snoek**, on ritual transmission and on women's Masonry. Technical, careful, and the
best corrective to the assumption that Masonic ritual is one thing.

## H.2 — IF YOU WANT TO UNDERSTAND RITUAL

**† Arnold van Gennep, *The Rites of Passage* (1909; Eng. trans. 1960).** Still the
indispensable starting point. Separation, liminality, incorporation.

**† Victor Turner, *The Ritual Process* (1969).** Liminality and communitas developed into a
theory. Read alongside the criticism — communitas is easier to assert than to demonstrate.

**Catherine Bell, *Ritual Theory, Ritual Practice* (1992)** and *Ritual: Perspectives and
Dimensions* (1997). The most important corrective in the field: "ritual" is a category
scholars impose, not a natural kind, and the resemblances that comparison turns up may be
artefacts of the comparing. This book's discipline derives substantially from Bell.

**Jonathan Z. Smith, *To Take Place* (1987).** Sacrality as emplacement. Short, difficult,
and the source of the argument in Chapter 12.

**Ronald Grimes, *Ritual Criticism* (1990).** Rites can fail. A framework for saying how.

**Mircea Eliade** — read with caution. Enormously influential, methodologically permissive,
and the source of much of the comparative overreach this book argues against. Useful for
understanding the literature; not reliable as evidence.

**Sir James Frazer, *The Golden Bough* (1890).** Read as a historical document. Its method —
resemblance as descent — is precisely what Chapter 45 is written against.

## H.3 — IF YOU WANT THE COGNITIVE SCIENCE

**† Harvey Whitehouse, *Modes of Religiosity* (2004)** and *Arguments and Icons* (2000). The
imagistic/doctrinal distinction, and the best available framework for asking what rare
high-arousal rites do to transmission.

**Robert McCauley and E. Thomas Lawson, *Bringing Ritual to Mind* (2002).** Ritual form and
cognitive representation.

**Pascal Boyer and Pierre Liénard**, on ritualized behavior and hazard-precaution systems.

**Dimitris Xygalatas** and colleagues, on field experiments with high-ordeal ritual. The
closest thing in existence to measurement of the effects this book discusses, conducted on
populations quite unlike Masonic ones — which is itself worth thinking about.

**Michael Polanyi, *Personal Knowledge* (1958)** and *The Tacit Dimension* (1966). Why some
instruction cannot be given in propositions.

**A warning about two famous findings.** Aronson and Mills (1959) on severity of initiation,
and Meyers-Levy and Zhu (2007) on ceiling height, are both cited constantly in the popular
Masonic literature. Both are weaker than their reputation: the first has a contested
replication record, the second is a priming effect of the kind the replication crisis has
treated most harshly. Use them as hypotheses, not as support.

## H.4 — IF YOU WANT WESTERN ESOTERICISM AS A FIELD

**† Wouter Hanegraaff, *Western Esotericism: A Guide for the Perplexed* (2013).** The clearest
entry point to the academic study.

**Wouter Hanegraaff, *Esotericism and the Academy* (2012).** How the field came to be, and how
"esotericism" was constructed as a category of rejected knowledge.

**Antoine Faivre, *Access to Western Esotericism* (1994).** The four-plus-two criteria.
Foundational and much criticised; read the criticism too.

**Frances Yates, *Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition* (1964)** and *The Rosicrucian
Enlightenment* (1972). Brilliant, transformative, and substantially superseded. The "Yates
thesis" is no longer accepted in the form she stated it. Read her for the questions.

**Julian Strube**, on Lévi's political and religious context. Essential for understanding why
"magic" changed meaning in the 1850s — the hinge on which Chapters 40 and 49 turn.

## H.5 — IF YOU WANT TO LEARN THE METHOD

This is the shortest section and the one most likely to be useful outside this subject.

**† Brendan McKay, Dror Bar-Natan, Maya Bar-Hillel and Gil Kalai, "Solving the Bible Code
Puzzle," *Statistical Science* 14 (1999), 150–73.** Open access. Twenty pages that teach
more about evidence than most methodology textbooks: a striking claimed pattern, a control,
and the collapse of the claim. The model for Chapter 38 and Appendix F.

**Robert Todd Carroll, *The Skeptic's Dictionary***, on apophenia, confirmation bias, and
retrofitting — for definitions and worked examples.

**On the replication crisis generally:** the Open Science Collaboration's 2015
*Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science* is the standard reference for why
Chapter 33's caution is warranted.

## H.6 — WHAT NOT TO RELY ON, AND WHY

Named, because vagueness here would be cowardice.

**Albert Pike, *Morals and Dogma* (1871).** A primary source of the first importance for
nineteenth-century American Masonic thought, and worthless as history of anything earlier.
Pike's sources were Lévi and the Orientalist literature of his own century.

**Manly P. Hall, *The Secret Teachings of All Ages* (1928).** Beautiful, encyclopedic, and
without apparatus. Almost nothing in it can be traced to a source.

**C. W. Leadbeater, *The Hidden Life in Freemasonry* (1926).** Valuable as evidence of what
Theosophically inclined Co-Masons believed in the 1920s. Not evidence of anything about the
eighteenth century.

**Michael Baigent, Richard Leigh and Henry Lincoln; Christopher Knight and Robert Lomas.**
The Templar-descent and Rosslyn literature. The chains fail at the same documentary gap
every time. Discussed in the preceding volume.

**Léo Taxil.** Included here only so the reader knows: the entire "Palladism" corpus was a
hoax, confessed by its author in 1897, and it continues to circulate.

**Anything on the subject that cites no sources.** The rule is not a snobbery about
footnotes. A claim without a citation cannot be checked, and a claim that cannot be checked
is not knowledge, whatever else it may be.
