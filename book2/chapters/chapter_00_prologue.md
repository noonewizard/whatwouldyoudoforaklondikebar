# PROLOGUE
## The Candidate

He is sitting in a small room with the door shut, and he is not wearing his own coat.

I want to be exact about what I am doing here, because this book is going to ask the reader
to distinguish between evidence and reconstruction on almost every page, and it would be
dishonest to blur the line in the first paragraph. The man in that room is a composite. He
is assembled from documents — printed exposures, catechisms, lodge minutes, the lecture
systems — and every physical detail I give him is attested in a published source. But he is
not a person. No single candidate is described in the record from beginning to end, and
nobody who went through this wrote down what it was like with the kind of detail a historian
would want.

So: a composite, built from documented practice, and labeled as one. That is the most any
honest account can offer, and it is more than enough.

He has been asked to empty his pockets. Coins, keys, a watch if he has one — anything of
metal or value, handed over to a stranger and set aside. He has been asked to loosen certain
garments, and he has done it, feeling faintly ridiculous, and the ridiculousness has already
started to wear off, which surprises him. One of his knees is bare. One foot is out of its
shoe. His left breast is uncovered.

He can hear them through the wall. Chairs scraping on boards. A voice saying something in a
register that is not conversation. A pause, then several voices together, then silence, then
a knock — three knocks, evenly spaced, which he will understand later and does not now.

Nobody has told him what is going to happen. That is not an oversight. He was told, when he
asked, that it was better not to know, and that everyone goes through the same thing, and
that he should not worry. The people who told him that were friendly and completely
unhelpful, and he noticed at the time that they seemed to be enjoying themselves.

He has been waiting about twenty minutes.

---

Everything in the previous four paragraphs is a mechanism.

The removal of metal does two things at once: it strips the markers of rank and wealth, and
it gives him an early, small, entirely voluntary act of submission to perform. The partial
undress is more effective than complete undress would be, because a man who is half-dressed
is continuously aware of it in a way that a man who is naked stops being after a minute. The
noise through the wall supplies the one thing the room otherwise lacks, which is evidence
that something orderly and consequential is happening a few feet away without him. The
refusal to tell him anything is not secrecy in any useful sense — the whole ceremony had
been available in print for decades by the time most of the men in this book underwent it —
but it guarantees that he cannot predict, and prediction is the thing that ritual most
reliably removes.

And the waiting is doing more work than all of it. Twenty minutes is long enough for a
person to run out of thoughts about the situation and begin simply attending to it.

None of this was designed by anyone, so far as I have been able to establish, and one of
this book's recurring problems is what to call a thing that works like an instrument and
was not built like one. But it is a machine. It has parts, the parts do identifiable jobs,
and when the parts are assembled in this order the output is remarkably consistent across
three centuries, six continents, and men and women who had almost nothing else in common.

What comes out of the room is a person who will remember this evening when he is eighty.

---

The question this book asks is not what the ceremony means.

That question has an enormous literature and most of it is useless, not because the answers
are wrong but because the question cannot be settled. A symbol means what its holder takes
it to mean, and the Masonic system has never authorised an interpretation of anything. Ask
what the square means and you will get an answer from 1730, a different one from 1772,
another from 1871, and a fourth from whichever member of the lodge you happen to ask. All of
them are entitled to theirs.

The question I want to ask is harder and, I think, answerable in a way the other is not.

**What does the ritual do?**

Not symbolize. *Do.* To attention, to memory, to identity, to a person's relationship with a
group and with authority and with his own death. The ceremony is a sequence of physical and
informational conditions imposed on a human nervous system for about an hour, and human
nervous systems have been studied. Some of what happens in that room is recoverable.

Much of it is not, and I will be saying so constantly. But the difference between "what does
it mean" and "what does it do" is the difference between an argument that can run for three
centuries without resolution — as this one has — and an argument that can be brought up
against evidence.

---

There is a prior question, and the man in the small room raises it without knowing.

In about forty minutes he is going to be killed.

Not really, and not in the first degree — the drama I am thinking of belongs to the third,
and he will not reach it for a year or more. But he will get there, and when he does he will
be laid out on the floor of a room in the character of a murdered man, and then raised from
it, and told that he has been brought from darkness to light and from death to life.

Why does our species keep doing this?

Because we do. Not only here. A Christian is buried in water and comes up new. A novice
enters religion, takes a different name, and is described as dead to the world. A Sufi
initiate seeks annihilation in order to subsist. Recruits are broken down in order to be
rebuilt. And across the ancient Mediterranean, for the better part of two millennia, people
walked to Eleusis and did something in a windowless hall that none of them ever wrote down.

The temptation, faced with a pattern that wide, is to conclude that these are all the same
thing — that there is one ancient initiatory tradition and Freemasonry is a branch of it.
That temptation has produced more bad history than any other idea in this subject, and the
previous volume spent three hundred pages dismantling the particular versions of it that
attach to the Craft. There is no Egyptian descent. There is no Templar succession. The
Rosicrucian ancestry runs backward. The documented line into Freemasonry comes from the
medieval building trade, and the documented line out of it goes to the Victorian occultists,
who took the apparatus and filled it with material they had bought in second-hand
bookshops.

But dismantling the false genealogy leaves the real question standing, and standing more
starkly, because now it cannot be answered by descent.

If these traditions are not related, why do they look alike?

The answer this book will work toward is that they are not variants of one tradition. They
are independent solutions to the same problem — and the problem is that human beings are
very hard to change, and that certain arrangements of darkness, restriction, ordeal, oath
and revelation change them anyway.

That is an unglamorous proposition. It is also, if it holds, a more interesting one than any
secret, because it would mean that what these traditions share is not a message but an
engineering discovery, arrived at repeatedly by people who had never heard of each other.

---

The door opens.

Somebody takes his arm. He is going to be blindfolded now, and a rope will be put around his
neck, and he will be led into a room he cannot see, among men he cannot see, to be asked a
question he cannot answer.

We are going to follow him the whole way in, and then the whole way out again, and at every
step I am going to stop and ask what the record actually shows.

It shows a great deal more than I expected when I started. It shows less than I wanted.

And what it shows most clearly is that the engine was finished and running by about 1730,
and that nobody had the language to describe how it worked until almost two hundred years
later — which means that every person who tried to explain this ceremony between those dates
was doing something rather remarkable. They were describing a mechanism they could feel
operating on them, using the only words available, which were the words of allegory and
antiquity and hidden wisdom.

They were not writing bad psychology. They were writing the best phenomenology anyone could
manage before there was such a thing.

We should read them accordingly.
