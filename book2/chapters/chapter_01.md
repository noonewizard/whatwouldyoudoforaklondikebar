# PART I — THE THRESHOLD

# CHAPTER ONE
## Before the Door

The minute books are dull, and their dullness is the point.

Open the records of almost any eighteenth-century lodge and you will find the same entries
in the same order, month after month: the lodge opened; the minutes of the last meeting were
read and confirmed; a candidate was proposed by one member and seconded by another; a
ballot was taken; the candidate was found clear; he was initiated; accounts were presented;
the lodge closed and the brethren dined. Sums are recorded. Absences are noted. Men are
excluded for non-payment with a regularity that suggests the problem was chronic.[^1]

Nothing in these books describes an experience. They are the paperwork of an association
that meets, admits, charges and eats.

And yet somewhere in that sequence — between *proposed* and *initiated* — is a gap of
several weeks in which a particular kind of anticipation was manufactured, and the manufacture
is the subject of this chapter.

---

### The interval

A man did not walk into a lodge and ask to join.

He was proposed, by somebody who already belonged and who was staking his own standing on
the recommendation. The proposal was made at one meeting and voted on at a later one, which
means that between the moment a man agreed to be put forward and the moment he was admitted
there was an interval, usually of weeks, in which he was neither outside nor in.

During that interval he was balloted for in his absence. In most workings the ballot was
secret and unanimity or near-unanimity was required; a single adverse vote could stop him,
and nobody would tell him who had cast it or why. He would simply not be admitted.

He also paid. Initiation fees in the eighteenth century were not trivial for a man of the
middling sort, and they were payable in advance of any benefit he could evaluate.

Three things are already true of him before the ceremony begins, and all three are
structural rather than accidental.

**He has been judged by people he cannot question.** The decision was taken in a room he
could not enter, by a process he could not observe, on grounds he will never learn.

**He has paid for something he cannot assess.** This is not fraud; every apprenticeship,
every profession, every university has worked the same way. But it does mean that by the time
he arrives he has already committed resources on trust.

**He has been made to wait.** And the waiting is not empty — he knows what is happening to
him is being decided, and he does not know how.

By the evening of his initiation, the lodge has not yet done anything to him. It has,
however, arranged for him to arrive in a particular condition: committed, uninformed,
socially exposed, and paying attention.

---

### What he knew

Here the record is better than one might expect, and it is worth being careful, because the
popular picture of the eighteenth-century candidate as an innocent about to be astonished is
substantially wrong.

The Masonic ceremonies had been in print since 1723. *A Mason's Examination* appeared in a
London newspaper that year; *The Grand Mystery of Free-Masons Discover'd* the year after;
Samuel Prichard's *Masonry Dissected* in 1730 gave the catechisms of all three degrees and
the legend of the third, went through three editions in a fortnight, and stayed in print for
the rest of the century. In France, the Abbé Pérau's *Le Secret des Francs-Maçons* did the
same from 1742. *Jachin and Boaz*, in 1762, was a bestseller and was still being reprinted in
the 1790s.[^2]

Any literate man in London or Paris who wanted to know what happened in a lodge could find
out for a few pence, at any point after 1730.

Some of them certainly did. The pamphlets sold in numbers that require a readership far
larger than the fraternity, and some of that readership must have been prospective members.
There is an entire sub-genre of period satire premised on the reader already knowing the
outline.

So the candidate's ignorance was not, in most cases, an absence of information. It was
something else, and the distinction matters enormously for everything that follows.

**He may well have known what was going to happen. He could not know what it would be like.**

That gap — between the description of a procedure and the undergoing of it — is the
foundation of this entire book, and it is not mystical. It is the same gap that exists
between reading a flight manual and flying, between the marriage service in the prayer book
and the moment in the church, between knowing that the injection will sting and the sting.
Information about an experience is not the experience, and no amount of the first produces
the second.

Which means that when a Masonic writer insists that the secrets cannot be communicated by
print, he may be making a mystical claim — Chapter 39 examines that version at length — or
he may be making a completely ordinary observation that happens to be true.

---

### "Of your own free will and accord"

There is a phrase in the ceremony, present in every published version I have consulted, in
which the candidate is asked whether he comes of his own free will.

It appears at the threshold, and in most workings it is asked while something sharp is
pressed against his bare chest.

Modern readers tend to find this sinister, and eighteenth-century anti-Masonic writers made
a good deal of it. But the historical function is visible once you stop reading it as
coercion. The question is not there to frighten him into consent. It is there because
*consent given under pressure is remembered differently from consent given casually*, and
because the ritual is about to extract a very serious promise from a man who agreed to all
this over dinner three weeks ago and has not thought hard about it since.

The point at the breast is a device for making the word *yes* cost something.

This is worth pausing on because it is the first clear instance of a pattern that runs
through the entire ceremony. **The Masonic ritual repeatedly takes an ordinary social
transaction and arranges physical conditions that make it un-ordinary.** A promise becomes an
obligation. A piece of moral advice becomes a revelation. An introduction to a group becomes
an admission to a body. Nothing new is added to the content. The content is placed in
conditions.

---

### Who was he?

The membership of eighteenth-century lodges is one of the better-documented questions in
this field, and the answer is less glamorous and more interesting than the mythology.

In England he was, increasingly through the century, a man of the middling sort: a
tradesman, a shopkeeper, a clerk, a professional man, an officer. Grand Lodge attracted
aristocrats and the Craft advertised them, but the lodges were filled from below that. In the
American colonies the early membership was more elite — merchants, officials, planters — and
became broader after the Revolution. In France the composition was different again, shaped by
salon sociability and by a hunger for gradation that English Masonry never matched.[^3]

He was, in most cases, joining for reasons that had nothing to do with symbolism. He wanted
the company, the dinners, the connections, the charity provision, the standing, and — in a
mobile century — the recognition system that let a stranger arriving in a strange town
present himself and be received.

I want that fact placed at the front of this book rather than buried in it, because it is the
single most important corrective to everything I am about to argue.

**If Masonic initiation is a technology of transformation, its most common product was a man
who liked the dinners.**

The minute books say so. They record thousands of members who paid, attended for a while,
lapsed, and left no trace of interest in what any of it meant. Chapter 44 is where that
evidence gets its full weight, and it deserves it.

But the same books record something else: men who did not lapse. Men who attended for
forty years, who held every office in turn, who learned thousands of words of ritual by
heart, who travelled to visit other lodges, who left money to the charity in their wills.
Men, in other words, for whom something evidently took.

Both populations went through the same hour in the same room.

Understanding why the same procedure produces both outcomes is one of the things this book
is for, and I will say now that I do not fully understand it.

---

### The threshold as a historical object

One last observation before the door.

Every society has boundaries, and every boundary has a crossing. What distinguishes an
initiatory crossing from an ordinary one is that the initiatory crossing is *made
difficult on purpose*.

There is no practical reason for a man to be balloted in secret, kept waiting, stripped of
his metal, partly undressed and led blind into a room. The lodge could simply have
introduced him. Most voluntary associations in history have.

The difficulty is the mechanism. A threshold that costs nothing to cross does not mark
anything; a threshold that costs is a threshold the crosser remembers being on the far side
of. Arnold van Gennep, writing in 1909 about rites of passage in dozens of societies,
identified the same three-part shape everywhere he looked — separation, a marginal or
liminal phase, then incorporation — and observed that the middle phase is where the work is
done.[^4]

I offer his schema as a vocabulary and not as a law, for reasons Chapter 6 will set out at
length: there is a serious argument that "ritual" is a category scholars produced rather than
one they found, and a book of this kind has to take that argument seriously rather than
assuming its way past it.

What can be said without any theory at all is this. In the weeks before his initiation, a
man was made to want something, made to pay for it, made to wait, and made to arrive in a
condition of committed ignorance.

Nothing supernatural has happened. Nothing has happened at all, in the sense of an event.

And he is already, measurably, in a different state from the one he was in when his friend
first raised the subject over dinner.

The rest of the evening will be conducted on a person who has been prepared.
