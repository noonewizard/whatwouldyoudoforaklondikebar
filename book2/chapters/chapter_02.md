# CHAPTER TWO
## The Chamber of Preparation

In the collection of the Library and Museum of Freemasonry there are objects that no
visitor photographs.

The tracing boards get attention, and the aprons, and the jewels. But somewhere in the
storage of most Masonic collections there is a strip of dark cloth with a tie at each end,
and a length of rope, and they are the two most functionally important objects in this book.
They are also, as objects, entirely without interest: a blindfold and a cord. You could
assemble both from a draper's shop in twenty minutes.[^1]

They belong to a room that most accounts of Freemasonry do not describe, because nothing
happens in it.

---

### The room that does nothing

The preparation room is a small chamber adjoining the lodge. In a tavern working it might be
a landing, a cupboard, an adjacent bedroom. In a purpose-built temple of the nineteenth
century it is a proper anteroom, sometimes furnished with a mirror, sometimes — in certain
continental workings and in the higher degrees — with objects intended to provoke
reflection on mortality.[^2]

Its function is separation, and the separation is physical before it is anything else. The
candidate is removed from the company of the people who brought him and placed alone.

What he does there is take things off.

He surrenders metal: coins, keys, buckles, a watch, a ring in some workings. He removes or
loosens particular garments according to a schedule that differs by degree and by
jurisdiction, so that particular parts of him are bare. He is hoodwinked. A cable-tow is put
about him.

Then he waits until somebody comes.

---

### Divestment as a documented practice

The elements are attested consistently across the published sources from the 1720s onward,
with local variation in almost every particular.

The removal of metal is explained in Masonic sources by an allusion to the building of
Solomon's Temple, where — the biblical text says — no tool of iron was heard in the house
while it was in building.[^3] That is the explanation the ritual itself gives, and it is a
good example of a feature this book will meet repeatedly: **a practice with an obvious
practical function, supplied afterwards with a scriptural rationale that has nothing to do
with the function.**

The practical function is not obscure. A man's coins, his watch, his buckles and his rings
are the portable evidence of his station. Removing them removes the means by which a room
full of strangers would otherwise rank him on sight, and — more to the point — removes the
means by which he ranks himself.

The partial undress works the same way and is more precisely calibrated. It would be simpler
to have a candidate change into a robe, and many initiatory systems do exactly that. Masonry
does not. It leaves him in his own clothes, disarranged.

The difference is not trivial. A robe is a costume: it says *you are now in a different
role*, and the wearer accepts the frame and relaxes into it. Disarranged ordinary clothing
says something else. It says *you are yourself, and not in order*, and it cannot be relaxed
into, because every minute the wearer is aware of the bare knee and the loose shirt and the
single shoe.

Whether anyone intended that distinction I cannot say and will not guess. But the
eighteenth-century ritual chose the more uncomfortable of two available options, and kept
choosing it for three hundred years.

---

### Making a place

Here I want to introduce the idea that governs this part of the book, because it dissolves a
problem that has confused a great deal of writing on Masonic architecture.

The problem is this. Masonic symbolism is architectural throughout — temples, pillars,
pavements, the east, the orders. Masonic buildings of the nineteenth century are monumental.
And yet for most of its history the lodge met in a rented room above a public house,
typically small, typically low-ceilinged, and used for dinners the rest of the week.

If the power of the ceremony depends on sacred architecture, how did it work in a tavern?

The answer, I think, is that it never depended on architecture at all. It depended on
*emplacement*.

The historian of religion Jonathan Z. Smith argued in 1987 that sacrality is not a property
that certain places possess. It is a property conferred by ritual attention: a place becomes
sacred because it is marked, bounded and used as sacred, and ritual is precisely the activity
that does the marking. His central image is of ritual as a mode of paying attention — the
sacred place being, in his phrase, a focusing lens.[^4]

That is exactly what a Masonic lodge is.

Nothing in the tavern room is sacred. The boards are boards. But an east is declared, and
stations are assigned, and a boundary is guarded, and a design is drawn on the floor in chalk
and charcoal — and for two hours the room is a different kind of object. Then the design is
mopped away by the most recently admitted member, the chairs go back, and it is a dining
room.

**The sacrality is constructed, temporary, and entirely explicit.** Nobody in the lodge
believes the tavern is holy ground. They believe that this arrangement, for this period,
functions as one.

Smith's argument is theoretical and was built on other material, and I am applying it across
two centuries to a subject he was not writing about. But the fit is unusually good, and the
reason it is good is that Freemasonry is a nearly ideal test case: a ritual system with an
intensely architectural imagination and no buildings.

---

### Why the preparation room matters

Three reasons, and the third is the one that will recur.

**It establishes the boundary.** There is now an inside and an outside, and the candidate is
in a third place which is neither. He cannot return to the ordinary evening — he is half
dressed and his money is in someone else's keeping — and he cannot enter, because the door is
shut and guarded.

**It transfers control.** He has surrendered his possessions, his sight and his freedom of
movement to people he cannot see, and he has done each of those things by his own consent,
one small step at a time. Nobody forced him. He handed over his watch himself.

**And it does all of this with an apparatus of almost comic cheapness.** A strip of cloth. A
rope. A small room. Twenty minutes.

That third point is the thread this book keeps pulling. The Masonic ceremony is not
expensive. It requires no building, no trained clergy, no consecrated ground, no
instruments, no drugs, no music, no images beyond a diagram somebody can chalk on a floor.
It can be performed by seven men in a rented room with equipment costing a few shillings.

Which is why it went everywhere. A tradition that needs a cathedral stays where the
cathedral is. A tradition that needs a blindfold and an agreement about which wall is east
can be carried in a sea chest and set up in a warehouse in Boston, a garrison tent in
Bengal, a room above a shop in Lagos, or — as happened, repeatedly — a prisoner-of-war camp.

The portability is not incidental to the history. It is most of the explanation for it.

---

### What the room cannot tell us

I have described what the preparation room does in functional terms, and I should now say
plainly what I have not described.

I have not told you what the man in it felt. Nobody can. There is no surviving first-person
account of an eighteenth-century Masonic preparation room written by a candidate in it, and
if there were, it would have been written afterwards, in the vocabulary the ceremony had by
then supplied him with, which is a problem of its own.

What I have described is a *design* — a set of conditions that we know were imposed, and a
set of effects that conditions of that kind are known to produce in human beings generally.
The step from the first to the second is an inference, and I will be making it repeatedly,
and labelling it every time.

The door is about to open. Before it does, there is one more condition to account for, and it
is the one that the whole remaining hour is built around.

He cannot see.
