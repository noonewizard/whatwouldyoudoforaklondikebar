# CHAPTER THREE
## Darkness

The oldest surviving Masonic ritual text is Scottish, written down in 1696, and it does not
mention a blindfold.

The Edinburgh Register House Manuscript describes two degrees, a word, a grip, and a
catechism of questions and answers. It is brief and practical. It tells you how to recognise
a mason and what to say. There is nothing in it about darkness at all.[^1]

By 1730 the darkness is there, and by 1762 it is elaborate. Prichard's candidate is in "a
state of darkness." The later English and French exposures describe the hoodwink, the
restoration of light, and the formula in which a man is asked what he most desires and
answers: light.[^2]

So the darkness is not original. It arrives with the eighteenth-century development of the
ceremony, in the same decades that produced the third degree, and it becomes the organising
condition of the whole event.

That dating matters, because it means the darkness was *added* — and things that are added
to a working system tend to be added because they do something.

---

### Three kinds of darkness

The word is doing three jobs at once in Masonic usage, and separating them removes most of
the confusion.

**Darkness as a physical condition.** The candidate is hoodwinked. He cannot see. This is a
fact about his sensory input, and it is the only one of the three that has any measurable
effect on a nervous system.

**Darkness as a ritual state.** He is "in darkness" in the sense that a person is "in
mourning" — a declared condition, with a beginning and an end, which others treat him as
occupying. The lodge is not dark; only he is. Everyone else can see perfectly well and is
watching him.

**Darkness as a metaphor for ignorance.** This is the interpretation the ritual itself
supplies and the only one it explains. He was in darkness; he seeks light; light means
knowledge, and specifically moral knowledge.

The popular literature blurs these constantly, usually in the direction of the third —
treating the blindfold as a symbol and forgetting that it is also a piece of cloth over a
man's eyes for forty minutes.

This book is interested in all three, but it is most interested in the first, because the
first is the one nobody writes about.

---

### What the cloth does

Strip out the symbolism and ask what happens to a person who is deprived of vision in an
unfamiliar room among people who can see him.

**He cannot anticipate.** This is the largest effect and the most consequential. Sighted
prediction is continuous and almost entirely unconscious: we know what is about to happen
because we can see it approaching. Remove vision and every event arrives without warning. A
hand on the arm, a voice from an unexpected quarter, a knock, a change of direction — each of
these becomes a discrete occurrence rather than part of a continuous scene.

The ceremony exploits this relentlessly. The candidate is moved without being told where.
He is stopped. He is addressed by people whose position he must infer from sound. Something
is pressed against his chest that he did not see coming.

**He must build the room from other channels.** Sound, touch, the feel of boards under one
bare foot, the direction of a draught, the number of paces. This is effortful, and
effortful processing is better retained than passive reception. He is, without being told
to, working hard at a task for the whole duration.

**He is dependent.** Someone has his arm. He goes where he is taken. This is not
metaphorical dependence; it is the practical kind, and it is imposed on an adult man who
spends the rest of his life not being led anywhere.

**And the restoration becomes an event.** This is the part that only makes sense once you
notice the whole hour is engineered toward it. In ordinary experience, light is not an
event; it is the condition under which events happen. The hoodwink converts illumination
into an occurrence with a time and a place — and places it at the moment the ceremony has
something to show him.

---

### The limits of what can be claimed

There is a literature on sensory deprivation and I want to be careful with it, because it is
easy to reach for and mostly inapplicable.

The classic mid-twentieth-century experiments involved hours or days of comprehensive
sensory isolation and produced disorientation, distorted time perception, and in some cases
hallucination. **None of that transfers.** A Masonic candidate is blindfolded for well under
an hour, in a room full of voices, being physically handled, in a condition of high
attention. He is not sensorily deprived in the experimental sense; he is sensorily
*redirected*.

What the general literature supports is modest and sufficient: that removing a dominant
sensory channel increases the salience of the remaining ones, that unpredictability sustains
attentional engagement, and that novel effortful encoding is better retained than routine
passive encoding.

Those are broad findings about human beings, established in laboratories in the twentieth and
twenty-first centuries, and applying them to an eighteenth-century tavern is an inference
across a considerable distance. I make it because the alternative is to say nothing about a
condition the sources describe on every page, and that would be worse. But it is an
inference, and it does not tell us what any particular man experienced.

---

### The absence that says the most

Here is the finding of this chapter, and it is a negative one.

If you assemble the conditions the Masonic ceremony imposes — sensory restriction, sustained
uncertainty, heightened arousal, physical ordeal, an hour of concentrated attention — you
have assembled a list that, in other ritual traditions, is associated with altered states of
consciousness. Trance, dissociation, visionary experience, possession.

**And no Masonic source describes anything of the kind.**

I have looked. Not in the eighteenth-century exposures, which had every commercial reason to
report something sensational and reported catechisms instead. Not in Preston, who wrote
extensively about what the ceremony conveys. Not in the sermons, not in the monitors, not in
the nineteenth-century interpretive literature, which was written by men — Oliver, Pike,
Waite — perfectly willing to make large claims about Masonic profundity.

Compare the contrast cases. The Anastenaria fire-walkers of northern Greece describe
possession by the saint. Pentecostal worship has ecstatic states as an explicit goal and an
explicit vocabulary. The ancient mystery cults were remembered, however vaguely, for
producing something overwhelming.

Masonic testimony is sober. Men describe being moved, impressed, struck by the solemnity.
They do not describe leaving themselves.

**The conditions are present. The reports are absent.**

Two explanations are available and I think the second is right.

The first is that such experiences occurred and were never recorded — that the sober
register of Masonic writing suppressed them. This cannot be excluded, but it requires an
unusually thorough silence across three centuries, four countries and a very large
literature, including a hostile literature that would have been delighted to report
Masonic ecstasies.

The second is that the ceremony is not built for that. **It is built for attention and
memory, not for dissociation.** Every component points the same way: the candidate is kept
alert, kept questioned, kept moving, required to speak, required to respond. He is never left
alone in the dark for long enough to drift. The ritual holds him in a state of intense
outward focus and then gives him something to look at.

If that is right, then Masonic ritual belongs to a different family from the ecstatic
traditions with which it is so often loosely grouped. It is not a technology for leaving the
world. It is a technology for arriving in it more sharply.

And it would explain something otherwise puzzling — why an apparatus this effective was so
readily adopted by the least mystical institutions imaginable: friendly societies, burial
clubs, trade unions, and the mutual-aid orders that insured half the working population of
nineteenth-century Britain.

They were not seeking visions. They were seeking members who would remember their
obligations, and the machine delivered that reliably.

---

### The room he cannot see

Return to him.

He is in the dark, in a room he has never entered, among perhaps a dozen men he cannot see,
several of whom he knows socially and will have dinner with in an hour. He is holding
nothing. He owns nothing on his person. Someone has his arm.

Somebody knocks on a door on his behalf, three times, and a voice on the other side asks who
comes there.

He does not answer. He is not permitted to. Another man answers for him, describing him to
the room in the third person while he stands in it — a poor candidate in a state of
darkness, who comes of his own free will and accord, properly prepared, humbly soliciting.

It is the last moment at which anything will be said about him rather than to him.
