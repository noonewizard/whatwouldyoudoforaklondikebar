# CHAPTER FOUR
## The Knock, and the Door Opens

Three knocks.

They appear in the printed rituals from the 1720s onward and they are given in a specified
rhythm which differs by degree, so that the knocking itself carries information: anyone
inside the room who hears it knows which ceremony is about to begin. *Three Distinct
Knocks*, the English exposure of 1760, takes its title from them.[^1]

It is a small thing and it is the hinge of the whole evening, because it is the moment at
which the ceremony stops being something done to a man in a side room and becomes something
done between two spaces.

---

### The oldest ritual object in the world

Thresholds are where ritual concentrates, everywhere, and the reason is not mysterious.

A door is a real discontinuity. On one side is one set of rules; on the other, another. The
boundary is the point at which a person is subject to neither, and for that reason it is the
point at which authority over them has to be renegotiated. Every culture that has doors has
customs about them: you knock, you announce yourself, you are invited, you cross with the
right foot, you carry the bride over, you take off your shoes, you say a word.

Arnold van Gennep gave the phenomenon its name in 1909 and made it central. *Limen* is Latin
for threshold, and the liminal phase of a rite of passage is the one in which a person has
left their old status and not yet acquired the new one. Van Gennep noticed that societies
mark this phase physically — with literal doorways, gates, bridges, and spatial passages —
with a consistency that crosses every cultural boundary he examined.[^2]

I am citing him as vocabulary rather than as law, for reasons I will set out properly in
Chapter 6. But the Masonic ceremony's use of the threshold is so explicit that it needs
almost no theoretical assistance. The candidate is outside; he is announced; he is
challenged; he is admitted; and from the moment he crosses, everyone addresses him
differently.

---

### What happens at the door

The sequence, assembled comparatively from the published sources of 1723 to 1762, and
varying in every particular by jurisdiction:

**The knocks.** Given on his behalf, in the prescribed rhythm.

**The challenge.** A voice inside demands who comes there.

**The answer, given for him.** He is described in the third person: a poor candidate in a
state of darkness, properly prepared, of his own free will and accord, humbly soliciting.

**The question of qualification.** Is he worthy and well recommended? The answer, again,
comes from his conductor.

**The question of consent.** And here, at last, he speaks. Asked whether he comes of his own
free will, he says that he does.

**The reception.** In most workings, something sharp is presented to his naked left breast
— a compass point, a poniard, a sword, depending on the working — with a formula explaining
that as this instrument is an instrument of torture to his flesh, so may the recollection of
it be to his conscience should he ever reveal the secrets improperly.

**The prayer.** In nearly all English and American workings, a prayer is offered before he
proceeds.

**The entry.**

---

### The grammar of being spoken for

I want to spend a moment on a feature that every account records and almost none discusses.

For the first part of this exchange, the candidate does not speak. He is *described*, aloud,
in the third person, to a room he cannot see, while standing in it.

This is a strange thing to do to an adult, and it is not accidental — the formula is stable
across the published sources and across jurisdictions that differ about nearly everything
else.

Consider what it accomplishes.

It removes his authorship of himself. For those minutes he is a case being presented rather
than a person arriving. Someone else is vouching for his worthiness, his preparation and his
motives, and the room is evaluating the vouching rather than him.

It also, and more subtly, makes the conductor his guarantor. A man is describing him in terms
he cannot check and taking responsibility for the description. If the candidate turns out
badly, the fault attaches to the man who spoke for him — which is exactly the structure of
the proposal and ballot that preceded the evening, now performed out loud.

And then, having established that he cannot speak for himself, the ritual asks him a
question that only he can answer.

*Do you come here of your own free will and accord?*

The effect of the preceding two minutes is to make that one word his. Everything else has
been supplied by others; this is the only thing in the ceremony so far that must come from
him. And it arrives, in most workings, with a point against his chest.

---

### The point at the breast

The instrument is the detail the anti-Masonic literature has always liked best, and it needs
handling carefully in both directions.

**What it is not.** It is not a threat, and there is no recorded instance of a candidate
being harmed. The instrument is presented, not used. The accompanying formula is explicitly
symbolic: the recollection is to prick his conscience.

**What it is.** It is a device for converting an abstraction into a sensation. The candidate
has already consented three times — when he agreed to be proposed, when he paid, when he
arrived. Those consents cost him nothing physical. This one is given while something
metallic is touching his skin and he cannot see what it is.

And the formula attached to it is doing something quite precise. It does not say *if you
betray us we will stab you*. It says *the memory of this feeling is what you will have
instead of a conscience about it*. The ritual is explicitly installing a physical memory to
be recalled later, on purpose, and saying so out loud.

That is the clearest statement in the entire Craft ceremonial of what the apparatus is for.
Not to convey information. To attach a bodily memory to a commitment, so that the commitment
has something to be recalled by.

I do not know whether the men who composed that formula understood what they had said. It
has the feel of something arrived at rather than designed. But it is there, in print, from
the eighteenth century, and it is the nearest thing this book has to a period statement of
its own thesis.

---

### The prayer

One element deserves mention because its absence from the popular picture is so complete.

In most English and American workings a prayer is offered at this point, and the candidate
— blindfolded, roped, partly undressed, with a blade at his chest — kneels while a room of
men asks God to bless the undertaking.

It is ordinary Protestant devotional language. It invokes the deity under the non-sectarian
formula that Anderson's *Constitutions* of 1723 made the price of admission, so that the
room may contain Anglicans, Dissenters, Catholics and Jews without anybody's conscience
being engaged. It is, by design, theologically almost empty and emotionally not empty at
all.

A reader arriving from the conspiracy literature expects something else here. There is
nothing else. At the most dramatic moment of the ceremony, the lodge stops and prays in
terms that would not startle a country vicar.

---

### Crossing

Then he is admitted, and the record is worth stating plainly: nothing dramatic occurs. He is
led in. The door closes. He is somewhere he has not been, among people he cannot see, and he
does not yet know the shape of the room.

Van Gennep's schema would call this the beginning of the liminal phase, and Victor Turner's
elaboration of that phase — status stripped, initiands levelled, the person unusually open
to what the ritual is about to supply — describes the condition well.[^3]

I want to register a caution here that will be argued properly in two chapters' time.
Turner built that account on Ndembu material in central Africa, and van Gennep on a
comparative survey of societies that did not include eighteenth-century England. When their
schema fits Masonic initiation — and it fits it well — that is a fit between a general
pattern and an instance. It is not evidence that the pattern is universal, and it is
certainly not evidence of any historical relationship.

There is also a live argument in ritual studies that the whole category is a scholarly
artefact. That argument deserves a hearing before I build five hundred pages on the word
*ritual*, and it will get one.

---

But before the theory, there is a prior question, and it is the one that makes this book
possible at all.

Everything I have described in these four chapters comes from documents. The documents were
written by men who had sworn not to write them. They were printed to make money by exposing
a society that existed to be un-exposed. They are hostile, commercial, sometimes padded and
frequently contradictory.

How does a historian work with a tradition that concealed itself — and how do you tell the
difference between a secret that was kept and a secret that was never there?

That is the next chapter, and it is the most useful thing in this book.
