# CHAPTER FIVE
## How to Read a Secret

In 1614 Isaac Casaubon destroyed a tradition with a dictionary.

He had been reading the *Corpus Hermeticum*, which Europe had believed for a hundred and
forty years to be the work of an Egyptian sage contemporary with Moses. Casaubon noticed
that the Greek was wrong — that these dialogues used vocabulary that did not exist in the
archaic period, and were written in the prose style of late antiquity, and quoted Plato,
which a contemporary of Moses could not have done. He concluded that they were composed in
Roman Egypt in the first centuries of the Christian era.

No competent scholar has ever overturned it. Modern editors state it as a matter of course.

And for the next three hundred years the texts went on being cited as ancient Egyptian
wisdom, including by people who knew about Casaubon.[^1]

That is the problem this chapter is about. Not the specific tradition — the previous volume
handled that — but the general difficulty: **how do you establish the truth about a body of
knowledge whose custodians claim it was deliberately hidden?**

---

### Why the difficulty is real

I want to state the strongest version of the case against my own method before dismantling
it, because the case is not stupid.

If a tradition conceals its practices, then the absence of documentation for a practice is
not evidence that the practice did not occur. Silence in the record is exactly what
successful secrecy produces. A historian who reasons "there is no evidence, therefore it
did not happen" is, in the case of a genuinely secret tradition, making a category error —
and the tradition's defenders say so, constantly, and they are not wrong to.

Worse: the gap in the evidence is shaped exactly like the thing being looked for. If you
are asking whether Freemasonry preserved an ancient teaching, and the answer would by
hypothesis be invisible, then the invisibility of the answer proves nothing at all.

This is a real methodological problem. It is not solved by asserting scepticism. It is
solved by asking better questions.

---

### The six questions

These are not original to me. They are ordinary historical method, assembled for this
particular job. They were the apparatus of the first volume and they carry over unchanged.

**1. Provenance. Where is the physical object?**

Not where the tradition is *said* to come from — where is the manuscript, the letter, the
minute book, the printed edition? Who has held it since? What is its shelfmark?

This question alone disposes of a startling proportion of the literature. The Golden Dawn's
authority rested on correspondence with a German adept named Anna Sprengel. Where are the
letters? They were produced, examined, and found wanting. The Rite of Strict Observance
rested on Unknown Superiors; Baron von Hund spent thirty years being asked to produce them
and never did.

**2. Chronology. Does the timeline permit it?**

The cheapest test available, and the most frequently skipped. A claim requiring an
organisation to have influenced something that happened before the organisation existed is
finished, and no amount of resonance repairs it.

**3. Transmission. Can you name the route?**

A person, a text, a translation, a purchase, a meeting. This is the question the literature
most reliably cannot answer.

The crucial refinement: **availability is not transmission.** That Agrippa's *Occult
Philosophy* was purchasable in English from 1651 tells us an English Mason *could* have read
it. It does not tell us that one did, and it certainly does not tell us that what he read
entered a ritual.

The corrective method here is one I take from Alison Butler, who applied it to the Golden
Dawn. Rather than asking what ideas were in the air, she reconstructed what books were
physically on the shelves — the catalogued contents of the founders' libraries.[^2] That is
transmission evidence. Ideas in the air are not.

**4. Intertextuality. Does the later source use the earlier one?**

Resemblance is weak. Quotation is strong. Paraphrase is strong. Shared idiosyncratic error
is decisive: if two texts make the same odd mistake, one copied the other.

**5. Institutional continuity. Did the organisation persist?**

Organisations that exist do things. They collect dues, keep lists, rent rooms, quarrel,
split, and leave paper. A claim of unbroken institutional descent across centuries with no
dues, no lists and no quarrels is a claim that an organisation existed in a manner unlike
every other organisation in recorded history.

**6. Anachronism. Does the claim contain something that could not yet exist?**

The sharpest instrument in the set. A Masonic symbol whose meaning depends on the English
letter G cannot be Egyptian. A Kabbalistic system that assigns tarot trumps to the paths of
the Tree of Life cannot predate the tarot, which is a fifteenth-century Italian card game.

From these follows one rule, and it governs everything: **a parallel is not a descent.**

---

### The answer to the objection

Now back to the difficulty. If secrecy produces silence, how can any of this work?

Three reasons, and they are sufficient.

**Secrecy leaks, and it leaks in predictable directions.** Organisations keep minutes.
Rituals must be taught, which means they must be transmitted, which means they exist
somewhere recoverable. Disgruntled members publish. Governments raid. Widows sell papers.

The record of this subject is a record of leakage. The Masonic secrets were in a London
newspaper by 1723. The Bavarian Illuminati's internal correspondence was seized and printed
by the Bavarian state in 1787. The Golden Dawn's rituals were published by Crowley from 1909
and by Regardie from 1937. Léo Taxil confessed his fabrications in a hall full of
journalists in 1897.

**Total absence of evidence, across centuries and countries, is itself a fact requiring
explanation** — and the explanation that fits it best is usually that there was nothing to
leak.

**The claim of secrecy is testable even when the secret is not.** We cannot examine an
undisclosed transmission. We can examine the *assertion* of one. When was it first made? By
whom? What did they gain? Did anyone contradict them at the time? Does the alleged ancient
teaching contain anything that could not have existed at the claimed date?

**And the burden does not shift.** "You cannot prove it did not happen in secret" is not an
argument; it is the abandonment of argument, and it licenses anything whatever.

---

### The four new questions

Everything above is inherited. What this book adds is a second apparatus, for a different
kind of claim.

The six questions handle *transmission* claims — assertions that something old was carried
into something newer. They do not handle *discovery* claims: assertions that a hidden
pattern has been found in material that was there all along. A cipher in a ritual text. A
numerical scheme in the degrees. A geometry in a street plan. A name concealed in a word.

For those, four further questions apply, and they come from statistics rather than from
history.

**7. Was the procedure specified in advance, or fitted to the result?**

A method chosen after seeing the answer is not a method. If you decide which transformation
to apply — reverse the letters, take every third one, substitute by a cipher, convert to
numbers and add — *after* noticing which one yields something interesting, you have not
discovered a pattern. You have selected one.

**8. What is the search space?**

How many words, letters, numbers, buildings or angles were available to try? A hit means
nothing until you know how many chances there were to get one. This is the multiple
comparisons problem, and it is the reason that in a large enough body of material,
*something* will always look designed.

**9. Does it survive a control?**

Run the identical procedure on matched material that cannot contain the message. Another
text. Another city's street plan. Another century's ritual.

**If the control produces comparable results, the finding is noise.**

This is not a rhetorical move. It is the test that settled the most famous case of its kind.
In 1997 Michael Drosnin's *The Bible Code* claimed that equidistant letter sequences in the
Hebrew text of Genesis encoded modern events. In 1999, Brendan McKay, Dror Bar-Natan, Maya
Bar-Hillel and Gil Kalai published a paper in *Statistical Science* demonstrating that the
same method applied to other texts produced the same class of results — and McKay found
assassination "predictions" for Indira Gandhi, Yitzhak Rabin, the Kennedys, Martin Luther
King, Lincoln and Trotsky in the English text of *Moby-Dick*.[^3]

The method works. That is the problem with it.

**10. Does the claim make a prediction that could fail?**

A pattern that accommodates any outcome explains none. If the scheme can absorb whatever the
evidence turns out to be, it is not a finding about the evidence.

---

### Why humans are so good at this

One more thing, because the four questions can sound like an accusation and they are not
meant as one.

The tendency to perceive meaningful patterns in noise has a name — apophenia — and it is not
a defect. It is the ordinary operation of a perceptual system that evolved in an
environment where the cost of missing a real pattern was considerably higher than the cost
of seeing a false one. A rustle in the grass that is nothing costs you a startle. A rustle
that is something and is missed costs you everything.

We are, in consequence, machines for finding structure, and we find it whether or not it is
there.

**Which means that the person who discovers a hidden geometry in a cathedral floor, or a
name in the initials of the degrees, or a pentagram in a street plan, is not being stupid or
dishonest.** They are doing exactly what a human perceptual system does, enthusiastically, on
material that rewards it. The four questions are not a way of calling such people fools.
They are the only way anyone — including the person doing the discovering — can tell the
difference between a pattern found and a pattern made.

And some patterns genuinely are designed. That is the part that makes this hard. Ciphers
exist. Freemasons really did use a substitution cipher for their records. Hebrew really does
have gematria, and Jewish exegetes really did use it. Euclid's forty-seventh proposition
really is on the front of Anderson's *Constitutions* of 1723, and Anderson really did
describe it as the foundation of all Masonry.

The question is never *is this a pattern*. Patterns are cheap. The question is always
whether the pattern was put there.

---

### What this chapter licenses

Everything that follows.

When I say, in a later chapter, that the letter G cannot be an ancient symbol, I am invoking
question 6. When I say that the resemblance between the rough ashlar and the alchemical
*prima materia* is real but not a descent, I am invoking 3 and 4. When I dismantle a claim
about hidden Hebrew in a Masonic word, I am invoking 7, 8 and 9 — and Chapter 38 runs that
test in public, on a live example, and shows the reader the control.

And when I say, repeatedly, that I do not know something — that nobody knows who wrote the
third degree, that no eighteenth-century Mason left a usable account of what the ceremony
felt like — I am reporting the result of applying question 1 and getting nothing back.

That is the honest state of this subject. It is a field with excellent evidence for what
people did, moderate evidence for what they believed, and almost none for what happened
inside them.

Which is the awkward position from which the rest of this book proceeds, and the next part
begins by asking what a ritual is, and whether the word means anything at all.
