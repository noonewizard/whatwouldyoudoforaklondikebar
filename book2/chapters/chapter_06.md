# PART II — THE TECHNOLOGY OF INITIATION

# CHAPTER SIX
## Ritual Is an Engine

I have been using a word for five chapters that I now have to defend, because there is a
serious argument that it does not name anything.

The word is *ritual*, and the argument is Catherine Bell's.

---

### The objection, stated properly

In 1992 Bell published a book that did something unusual for the field: it turned on the
field itself. *Ritual Theory, Ritual Practice* argued that "ritual" is not a natural kind —
not a category found in the world, like *mammal* or *igneous rock*, but one produced by
scholarly practice and carrying assumptions its users rarely examine.

Her central charge is about a dichotomy. Theories of ritual, she observed, characteristically
set thought against action: ritual is what people *do* as opposed to what they *think*, the
enactment of beliefs held elsewhere. That opposition is not found in the practices being
described. It is imported by the observer, and it belongs to a long Western philosophical
habit of separating mind from body and belief from behavior. Having imported it, scholars
then discover it everywhere, and write books explaining how ritual expresses, enacts or
embodies ideas.

Bell's alternative is to speak of *ritualization*: not a class of activities, but a strategy
— the way certain acts are differentiated from ordinary ones, marked out as privileged, and
thereby made to produce authority. On this account there is no such thing as ritual. There
are only things done ritually, and the doing-ritually is the object of study.[^1]

Ronald Grimes has pressed a related point for longer and from a different direction. His
method, which he calls *ritual criticism*, insists that rites be assessed in their
particularity — this rite, in this place, for these people, and whether it works. He has
criticised Turner, Schechner, Geertz, Goffman and Bell in turn, and the criticism is
consistent: the field keeps generating grand theory and the grand theory keeps flattening
the material.[^2]

If Bell is right, this book's governing question — *what does the ritual do?* — is malformed
before it starts.

---

### Why I am proceeding anyway

Three reasons, and I want them on the record rather than implied.

**First: the objection is to the general category, not to the particular case.** Bell's
argument is that *ritual* as a universal class does not carve reality at its joints. It is
not an argument that the Masonic initiation ceremony of 1730 did nothing to the men who
underwent it. The ceremony is an actual sequence of actual events, documented in print,
performed on real people for three centuries. Its existence does not depend on the
respectability of a scholarly noun.

**Second: my claim is deliberately narrow.** I am not claiming that ritual as such has a
universal structure with universal effects. I am claiming that *this* ceremony has
identifiable components, that those components have describable functions, and that the
functions are of kinds human beings are known to be susceptible to. That claim survives
Bell's objection because it never invokes the general category as evidence.

**Third: Bell's own alternative is useful here.** Ritualization — the strategic
differentiation of certain acts from ordinary ones — is an unusually good description of
what a Masonic lodge does. Nothing in a tavern room is inherently ritual. The lodge makes
certain acts different from other acts: it declares an east, guards a door, prescribes a
rhythm of knocking, requires a formula rather than a sentence. **The differentiating is the
whole activity**, and Bell gives us the word for it.

So: van Gennep and Turner will be used as vocabulary. Bell will be used as discipline. And
where a schema fits Masonic initiation, that is a fit between a general pattern and an
instance, never evidence of a universal and never evidence of a historical relationship.

With that established, the machine.

---

### Seven mechanisms

Strip the ceremony of its interpretations and what remains is a sequence of conditions. Each
is documented in the published sources. Each has an identifiable function. I give each a
separate confidence rating for *practice* and for *effect*, because those are different
kinds of claim and conflating them is the characteristic failure of this literature.

**1. Controlled information.** The candidate arrives with near-zero prior knowledge, by
convention. Material is withheld until the appropriate degree. He is asked questions he
cannot answer. A secret is announced before it is given.
*Practice: documented beyond dispute. Effect: plausible.* Attention is substantially a
function of uncertainty, and an environment that withholds, delays and then releases
produces sustained orientation toward the source.

**2. Sensory restriction.** The hoodwink; a room learned by ear and by one bare foot.
*Practice: documented. Effect: plausible*, within the limits set out in Chapter 3.

**3. Controlled arousal.** The point at the breast; the cable-tow; the penalties recited in
the obligation; in the third degree, a staged assault and a fall.
*Practice: documented. Effect: well supported* — that arousal modulates memory encoding is
among the more robust findings about human beings, and it is also the least surprising thing
in this book. The ceremony is memorable because it is alarming.

**4. Embodied encoding.** Kneeling. Specified hand positions. Bare knee, breast, foot.
Movement in a fixed direction. The grip.
*Practice: documented. Effect: plausible.* Procedural and spatial memory persist where
propositional memory fails, which is why the catechism is learned by heart and recited
rather than read.

**5. Public commitment.** An oath spoken aloud in the candidate's own voice, kneeling,
before witnesses, with consequences named, followed by a physical act.
*Practice: documented. Effect: plausible — with a qualification I will make in Chapter 10
rather than hiding here.*

**6. Staged revelation.** The hoodwink removed at a prepared instant, on a prepared image.
*Practice: documented. Effect: plausible.* And note the content is deliberately modest —
a book and two tools — which invites the recipient to supply the significance rather than
evaluate a claim.

**7. Social ratification.** Investiture with the apron; a place assigned; the right to visit
and be examined; the catechism as a portable proof of identity for life; the event minuted.
*Practice: documented. Effect: documented.* **This is the best-evidenced mechanism in the
list, because it is the only one that leaves records.** We can watch it in membership rolls,
visitors' books, certificates and charity accounts.

---

### The thing the models miss

Every stage-model of initiation — van Gennep's, Turner's, and the nine-stage version I
have seen proposed for Masonry — describes a single passage. Ordinary identity, separation,
liminality, instruction, encounter, reintegration, new identity.

They are all wrong about Freemasonry in the same way, and the error is instructive.

**The candidate does not undergo the ceremony once.**

He undergoes it, and then he watches it. He attends the lodge for years and sees it
performed on other men, repeatedly, from progressively senior positions in the room. He
learns the catechism by heart. He takes junior offices and speaks parts of it. He is
eventually the man holding the arm, then the man asking the questions, then the man in the
east conducting the whole thing.

A Mason of thirty years' standing has been through that hour perhaps a hundred times, in
every role, from every angle.

**No ritual-studies model accounts for this**, so far as I have been able to establish, and
it may be the most consequential feature of the entire system. It converts a single event
into a rehearsed sequence, distributed across decades, with the participant occupying every
position in it. Whatever the first night did to him, the following thirty years did
something different and probably larger.

Chapter 47 is where that argument is made properly. I flag it here because the seven
mechanisms above describe an *evening*, and the evening is not the unit of analysis.

### The word "engine"

A note on the metaphor in this chapter's title, because it is doing real work and could
easily do too much.

A technology is a reliable arrangement of means to produce an effect. It need not be
mechanical. A contract is a technology; so is double-entry bookkeeping; so is the sonnet.
What distinguishes a technology from a mere practice is that it can be *transmitted* —
written down, taught, reproduced elsewhere by other people, and expected to work again.

By that standard Masonic initiation qualifies without difficulty. It was specified, taught,
carried to six continents by men who had learned it in one room and reproduced it in
another, and adopted wholesale by organizations with entirely different aims on the evident
assumption that it would keep working.

But there is a trap in the word, and I want to avoid falling into it for forty-five chapters.

**"Technology" implies a designer, and there isn't one.** Nobody composed the Masonic
initiation. There is no draft, no memorandum, no theoretical document. The third degree
appears fully formed in a hostile pamphlet in 1730 and has no known author. The ceremony
assembled by accretion across decades, in a period whose participants had no vocabulary of
psychological engineering and would have found the idea unintelligible.

So the formulation I will hold to, and which I think is exactly right:

**Masonic ritual is a technology in the sense that a violin is.**

Nobody derived the violin from acoustics. It was refined over generations by craftsmen
optimising for an effect they could hear and could not explain, discarding what sounded
worse and keeping what sounded better, until the object was extraordinarily well adapted to
a purpose none of them could have stated in physical terms.

That is a real technology and a real achievement. It is not the same kind of thing as a
circuit, and the difference matters. A circuit can be reverse-engineered to its designer's
intention. A violin can only be reverse-engineered to its function — and when you ask why
the f-holes are that shape, the honest answer is that they sounded right to people who did
not know why.

Everything in this book should be read in that register. I will describe what the
components do. I will not claim that anyone put them there for that reason.

### What follows

The next four chapters take four of the seven mechanisms and examine them in detail: the
body, the passage, the voice, and the oath.

They are the four for which the evidence is best and the interpretation hardest, and each
of them raises the same difficulty in a different form.

We know what was done. We know what conditions of that kind do to people in general. We do
not know what happened to anybody.
