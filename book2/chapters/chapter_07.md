# CHAPTER SEVEN
## The Body Remembers

There is a kind of knowledge that cannot be written down, and every trade in history has
known it.

Ask a joiner how he knows when a plane is set right. Ask a rider how he knows a horse is
about to shy. Ask anyone who has learned to ride a bicycle to explain the correction they
make when the bicycle leans — not the physics of it, the actual instruction their body
follows. They cannot. The knowledge is real, it is reliable, it is demonstrable on demand,
and it does not exist in propositional form.

Michael Polanyi's formulation, from 1966, has never been improved on: **we know more than we
can tell.**[^1]

This chapter is about the fact that Masonic ritual is transmitted almost entirely in that
register, and about what follows from it.

---

### Learned by heart, never by book

In most jurisdictions, Masonic ritual is not read. It is memorized.

The scale is worth stating because it is routinely underestimated. A Mason who progresses
through the offices of a lodge in the English constitution commits to memory a body of
material running to many thousands of words — the ceremonies of three degrees, the opening
and closing, the charges, the explanations of the tracing boards, the lectures where these
are still worked. He learns it aurally, from another man, in sessions of practice, and he
delivers it standing up in front of people who know when he gets it wrong.

This is not a survival of illiteracy. It persisted through the nineteenth and twentieth
centuries among clerks and schoolmasters, and persists now. Some jurisdictions permit
printed rituals for private study; some have historically forbidden even that, requiring the
text to be learned mouth to ear.[^2]

The practice is old. The Scottish catechisms of the 1690s are memorization documents: their
question-and-answer form exists precisely so that the material can be held and tested
without writing. And the second Schaw Statute of 1599 requires fellows of craft to be
examined in "the art of memory and the science thereof," which — whatever else it meant, and
Chapter 34 takes up the dispute — establishes that a Scottish mason's competence was tested
on what he could hold in his head.[^3]

So the tradition has been transmitting itself by memory for at least three hundred and
twenty-five years, and more probably longer.

---

### Why that changes the content

Here is the part that matters for this book's argument.

**A tradition transmitted by memory is a different kind of object from a tradition
transmitted by text**, and the difference is not merely one of medium.

A text is stable, inspectable and separable from its holder. You can compare two copies. You
can find contradictions. You can argue about interpretation with the object in front of
both parties. You can also, crucially, *have* it without doing anything — a book on a shelf
is a possession.

Memorized ritual has none of these properties. It cannot be inspected except by performance.
It cannot be possessed without labor. It cannot be compared without two people agreeing to
perform simultaneously, which is what the examination of a visiting brother actually is.
And it decays unless used.

Which produces a consequence that no Masonic author I have read seems to have noticed:
**the Craft's knowledge is constitutively active.** You cannot be a lapsed holder of it in
the way you can be a lapsed owner of a creed. Either you can still give the catechism or you
cannot.

That is a remarkable property for a body of secret knowledge to have, and it explains
something about the institution's durability. The knowledge is maintained by the same
activity that constitutes membership. There is no way to keep the one without doing the
other.

---

### The body in the ceremony

Now the ceremony itself, read as a physical rather than a symbolic sequence.

The candidate kneels. In most workings his hands are placed in a specified position on an
open book with a square and compasses upon it, and he cannot see any of this — it is
arranged for him by other people's hands. One knee is bare against boards. One foot is
unshod. He stands in prescribed attitudes at prescribed moments. He walks a prescribed
course. He receives, and later gives, a grip — a particular arrangement of pressure between
two hands that must be executed correctly to be recognized.

None of this is decorative. Every item is prescribed in the sources and every item is
checkable: the grips and steps are among the things a visiting brother is examined on.

And all of it is stored the way physical skills are stored, not the way facts are.

I am going to be careful here, because embodied cognition is a research program rather
than a settled result, and its bolder formulations — that thought is *constituted* by bodily
state rather than merely influenced by it — remain contested.[^4] What is not contested is
the more modest proposition: that procedural memory and declarative memory are different
systems with different properties, that procedural memories are unusually durable, and that
material encoded with motor and spatial involvement is retained better than material read
from a page.

Applied to a man kneeling in the dark in 1740, that is an inference across two and a half
centuries and a great deal of cultural distance. I make it because it is the best available
account of why the Masonic apparatus works the way the record says it worked, and I label it
as inference.

---

### Three consequences

**It survives illiteracy.** For most of the period covered by this book, a substantial
proportion of the men joining lodges could read poorly or not at all. A ritual carried in
bodies and voices is available to them on equal terms. A ritual carried in books is not, and
would have produced a two-tier membership immediately.

**It survives distance.** A man carrying the ritual in his head carries it across an ocean
at no cost and with no risk of confiscation. This is not hypothetical: the eighteenth-century
military lodges that spread Freemasonry across the British Empire moved with regiments, and
what they moved was memory. A lodge could be constituted anywhere there were enough men who
knew the words.

**And it survives suppression.** Freemasonry was banned and its members persecuted under
several regimes in the twentieth century. Documents can be seized. What is in a man's body
cannot be, and lodges have reconstituted themselves after decades of prohibition from the
memories of survivors.

These are not mystical properties. They are the ordinary logistical advantages of storing
information in people rather than in objects, and they are very large advantages indeed.

---

### The cost

I should state the other side, because a chapter that only lists advantages is advocacy.

Memorized transmission *drifts*. Every generation loses a phrase and supplies another; every
lodge develops local habits; every jurisdiction diverges. The variation in Masonic ritual
across England, Scotland, Ireland, France and the American states is enormous, and a
substantial part of it is simply the accumulated error of oral transmission over three
centuries.

The Craft has tried to arrest this more than once. The most significant attempt followed the
union of the two English grand lodges in 1813, when a body called the Lodge of Reconciliation
was appointed to settle a common working from the rival traditions. It is, so far as I know,
the only occasion in Masonic history on which a named committee deliberately sat down to
determine what the ritual should be.[^5]

That episode deserves much more attention than it has had, and I flag it here as the single
most important piece of unexamined evidence bearing on this book's central question. If we
want to know whether Masonic ritual was ever *designed* — as opposed to accreted — the Lodge
of Reconciliation is where the evidence is. What they kept, what they discarded, and what
reasons they gave would tell us a great deal about whether anyone in the period understood
the apparatus as an apparatus.

I have not been able to examine those records for this book. It is the largest gap in it.

---

### What the body cannot tell us

The candidate kneels, and his knee is against the boards, and he says words after another
man in a room he cannot see.

We know he did that. We know what kinds of memory such conditions produce in human beings
generally. We do not know what he remembered, or how it felt, or whether he thought about
it again.

But we can say one thing that is not inference. **Fifty years later, if he was still
attending, he could still do it.** The examinations record it; the visiting system depended
on it; men who had not been in a lodge for a decade were tested and passed.

Whatever else the ceremony installed, it installed something durable enough to be produced
on demand after half a century.

That is not nothing, and it is measured rather than imagined.
