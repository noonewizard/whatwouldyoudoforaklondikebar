# CHAPTER EIGHT
## The Blindfold and the Passage

He is led around the room.

Not once. Three times in the first degree, in most workings, and a different number in the
others, always in the same direction, always with stops at the same points. At each station
a voice challenges him and his conductor answers. Then he moves on.

The exposures record the practice from the 1720s and it is one of the most consistent
features across jurisdictions that disagree about nearly everything else.[^1] It is also,
to a modern reader, the most obviously pointless thing in the ceremony. Why walk a
blindfolded man in circles?

---

### What he is actually doing

He is building a map.

Deprived of sight, a person in an unfamiliar space constructs a representation of it from
whatever channels remain: the sound of his own footsteps and their echo, the direction of
voices, the feel of the floor, the number of paces between events, the turns. This is not
optional and it is not conscious. It is what a nervous system does when it is moved through
a space it cannot see.

And the ceremony is unusually well arranged for it. The route is fixed. The stations are at
cardinal points. The same voices speak from the same positions. He is stopped, questioned,
and moved on, so that each segment of the journey is bounded by an event rather than
blurring into the next.

By the time the hoodwink comes off he has walked the room perhaps a dozen times without
seeing it.

**And when he does see it, he recognizes it.**

That is the effect, and it is worth dwelling on because it is strange and specific. A man
opens his eyes on a room he has never laid eyes on, and it is *already familiar*. He knows
where the east is. He knows the officers are at three points because he has been challenged
from three points. He knows the distances.

The sensation of recognizing a place you have never seen is uncommon in ordinary life, and
I would guess — this is speculation and I mark it as such — that it accounts for a
substantial part of what candidates across three centuries have described as the peculiar
impressiveness of that moment.

---

### Learning a hierarchy by walking it

There is a second thing happening, and it is the more consequential.

The room he is learning is not neutral. It has an east, where the principal officer sits,
often on a raised seat. It has a west and a south with subordinate officers. It has a
center. It has a guarded door.

He learns all of this in the dark, in the order the ritual chooses, by being stopped at each
station and required to account for himself.

Which means that **he acquires the lodge's authority structure as a spatial fact before
anybody explains it to him as a social one.** He does not learn that the Master is senior;
he learns that there is a voice in the east which the others defer to and to which he is
conducted last. He does not learn the officers' titles; he learns that there are three
positions from which he must be challenged before he can reach the center.

Nobody states any of it. He walks it.

I want to be careful not to over-claim here, because this is exactly the kind of point that
invites embroidery. The modest version is sufficient: information conveyed through
navigation is encoded differently from information conveyed through assertion, and a person
who has traversed a structure has a representation of it that a person who has been told
about it does not.

---

### Jonathan Z. Smith, again

In Chapter 2 I introduced Smith's argument that sacrality is a property of emplacement
rather than of substance — that ritual is what makes a place significant, not the reverse.
The circumambulation is where that argument becomes concrete.

Consider what actually distinguishes the lodge room from the dining room it was an hour ago
and will be an hour hence. Nothing material. Same boards, same walls, same ceiling. The
difference is a set of declarations — this is the east; the Master sits here; the door is
guarded — and a set of movements performed in accordance with them.

**The perambulation is the act by which the declarations become real.** A space is not
oriented until somebody moves through it as though it were. Walk the circuit, stop at the
stations, be challenged from the assigned points, and the tavern room has an east.

Smith was writing about other material and I am applying his framework at a considerable
distance. But Freemasonry is very nearly the ideal case for it, because the Craft has the
most architectural imagination of any tradition in this book and had, for most of its
history, no architecture whatever. It had to make the temple out of assertion and movement,
every time, in whatever room was available.

---

### The direction

A small point that illustrates how careful one has to be in this subject.

The perambulation is conducted in a fixed direction, and in most workings that direction is
clockwise — north, east, south, west, following the apparent course of the sun.

A very large popular literature treats this as evidence of solar worship, or of an
astronomical cult, or of survival from a pre-Christian sun religion. The reasoning is
always the same: the movement follows the sun, therefore it descends from something that
venerated the sun.

Apply the questions from Chapter 5 and the claim evaporates.

*Chronology* permits it, trivially — sun-following ritual movement is older than everything.
*Transmission*: nobody has ever produced a route. *Intertextuality*: Masonic sources explain
the direction by reference to the Temple and to the course of the sun as a natural emblem
of regularity, which is a stated and sufficient explanation requiring no ancient cult.
*Institutional continuity*: none.

And the parallel is worthless as evidence anyway, because clockwise circumambulation is
close to universal in cultures with any circular ritual movement at all. There are two
directions available. Half of everything goes one way.

What the sources actually support is duller and more useful: the lodge is modeled on a
building oriented east–west, the sun's course supplies the natural direction of travel in
such a building, and the Craft — which was busy identifying itself with geometry, order and
regularity — found in that a congenial emblem. No cult required.

---

### The half-of-everything rule

The clockwise case is worth generalizing, because it names a fallacy this book meets in
almost every chapter and the name is useful.

**Where a feature has only a small number of possible forms, a match between two traditions
carries almost no information.** Circumambulation can go clockwise or anticlockwise. A
candidate can be blindfolded or not. An initiation can happen at night or in daylight. A
ritual space can be oriented on a cardinal direction or not — and if it is, there are four
options, one of which is east, and east is where the sun rises, which is the reason any
tradition that picks one tends to pick it.

The literature treats such matches as evidence because each one, considered alone, feels
striking. The correct question is never *how likely is this match?* but **how many things
could the match have been?** Two traditions agreeing on a binary feature agree by chance half
the time. Two traditions agreeing on a four-way feature agree by chance a quarter of the time.
Agreement across four such features — night, blindfold, clockwise, east — looks overwhelming
and has a chance probability of about one in sixty-four, which, set against the hundreds of
initiatory traditions available for comparison and the freedom to choose which features to
count, is not a finding at all.

**What would carry information is a match on something arbitrary.** Not the direction of
movement but a specific number of circuits with a specific challenge at a specific point,
expressed in idiosyncratic wording, where dozens of alternatives were available and no
functional reason selects this one. That is the evidence of descent textual critics use, and
it is what Chapter 48 looks for across the whole comparative field and does not find.

The rule, stated for use: **count the alternatives before being impressed.** Most of the
parallels in this literature survive only because nobody does.

---

### The east that isn't east

And now the observation that disposes of an entire genre.

Masonic lodges met in hired rooms. A tavern's upper chamber does not face east because a
lodge wishes it to. The room has whatever orientation the building has, and the building was
put up by a landlord with no interest in the matter.

So the lodge's "east" is **wherever the Master's chair is placed.** It is a ritual
direction, not a magnetic one, and Masonic sources say so plainly — the Master is said to
be in the east, and the east is where he is.

This is fatal to every claim about astronomical alignment in Masonic practice. You cannot
align a rented room. The direction is declared, not surveyed, and the declaration is the
whole of it.

It is also, incidentally, further evidence for Smith's thesis. A tradition that could orient
its temple by fiat had discovered something about sacred space that took religious studies
until 1987 to articulate: that the orientation is an act, not a measurement.

---

### The limits, restated

What can be claimed from this chapter:

He walked a fixed route in the dark. He was challenged at fixed points. He acquired a
spatial representation of a room and of a hierarchy through movement rather than
instruction. When he could see, the room was already familiar.

What cannot:

That any of this produced a particular state in any particular man. That the effect was
intended. That the eighteenth century understood what it was doing.

The ceremony is now nearly at its center. He has been prepared, challenged, admitted and
conducted. He is about to be required to speak — and the language he will be required to
speak in is not the language he uses for anything else.
