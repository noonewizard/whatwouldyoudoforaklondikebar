# CHAPTER NINE
## The Voice

*Are you a Mason?*

*I am so taken and accepted among brothers and fellows.*

*How shall I know you to be a Mason?*

*By signs and tokens and perfect points of my entrance.*

*What are signs?*

*All squares, angles and perpendiculars.*

That is the shape of it, in the form the eighteenth-century exposures record, with wording
that varies by source and by jurisdiction and that I have given here as a representative
pattern rather than as any single authoritative text.[^1]

It is the oldest continuously transmitted content in Freemasonry. The Scottish manuscript of
1696 is already in this form, question and answer, and so is everything after it. Before the
third degree existed, before the tracing boards, before the moral lectures, before Anderson
— there was a catechism.

And it is not written the way people talk.

---

### Three things ritual language is not

**It is not conversation.** Conversation is improvised, redundant, interruptible and
tolerant of paraphrase. Ritual language is fixed. You cannot answer the catechism in your
own words; an answer in your own words is a wrong answer, even if it means the same thing.

**It is not explanation.** The catechism does not tell the candidate what things mean in any
expository sense. It asserts and it interrogates. *How many lights has a lodge? What
supports it? What is its covering?* The answers are short, concrete and frequently
enigmatic. Nothing is argued.

**It is not private.** This is the important one and I will come back to it.

---

### The catechism as a verification device

Before it is anything symbolic, the question-and-answer form is a piece of practical
technology, and the practical purpose explains more of its features than any esoteric reading
does.

The problem it solves is concrete. A man arrives at a lodge in a town where nobody knows him
and says he is a Mason. There are no membership cards in 1696, no central register, no
photographs, no telephone. The lodge has to decide whether to admit him — and admission
carries real consequences, since a member in distress has a claim on relief.

A catechism solves this in a way that a password does not. A single word can be overheard,
guessed, or sold. A structured examination of thirty exchanges cannot: it requires the
candidate to produce the right answer to an unpredictable question, in the right form, with
the right responses to follow-up. It tests not possession of a token but **fluency in a
system**, which can only be acquired by having been taught it by someone who had it.

Several otherwise puzzling features follow directly from this function.

**Why it is memorised verbatim rather than paraphrased.** A paraphrase would pass a test of
understanding and fail a test of provenance. The examiner is not checking whether the visitor
grasps the meaning; he is checking whether the visitor learned the words from a legitimate
source. Exact wording is the evidence. This is why jurisdictional variation matters so much
in practice and why visitors from other constitutions have historically needed accommodation.

**Why the questions are asked by one man and answered by another.** The form distributes the
knowledge: neither party holds the whole thing alone, and the exchange only exists when both
are present. Chapter 36 develops this into the argument about what kind of thing the Masonic
secret is.

**And why it survived the exposures.** A published catechism can be memorised by an impostor,
and this is exactly what the lodges feared when *Masonry Dissected* appeared in 1730. The
institution's response — the alteration of certain details, the reliance on personal vouching
alongside examination — tells us that the form was understood at the time as a security
mechanism and was defended as one.

None of this excludes the other things the catechism does, which the rest of this chapter
examines. But an account of ritual language that begins with mystery and works backward will
miss the fact that the oldest surviving content in Freemasonry has an entirely mundane job to
do, and does it well.

---

### Why formulae

The fixity has an obvious practical explanation and it is correct as far as it goes: **fixed
forms survive oral transmission and free forms do not.**

Anyone who has played the game where a sentence is whispered round a circle knows that
ordinary prose degrades within a few transmissions. Formulaic language degrades far more
slowly, because it has redundancy built into it — rhythm, parallelism, alliteration, fixed
pairs. *Squares, angles and perpendiculars* is easier to carry than a sentence conveying the
same content, and it is easier to *check*: a man who gets one word wrong is audibly wrong,
whereas a man who paraphrases is merely different.

This is the machinery by which oral traditions everywhere have transmitted material across
centuries, and the Craft is a textbook case. The 1696 catechism and its successors are
memorisation documents in form as well as function.

But there is a second reason, and it is the one that matters for this book.

**Fixed language cannot be owned.**

If the answer must be given in prescribed words, then giving it correctly is not an
expression of the speaker's view. It is a performance of the tradition's. A man who recites
the catechism is not saying what he thinks; he is demonstrating that he holds what everyone
holds, in the form everyone holds it.

That is a remarkable social device, and it is why the same apparatus works for men who
disagree about everything else. Anderson's *Constitutions* of 1723 removed doctrinal
requirement from Masonic membership — the famous formula about the religion in which all men
agree — and it could afford to, because the ritual does not ask anyone what they believe. It
asks them to say the words.

---

### Dialogue as an object

Now the property I flagged above, which I have not seen noticed in the Masonic literature
and which I think is the most interesting thing about the catechism.

**It only exists between two people.**

A catechism is not a text a person can hold. Half of it is questions. A man alone with the
whole thing memorised is in the position of someone who knows both parts of a duet: he
possesses the material and cannot perform it.

To produce the knowledge, two people have to agree to produce it together, in a specific
order, each holding up his half.

Consider what that does.

**It makes membership continuously demonstrable.** You are a Mason to the extent that you
can answer, and answering requires someone to ask. Status is not a possession but a
repeatable transaction.

**It makes the examination of strangers possible.** This was the original practical purpose
and it persisted for centuries: a travelling man presents himself at a strange lodge and is
examined, and the examination is precisely this — a dialogue in which each party
demonstrates that he holds the other half.

**And it makes solitary defection useless.** A man who leaves the fraternity takes the words
with him and can do nothing with them alone. He can publish them, as Prichard did in 1730,
and the publication is inert, because printing a catechism does not produce a lodge.

That last point is the answer to a question the previous volume raised and could not fully
close: why did publishing the secrets change nothing?

Part of the answer is the one I gave there — that the secret was performative rather than
informational. But there is a more precise version available here. **The Masonic secret is
not a fact; it is a two-person procedure.** You cannot steal a procedure by printing it any
more than you can steal a handshake by describing one.

---

### Rhythm and the recitative voice

A note on delivery, which the printed sources cannot capture and which anyone who has heard
ritual performed will recognise.

Ritual language across traditions is typically delivered in a register distinct from
speech: slower, more evenly stressed, pitched differently, with pauses at fixed points.
Liturgy is chanted or intoned. Legal oaths are recited. Military drill is barked.

This is not affectation. Marking language as *not-ordinary* is the auditory equivalent of
marking a space as not-ordinary, and it does the same work — Bell's ritualization, in
sound. A sentence delivered in the ritual register is audibly a different kind of act from
the same sentence delivered conversationally, and everyone present knows it without being
told.

It also assists memory. Rhythmic, evenly stressed delivery is easier to hold and easier to
retrieve, which is why we remember songs we have not heard for thirty years and cannot
remember prose we read last week.

---

### Saying it yourself

The catechism is the tradition's voice. The obligation is the candidate's.

At the centre of the ceremony, after the perambulation and before the light, he is required
to speak — not to answer a question but to make a promise, repeating it phrase by phrase
after the Master, in the first person, kneeling, in a room full of witnesses.

Everything in the preceding forty minutes has been arranged by other people. He has been
described in the third person, conducted where he did not choose to go, and asked questions
whose answers were supplied for him.

**This is the first thing he does.**

And what he does is bind himself, in his own voice, in words he did not compose, to
conditions he has not been permitted to read in advance.

That combination — his voice, someone else's words, unread terms, public delivery — is the
subject of the next chapter, and it is where the psychological literature on this subject is
at once most tempting and least reliable.
