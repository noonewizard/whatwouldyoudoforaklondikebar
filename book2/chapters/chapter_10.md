# CHAPTER TEN
## The Oath and the Witnesses

The penalties are the part everybody knows.

Having one's throat cut across, the tongue torn out by the root, the body buried in the
sands of the sea at low-water mark — these appear in the eighteenth-century exposures, they
were quoted with relish by every anti-Masonic writer from the 1720s onward, and they have
supplied three centuries of people who wanted Freemasonry to be sinister with their best
material.[^1]

They were never carried out. There is no recorded instance of a Masonic body inflicting a
physical penalty on anyone for revealing anything, and the men who wrote and recited these
formulae had, by all evidence, no expectation that they would be. Several English
jurisdictions removed or modified the penal clauses during the twentieth century, and the
institution was unaffected, which tells you what they were doing.[^2]

What they were doing is the subject of this chapter, and it turns out to be more interesting
than either a threat or an embarrassment.

---

### Where the language comes from

The penalties are not a Masonic invention and they are not occult. They belong to a
recognisable family of early modern oath formulae.

Oaths of that period routinely specified consequences, often physical, often anatomically
vivid, invoked upon the swearer by his own mouth. Guild oaths, apprenticeship bonds, legal
compurgation, oaths of allegiance, the oaths sworn by jurors and officeholders — the genre
is full of imprecatory language, and the imprecation is understood by everyone involved as a
way of *weighting the promise*, not as a schedule of punishments anyone intends to
administer.

A man who says *may God strike me down* is not describing a procedure. He is putting
something at stake in order to mean it.

So the Masonic penalties are, in their historical context, an ordinary piece of period
legal-devotional rhetoric that Freemasonry preserved after the rest of the culture had
abandoned it — which is how they came to look so strange by the nineteenth century, and so
lurid by the twenty-first.

That is a useful corrective, and it is not the whole story. The formulae *are* unusually
elaborate, and the Craft kept them long after their cultural context had dissolved, and
something in the apparatus evidently wanted them.

---

### What the obligation actually does

Set the penalties aside and look at the structure of the act.

The candidate is kneeling. His hands are placed on a book he cannot see. He repeats, phrase
by phrase after the Master, a promise in the first person. He has not read it. He is hearing
each clause a fraction of a second before he says it.

Witnesses are present. In some workings they stand. The room is silent.

At the end he performs a physical act — in most workings, kissing the book.

Then he is told he may rise, and from that moment everyone in the room treats him
differently.

**Six features, each doing work:**

*It is spoken, not signed.* A signature is a mark made by a hand. A spoken promise is made
with the whole apparatus of the body and is heard by the promiser.

*It is in the first person.* Not *members undertake* but *I do hereby and hereon solemnly
and sincerely swear*.

*It is repeated after another.* Which means he cannot pace it, cannot skim, cannot decline
a clause without stopping the ceremony in front of everyone.

*It is public.* The witnesses are not decoration; they are the mechanism by which the
promise becomes a social fact rather than a private intention.

*It is physically marked.* Kneeling, hands placed, book kissed.

*And it is irreversible in form.* There is no procedure for un-swearing.

---

### The literature, and its problem

Here is where I have to do something that most books on this subject do not, and it is the
reason this chapter exists in the form it does.

There is a famous experimental finding that appears to explain all of this, and it is
routinely invoked in discussions of fraternal initiation. In 1959 Elliot Aronson and Judson
Mills published a study in which participants who underwent a more severe initiation in
order to join a discussion group subsequently rated that group more favourably than
participants who underwent a mild initiation or none. The interpretation — that people
resolve the dissonance of having paid a high price for something dull by revaluing the
thing — became one of the best-known results in social psychology.[^3]

It is, on its face, precisely a theory of why Masonic initiation works.

**And I am not going to lean on it, because its replication record is mixed.**

I want to be exact about what I am saying. I am not saying the finding is false. I am saying
that it is a single small study from 1959, conducted on a narrow population under conditions
very unlike a Masonic lodge, that the broader dissonance literature it belongs to has been
among the areas affected by psychology's replication problems, and that the specific
severity-of-initiation effect has not been established with the robustness that its
citation frequency implies.

A great many books reach for Aronson and Mills at exactly this point, cite it as though it
were a law of nature, and move on. Doing so would be the same error this book spends
Chapter 38 warning against: taking a striking result and treating its strikingness as
evidence.

So the honest position is narrower. **The general proposition that public, voluntary,
effortful commitment shapes subsequent behaviour and self-description more than private or
costless assent is reasonably well supported across several literatures.** The specific,
quantified claim that severity of initiation produces group liking is not something I can
responsibly build on.

That is less satisfying. It is also what the evidence supports.

### The witnesses

The room is the part that gets least attention and may matter most.

A promise made alone is a resolution. A promise made in front of twelve men who know your
name, your trade and your address is a different category of object — and the twelve are not
passive. They have all made the same promise. Most of them have watched it made many times.
They are, in a precise sense, a body constituted by shared obligation, and the candidate is
being added to it in their presence.

Two consequences follow, and both leave records.

**Enforcement is social, not penal.** The throat-cutting was never the sanction. The sanction
was that these men, whom you see monthly and do business with, would know. Lodge minutes
record the actual mechanism: charges brought, committees appointed, members admonished,
suspended and excluded.[^4] The penalties in the obligation are theatre; the ballot and the
exclusion list are the enforcement.

**And the obligation is mutual.** The candidate swears to the lodge, but every man in the
room has sworn to him. What he is joining is a web of undertakings, and the ceremony is the
moment he is knotted into it.

This is where Masonic charity comes from, and it should be said plainly because it is the
most consequential thing the institution ever did. Masonic relief — to distressed brethren,
to widows, to orphans — ran into very large sums across three centuries and is documented in
accounts rather than in rhetoric. It was not sentiment. It was the cashing-out of an
obligation sworn in a room.

---

### The strange asymmetry

One observation to close, because it has bothered me throughout.

The obligation is the most dramatic moment in the ceremony and the one with the most
elaborate language. And its content is almost entirely *negative*: he swears what he will
not reveal, will not do, will not wrong.

The positive content — aid a brother, keep his lawful secrets, answer his sign — is real but
comparatively brief.

If you wanted to design a ceremony that produced virtuous men, you would presumably load the
dramatic centre with positive undertakings. Masonry loads it with prohibitions and
concealment.

Two readings are available. The uncharitable one is that the institution's deepest concern
was always its own boundary rather than its members' conduct — that the apparatus is a
membership device wearing a moral vocabulary.

The charitable one, and I think the better-evidenced, is that the negative form is doing
something the positive could not. **A prohibition is checkable and a virtue is not.** You
cannot verify that a man is behaving uprightly; you can verify that he has not disclosed the
words. A boundary that can be policed sustains an institution; an aspiration cannot.

And the moral content is not absent — it is simply placed elsewhere. It arrives two minutes
later, when the hoodwink comes off, in the form of a book and two tools and an instruction
about how to live.

Which is, in its way, a better piece of design than loading it into the oath. He swears in
the dark about what he will not say. He is told about virtue in the light, holding the
instruments of a trade.

The ceremony puts the fear before the instruction, and then gives him the instruction at the
moment he can see.
