# PART III — THE ARCHITECTURE OF THE SOUL

# CHAPTER ELEVEN
## Solomon's Temple

The previous volume established what the Masonic Temple is made of, and I will state the
finding in a paragraph rather than re-argue it.

Everything in it derives from available sources. The dimensions are from 1 Kings and 2
Chronicles. The pillars, with their names, are from 1 Kings 7:21. The craftsman Hiram is
from 1 Kings 7:13–14, where he makes bronze vessels and is neither an architect nor
murdered. The visual imagination is substantially Villalpando's — the enormous illustrated
reconstruction published by two Spanish Jesuits between 1596 and 1604, which gave educated
Europe a detailed picture of a building nobody had seen. There is no archaeological
knowledge in Masonic ritual and no evidence of any independent channel.[^1]

That settles the provenance question. It leaves the more interesting one untouched.

**Why did this building, out of all the buildings available, become the organising image of
the most successful initiatory system in modern history?**

---

### Six properties of an ideal symbol

I have come to think the answer is that the Temple has, by accident, a combination of
properties that no other candidate possesses — and that the combination is what did the
work, not the theology.

**It is destroyed.** This is the first and most important. A building that still stood could
be visited, measured, and disagreed about. Somebody could go and check. The Temple is
available only as description, which means it is available only as imagination — and
imagination is the one faculty every member brings and no member can be wrong about.

**It was built to a given plan.** The text is emphatic that the design was disclosed, not
devised. Which means the Temple can stand for any undertaking executed according to
specifications the builder did not write — which is to say, for a life.

**It was built by a workforce.** The biblical account gives numbers: tens of thousands of
labourers, hewers and bearers of burdens, with overseers. It is not one architect's vision.
It is a *collective* achievement with a division of labour and a hierarchy of skill, and
that maps onto a body of apprentices, fellows and masters with no adjustment whatever.

**It is sacred without being sectarian.** Jews, Christians and Muslims all recognise it. In
1723 Anderson made non-sectarian religion the condition of Masonic membership, and a room
containing Anglicans, Dissenters, Catholics and — within a few years — Jews needed a
religious symbol that none of them would contest. There is precisely one building in the
world that qualifies.

**It has a threshold, an orientation and a centre.** Porch, pillars, holy place, holy of
holies. The Temple is not a hall; it is a graded sequence of spaces with access restricted
at each stage. A system of three degrees is already latent in the floor plan.

**And it can be rebuilt inwardly.** This is the move that makes the whole thing work, and it
is not a Masonic invention — it is seventeen centuries older. Paul asked the Corinthians
whether they did not know that they were the temple of God. Once the building is gone, the
injunction to build it becomes available as metaphor, and it had been available, in every
sermon in Europe, long before anyone thought of applying it to a lodge.[^2]

---

### The lodge as a model of it

The practical consequence is that the lodge room is not a place. It is a *plan*.

Masonic sources describe the lodge as situated east to west, as the Temple was. It has
pillars, or representations of them. It has a chequered pavement. It has a raised east. Its
officers occupy positions that correspond to points in a structure that has not existed for
two thousand years.

Which produces a fact I find genuinely strange and worth sitting with: **every Masonic lodge
in the world is a working model of a building known only from a text.** Every member who
walks that floor is walking a reconstruction of a reconstruction of a description.

And because it is a plan rather than a place, it can be laid out anywhere. This is the
portability argument from Chapter 2, arriving from the other direction. A tradition whose
sacred architecture is a *diagram* needs no architecture at all. You need a room, an
agreement about which wall is east, and men who know where to stand.

The Temple travels because the Temple was never anywhere.

---

### The unfinished building

There is a detail in the biblical account that Masonic tradition seized on and that
repays attention.

The Temple is completed. Solomon dedicates it. And then, in the tradition Freemasonry
actually works with, the master builder is killed before the work is done, and the word that
would have completed the transmission dies with him.

That is not in the Bible. It is the Masonic legend, first in print in 1730, and Chapter 23
takes it up properly. What I want to note here is the architectural consequence.

**Masonry's Temple is permanently unfinished.**

Not ruined — unfinished. There is a difference. A ruin is a completed thing that decayed; an
unfinished building is a thing still owed work. And the whole moral apparatus of the Craft
depends on the second reading: the candidate is told that he is a stone being prepared for a
building that is still going up, and the building is never described as complete.

A symbol of perfection would be inert. You would either match it or not. A symbol of
*ongoing construction* generates obligation indefinitely, because there is always more
course to lay.

I do not think anyone designed that. I think the Craft inherited a legend about an
interrupted work and discovered, over generations, that the interruption was more useful
than a completion would have been.

---

### What the Temple does not do

Two things need saying, because both are claimed constantly.

**It does not encode measurements.** The proportional and numerical claims made for Masonic
Temple symbolism — that the dimensions encode astronomical constants, or sacred ratios, or
the Earth's circumference — are subject to the tests in Chapter 5 and Chapter 38, and they
fail them in the ordinary way. The search space is enormous; the procedures are chosen after
the fact; nobody runs a control. A building described in cubits whose length is disputed,
transmitted through two textual traditions that disagree with each other, is not a reliable
substrate for numerical discovery.

**And it does not make the lodge a temple in any religious sense.** This is worth saying
plainly because it is the point at which Masonic and anti-Masonic writing most often agree
with each other and are both wrong. The lodge is not a place of worship. It does not
sacrifice, it does not administer sacraments, it has no clergy, and its members were and are
overwhelmingly members of actual religions which they attended on Sundays. The Temple is a
*model*, in the engineer's sense — a structure borrowed for its shape.

The men who built this system were not constructing a religion. They were constructing a
room in which men of different religions could stand together, and they needed a shape that
all of them would recognise and none of them would own.

---

### The one building everyone could agree about

Which brings me to the thing I did not expect to find when I started this chapter.

The Temple's selection is usually explained mystically — that Masonry chose it because of
its profundity, its antiquity, its hidden wisdom.

I think the explanation is nearly the opposite, and much more impressive.

**They chose it because it was the only sacred building in Europe's inheritance that
belonged to nobody in the room.**

A cathedral is Catholic. A meeting-house is Dissenting. A synagogue is Jewish. Any actual
building in eighteenth-century Europe came with a confession attached, and a fraternity that
had just removed confession from its membership requirements could not use one.

Solomon's Temple is in the scriptures of three religions, is claimed exclusively by none of
them in its Masonic use, is physically gone, and cannot be visited by anyone to check whose
it is.

It is the perfect neutral ground, and neutral ground was exactly what the institution
needed.

Which means the Craft's most mystical-looking choice may be its most practical — and that
pattern, a practical solution acquiring a mystical explanation afterwards, is going to
recur in every chapter of this part.
