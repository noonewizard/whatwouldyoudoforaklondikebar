# CHAPTER TWELVE
## Building the Invisible Temple

In 1926 the Detroit Masonic Temple opened with a tower of fourteen storeys, a theatre
seating some five thousand people, a ballroom, a chapel, a drill hall, and — by most
accounts — over a thousand rooms. It remains the largest Masonic building in the world.[^1]

Ninety years earlier, in the same country, a lodge would have met upstairs in a tavern.

Something changed, and this chapter is about what — because the change cuts directly against
the argument I have been making for eleven chapters.

---

### The claim so far

I have argued that Masonic ritual space is *constructed rather than grand*: that the lodge
works by arrangement — an east declared, stations assigned, a threshold guarded — and not by
architecture, and that this is precisely why the form travelled. A tradition needing a
cathedral stays where the cathedral is. A tradition needing a diagram crosses oceans.

That argument is well supported for the eighteenth century and most of the nineteenth. It is
the reason lodges could be opened in warehouses, garrison tents, ships and prisoner-of-war
camps.

And then, from roughly the 1850s, Freemasonry started building temples the size of railway
stations.

If the architecture never mattered, why?

---

### The moral architecture first

Before the anomaly, the tradition it interrupted — because the Craft's oldest architectural
doctrine is explicitly *anti*-architectural.

The core instruction is that the candidate is himself the building. He is told that the
lodge is a symbol of the world and of the man; that he should erect in his own heart a
temple to virtue; that the tools in his hand are for working on himself. William Preston's
lecture system, from 1772 onward, develops this at length, and the monitorial literature of
the nineteenth century repeats it constantly.[^2]

The formulation is not original to Masonry. Paul had it, and so did a millennium and a half
of Christian preaching in between. What the Craft adds is the *trade vocabulary*: not an
abstract inner temple but one built with a square, a level, a plumb-rule and a gauge, to a
plan, by a workman who is also the work.

And the move has a consequence the Craft's own writers recognised early. **If the real
temple is internal, then no external building matters at all.**

That is why the tavern arrangement was never felt as a deficiency. A lodge in a public house
is not a compromised version of a lodge in a temple; on the Craft's own doctrine it is the
same thing, because the significant construction is happening inside the men present.

---

### The anomaly

So the nineteenth-century building programme needs explaining rather than admiring, and it
is a substantial phenomenon: purpose-built Masonic halls and temples across Britain,
continental Europe and above all the United States, increasingly large, increasingly
monumental, frequently classical or Egyptian in idiom, culminating in buildings like the
House of the Temple in Washington and the Detroit and Philadelphia temples.

Four explanations are available. They are not exclusive and I think three of them are
substantially right.

**1. They could afford it.** The most boring explanation and probably the largest. American
Masonic membership grew enormously through the later nineteenth century, and a fraternity
with hundreds of thousands of dues-paying members has capital. Organisations with capital
build.

**2. Post-Morgan respectability.** American Freemasonry had been nearly destroyed by the
Anti-Masonic reaction after 1826 and spent the following half-century rebuilding a public
reputation. A large, permanent, conspicuous building on the main street is an argument about
legitimacy. It says: we are not a conspiracy meeting over a bar; we are an institution, like
the bank and the courthouse.

**3. The high degrees needed stages.** This is the practical driver and it is
under-appreciated. Craft ritual requires almost nothing. The Scottish Rite degrees, as
reworked in America in the later nineteenth century, are *theatre* — costumed, scenic,
sometimes with painted backdrops, lighting effects and casts of dozens. You cannot stage
that in a tavern room. The Detroit theatre seating five thousand is not a metaphor; it is a
theatre.

**4. The symbolism had become meaningful in a new way.** And this is the explanation that
would support this book's thesis, which is exactly why I distrust it most.

The argument would run: by the later nineteenth century, Masonic symbolism had been
reinterpreted — by Pike, by the Theosophists, by the whole occult revival — as an esoteric
system of cosmic significance. A tradition that has come to understand its own imagery as
sacred architecture will eventually want to build some.

**Test it.** If explanation 4 is doing the work, we would expect the monumental temples to
be commissioned by the bodies most committed to the esoteric reading, to be justified in
esoteric terms in their building records, and to embody specific symbolic programmes in
their design.

Some of that is true. The House of the Temple, designed by John Russell Pope for the
Scottish Rite's Southern Jurisdiction and completed in 1915, is modelled on the Mausoleum at
Halicarnassus and is dense with deliberate symbolic programme. That is a building that means
something on purpose.

But it is also the headquarters of the body Albert Pike had led, built by the most
intellectually ambitious branch of American Masonry, and it is not typical. The ordinary
Masonic hall of 1890 in an Ohio county town is a large brick building with a lodge room
upstairs and shops let on the ground floor to pay for it.

**Verdict:** explanations 1, 2 and 3 account for most of the phenomenon. Explanation 4
accounts for some of the flagship buildings and should not be extended beyond them.

I record this because it is a case where the evidence declines to support the reading most
congenial to my argument, and a book that only reports the congenial findings is not doing
its job.

---

### What the buildings cost the tradition

An observation that I have not seen made and that I think follows.

The monumental temples arrived at the moment American Masonic membership peaked, and they
outlived it. Membership in the United States declined steeply from the mid-twentieth century
onward, and the buildings remained — enormous, expensive, and increasingly empty.

The Detroit Masonic Temple, built for a fraternity that thought it would keep growing, is now
substantially a commercial concert venue. Masonic halls across Britain and America have been
sold, converted, demolished.

There is a lesson in that about the argument of this part of the book. **The tradition's
portable period was its expansive one.** When the Craft needed nothing but a room and an
agreement, it spread across the world. When it acquired buildings, it acquired fixed costs,
fixed locations and an obligation to fill them.

I would not want to push this into a moral. But it is at least consistent with the claim
that the apparatus was never architectural, and that the century in which Freemasonry
forgot this was the century in which it began to contract.

---

### The temple that is not there

Return to the doctrine.

A Mason is told that he is building a temple in his heart, and that the tools for it are a
set of instruments for testing whether things are true, level and upright. He is told the
work is never finished.

That instruction requires no building, no priest, no altar, no sacrifice and no belief
beyond the minimum Anderson specified in 1723. It can be carried out in a tavern, a tent or
a prison camp, and has been.

It is also, considered coldly, an unfalsifiable and entirely private undertaking with no
external criterion of success. Nobody can inspect another man's inner temple. Nobody can
fail.

Which raises the question this part of the book has to answer eventually, and which Chapter
45 will take up: **does a symbolic instruction with no external criterion change anyone's
behaviour?**

Before that there are the instruments themselves — a set of objects from a workman's bag
that were converted, at a datable moment, into a complete ethical vocabulary.
