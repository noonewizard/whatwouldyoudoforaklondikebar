# CHAPTER THIRTEEN
## The Rough and the Perfect Ashlar

On the pedestals of most lodges there are two stones.

One is irregular — as it comes from the quarry, with rough faces and no true angle. The
other is squared: six flat faces, right angles, ready to be laid into a wall and bedded true
against its neighbors.

They are a teaching aid. The candidate is told, in language that varies by jurisdiction but
not in substance, that the first represents him as he is and the second as he may become,
and that the instruments by which one becomes the other are in his own hands.[^1]

It is the simplest piece of symbolism in Freemasonry and I think it is the deepest, because
of something it does that the tradition never quite says out loud.

---

### The doubling

The candidate is the stone.

He is also the mason.

Both of these are told to him in the same ceremony, with the same set of objects, and the
Craft does not appear to find the combination remarkable. He is handed a gavel and a chisel
and informed that with them he is to work the rough ashlar; and he is informed that he is
the rough ashlar.

Which means he occupies, simultaneously, the position of the craftsman and the position of
the material.

I want to take this slowly, because it is the structural center of the whole system and it
is easy to read past as a pleasant metaphor.

A stone does not shape itself. That is the entire logic of masonry: the material is inert
and the craftsman acts on it from outside, according to a plan the stone does not have. The
relation is asymmetrical and it is the asymmetry that makes building possible.

Apply that to a person and the asymmetry collapses. There is no external craftsman. There is
nobody standing outside the man with the chisel. The instruction is that he must do to
himself what a mason does to a stone, which requires him to be simultaneously the agent who
knows the plan and the substance that does not.

**This is not a paradox the Craft invented. It is the structure of every project of
self-improvement that has ever been undertaken**, and it is genuinely strange whenever
anyone looks at it directly. You cannot be improved without being both the improver and the
improved, and the improver has to be somehow ahead of the improved, or there would be
nothing to aim at.

---

### The same doubling elsewhere

Here I have to be careful, because this is precisely the point at which the popular
literature reaches for descent and finds it everywhere.

The doubling appears in several traditions this book touches.

**In alchemy**, the operator works on a material which the tradition repeatedly tells him is
also himself. *Solve et coagula*: dissolve and reconstitute — and the texts are ambiguous,
deliberately, about what is being dissolved.

**In Iamblichan theurgy**, the practitioner performs an operation whose object is his own
ascent. He is both the one conducting the rite and the thing the rite is for.

**In Hermetic regeneration**, as described in the thirteenth treatise of the *Corpus
Hermeticum*, the initiate undergoes a rebirth which he must also in some sense bring about.

**And in the Masonic ashlar**, the same shape.

The temptation is obvious: four traditions, one structure, therefore a transmission. The
previous volume spent a great deal of effort demonstrating that no such transmission
existed, at least not before the eighteenth century, and I am not going to re-run the
argument.

What I want to propose instead is the explanation that the absence of transmission forces on
us.

**They arrive at the same structure because the structure is not a doctrine. It is a
constraint.**

Any system whatever that sets out to change a person *through that person's own deliberate
action* must produce this doubling, because the doubling is what deliberate self-change
consists of. It is not a secret shared between traditions. It is the shape of the problem,
and anyone working seriously on the problem will find it.

That is a weaker claim than descent and a much more interesting one, because it applies to
things that have nothing to do with each other. A person learning an instrument, a patient
in therapy, an athlete in training and a novice in a monastery are all in this position, and
none of them inherited it from Hermes.

---

### Why a stone

Granted the doubling, why express it with masonry rather than anything else?

Because the trade supplied it, is the honest first answer, and this book has tried to be
consistent about the difference between explaining why a thing exists and explaining why it
works.

But the stone is unusually well suited, and three features are worth naming.

**The change is subtractive.** A mason does not add anything to an ashlar. He removes. The
perfect stone was inside the rough one the whole time and the work consists of taking away
what is not it. That is a specific and demanding model of self-improvement, and it is
different from the model in which a person acquires virtues they lacked. The Craft's
imagery says the material is already adequate and the work is removal.

**The standard is external and indifferent.** The stone is not finished when the mason feels
it is finished. It is finished when it passes the square — and the square does not know who
is holding it. Chapter 14 takes this up, because it is the whole moral theory of Freemasonry
in one object.

**And the stone is for a wall.** This is the feature most often lost. The perfect ashlar is
not an end in itself; it is *fit to be built with*. It has been squared so that it will bed
true against other stones. The individual perfection is instrumental to a collective
structure, and a stone that was beautifully finished but would not sit in a course would be
a failure.

That last point is the answer to a charge sometimes levelled at Masonic moral teaching —
that it is a private self-improvement scheme with no social content. The imagery says
otherwise, explicitly and from the beginning. You are being squared *in order to be built
with*.

---

### What the stones cannot tell us

Two limits.

**The symbolism is not alchemical, on the evidence.** The parallel with *prima materia* and
the Stone is real and has been noticed by every writer on the subject since the eighteenth
century. It fails the transmission test comprehensively: no document, no route, no shared
vocabulary — the Masonic language is architectural throughout, with no sulphur, no mercury,
no nigredo. The previous volume set this out at length. What survives is the convergence
argument above, which is better.

**And the instruction has no criterion.** Nobody can inspect another man's inner ashlar.
There is no examination, no measurement, no point at which anyone is certified as squared.
The tradition tells a man that he is being worked on and never tells him whether it took.

I said in the last chapter that this is unfalsifiable, and it is. But I want to add something
now that complicates the criticism.

**An instruction without a criterion is not necessarily an instruction without an effect.**
A man who has been told, in circumstances he will remember for fifty years, that he is
unfinished material and that the tools are in his hands, has been given a self-description.
Self-descriptions do things. Whether they do enough to show up in behavior is the question
Chapter 45 is for, and the answer there is more equivocal than either the Craft's defenders
or its critics would like.

What can be said here is narrower and, I think, secure. The Craft found a way to state the
problem of self-change in two objects that a man can hold in his hands, in a form that
requires no doctrine, no metaphysics and no literacy.

For an institution trying to say something serious to a room containing a duke, an
apothecary and a man who could not read, that is not a small achievement.
