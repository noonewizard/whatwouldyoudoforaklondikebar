# CHAPTER FOURTEEN
## The Square and the Compasses

It is the most widely recognised emblem in this book and possibly the most widely recognised
emblem in the world that is not a national flag or a corporate logo. It is on lodge doors in
Accra and gravestones in Ohio, on rings and watch-fobs and the sides of buildings, and the
overwhelming majority of people who recognise it could tell you only that it means
Freemasons.

Two draughtsman's instruments, interlocked, sometimes with a letter between them.

This chapter is not about where they came from — the previous volume dated the layers and
the finding was that everything documented is craft-derived or biblical and everything
esoteric is nineteenth century. This chapter is about what a symbol like this *does*, and
the square and compasses is the best available specimen for the question, because its
meanings can be dated and therefore counted.

---

### Eight layers

Here is what the emblem carries, with the earliest attestation of each layer. This is the
worked example of what I will call symbolic compression, and the point of setting it out
this way is that compression is usually discussed as a mystical property and is in fact a
historical process with a chronology.

**Layer 1 — A tool for testing a right angle.** Immemorial. Egyptian tomb paintings show
squares; Roman examples survive; every building culture that uses rectangles has one. Not a
symbol at all: a fact about the trade.

**Layer 2 — The emblem of a trade.** Medieval. Guilds and companies used their tools as
identifying devices — shears for clothworkers, a last for cobblers, compasses for masons.
The London Company of Masons' arms include them. Masons' gravestones across early modern
Europe carry them. Still not esoteric: this is a signboard.

**Layer 3 — The practical form of a liberal science.** The Old Charges, from about 1390.
Masonry is geometry; geometry is one of the seven liberal sciences; therefore the mason's
tools are the instruments of a philosophy. This is the layer that makes everything after it
possible, and Chapter 16 is about it.

**Layer 4 — Rectitude of conduct.** By the 1720s at latest, and probably earlier: "on the
square" is idiomatic English by the seventeenth century. The transfer from physical
straightness to moral straightness is so natural that the language had already made it
before Freemasonry got there.

**Layer 5 — The standard that does not care who you are.** Eighteenth-century moral
lectures. This is the developed version and it is a real ethical proposition, which I will
come to.

**Layer 6 — A badge of membership.** Eighteenth to nineteenth century, becoming a *logo* —
the two instruments interlocked, standing alone, on buildings, certificates, jewels, aprons
and eventually mass-manufactured regalia.

**Layer 7 — Polarity: matter and spirit, passive and active, terrestrial and celestial.**
From the 1850s. This is Éliphas Lévi's world and it arrives in Masonry substantially through
Albert Pike, who took a great deal from him. It is absent from every eighteenth-century
Masonic source.

**Layer 8 — A marker of conspiracy.** Twentieth century, and mostly the second half of it.

**Count them: four documented Masonic layers, two pre-Masonic trade facts, two later
additions.**

---

### What compression is

Now the theoretical point, and I want to make it carefully because "symbols contain multiple
meanings" is the sort of observation that sounds profound and explains nothing.

A symbol of this kind is not a container into which meanings were placed. It is an object
that has been used by a great many people, for a long time, in a context that declined to
fix what it meant — and the layers are the sediment of that use.

Three conditions produce it, and all three are historically identifiable in this case.

**A concrete object.** Not an abstract sign. You can hold a square. It has a shape that does
something, and every meaning attached to it is anchored to that function: the square *tests*,
so its meanings are about testing.

**Long use by many people.** Three centuries and millions of members, each of whom
encountered it in a ceremony designed to make it memorable.

**And an institution that refused to define it.** This is the decisive condition and it is
Freemasonry's structural peculiarity. There is no Masonic magisterium. Nobody has ever been
authorised to say what the square means. Anderson's *Constitutions* of 1723 removed
doctrinal authority from the institution in order to admit men of different religions, and
the consequence — probably unintended — was that it could never afterwards adjudicate an
interpretation of anything.

**A symbol under those three conditions accumulates. It cannot do anything else.**

And the accumulation has a direction: new layers are added and old ones are almost never
removed. A modern Mason may hold layers 3, 4, 5 and 6 simultaneously and be dimly aware of 7
from his reading, and he is not confused. He is holding a symbol that has been in service for
six hundred years.

---

### The ethical proposition

Layer 5 deserves its own treatment because it is the most substantial thing Craft Masonry
has to say and it is usually passed over as platitude.

Consider what the instruments actually are. A square tests whether an angle is true. A pair
of compasses defines a limit. Neither *makes* anything. They are instruments of
verification: their entire function is to determine whether something conforms to a standard
that is external, impersonal and not negotiable.

Transfer that to conduct, and you have an ethics with no metaphysics in it.

To act on the square is to act correctly, and correctness is not a matter of opinion,
because a right angle is not a matter of opinion. The standard does not know your rank. It
does not know whether you are the Grand Master or the newest apprentice. A wall is either
plumb or it is not, and the plumb-line has no view about your social position.

**In a room containing a duke and an apothecary — which eighteenth-century lodges regularly
did — that is not a platitude. It is a proposition about authority.**

It is also, and this is the elegant part, a proposition that requires no shared theology to
state. A Catholic, a Dissenter, a Jew and a deist can all agree that a right angle is a
right angle. The Craft found an ethical vocabulary that survives the removal of doctrine,
which is exactly the problem Anderson's formula created and exactly what was needed to solve
it.

---

### Why the compasses are the harder one

The square's moral transfer is nearly automatic. The compasses' is not, and the Masonic
interpretation of them is more interesting than it looks.

The instruction, across the sources, is that the compasses teach the candidate to
circumscribe his desires and keep his passions within due bounds.[^1]

Note what that does *not* say. Compasses are for drawing circles and transferring
measurements — for *making* and *comparing*. The Masonic reading ignores both and selects a
third property: that a pair of compasses, once set, defines an extent beyond which the point
will not travel.

The moral content is therefore **limitation**, not measurement. And it is limitation of a
particular kind: not externally imposed, but set by the operator and then respected by him.
A man sets his own compasses and then works within the radius he has chosen.

Which is, once again, the doubling from the last chapter. He is the hand that sets the
instrument and the point that must stay inside the arc.

### The relative position

A small technical detail that illustrates how differently symbols work from inside.

In many jurisdictions the arrangement of the compasses relative to the square on the open
volume of sacred law differs by degree — both points concealed, one revealed, both revealed
— so that the arrangement on the pedestal tells anyone who knows the system which degree the
lodge is working in.[^2]

This is not a cipher. Nothing is encrypted. The information is entirely public and entirely
contextual: a member reads it instantly and a visitor sees a decorative arrangement.

I mention it because it is a useful model for how the whole Masonic symbolic system operates,
and how it does *not*. Nothing is encoded. Things are simply unremarked — and the difference
between unremarked and encoded is the difference between a tradition and a puzzle.

A great deal of the literature on this subject fails to make that distinction, and Chapter 38
is where the failure gets examined.

---

### What the emblem tells a historian

Three things, and they are all negative in a useful way.

**Freemasonry's self-image is a craft, not a cult.** The badge is two tools. Not a deity, not
a sacred letter, not a cosmic diagram — the contents of a workman's bag.

**Its ethics are verificationist.** The instruments test. The moral system carried by them
is one of conformity to an external standard indifferent to the person being tested.

**And it was content to be legible.** An organisation seriously concerned with concealment
does not put its emblem on the front of its buildings, its members' gravestones and its
letterhead.

That third point is worth holding onto for Part IX. The institution accused of hiding
everything has, for two centuries, advertised its premises with a sign.
