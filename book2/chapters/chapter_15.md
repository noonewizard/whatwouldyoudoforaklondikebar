# CHAPTER FIFTEEN
## The Level, the Plumb, and the Pillars

Three more objects, and then we leave the workshop.

Two of them are instruments and the third is a building. All three do something the square
and compasses do not, and the difference is worth the chapter.

---

### The level

A level tells you whether a surface is horizontal. That is its entire function.

The Masonic interpretation is equality — that we meet on the level, that the level reminds
us we are descended from the same stock and are travelling to the same destination, and that
distinctions of rank are laid aside within the lodge.[^1]

The word did real work in the eighteenth century. English lodges contained men who would not
otherwise have sat down together: a peer, a physician, a wine merchant, a shopkeeper, an army
officer. Outside the room the gradations were minute and enforced continuously. Inside, the
formula said something different.

I want to be careful here in both directions, because this is the point at which Masonic
self-description is most flattering and therefore least reliable.

**The claim is overstated in the literature.** Lodges were not egalitarian in any modern
sense. Grand Lodge cultivated aristocrats deliberately and advertised them. Officers were
frequently the socially senior men. The "level" operated inside a hierarchy of degrees and
offices which the Craft took extremely seriously, and a system that ranks its members in
three degrees and a dozen offices is not a system without rank.

**And the claim is real.** Within its limits — and the limits were substantial — the lodge
was a room in which a man addressed another man as *brother* who would have addressed him as
*sir* in the street. There is no equivalent institution in eighteenth-century England, and
the men involved noticed.

The exclusions matter more than the internal gradations and should be stated rather than
softened. Women were excluded by the 1723 *Constitutions*, and remained so in mainstream
Masonry, although they were being initiated in Masonic-form ritual in France from 1744.
Anderson's requirement that a candidate be "free-born" was a relic of guild qualification in
England and became, transplanted to a slave society, an instrument for excluding formerly
enslaved men. African Lodge No. 459 in Boston was warranted from England in 1784 and American
Masonic bodies declined to recognise Prince Hall Masonry for the better part of two
centuries, on jurisdictional grounds that were applied to no other case with the same
rigour.[^2]

So: a genuine and unusual claim about equality, administered by people with a much narrower
view of who counted than the claim implies.

That is not hypocrisy exactly. It is a universalist formula stated in advance of anybody's
willingness to honour it — and the people it excluded picked it up and held the institution
to it, which is a substantial part of why the language mattered.

---

### The plumb

A plumb-rule tells you whether a line is vertical. It works by gravity, which means it
consults nothing but the earth.

The Masonic interpretation is uprightness of conduct, and the pun is the same in Latin,
Greek and every European language I have checked, which is why nobody had to invent it.

What interests me is a property the plumb has that the square and the level do not.

**A square requires two things to compare.** It tests an angle: this face against that face.
**A level requires a surface.** But a plumb-line requires nothing at all. Hang it, and it
hangs. It is the only one of the three instruments that produces a standard from itself,
without reference to the work.

That is a striking thing for a tradition to place in a man's hands and tell him to apply to
his own character. The square says: *are you true to the thing next to you?* The level says:
*are you where you should be relative to the others?* The plumb says: *never mind any of
that; are you aligned with the one direction that is not a matter of anyone's opinion?*

I do not claim that any eighteenth-century Mason thought of it this way. The sources give
the simple moral transfer and nothing more. But the instruments were selected from a trade
that had many tools, and these three were the ones that stayed, and they happen to constitute
a rather complete set: relation, position, and absolute alignment.

---

### The pillars

And now the building, because the two pillars at the entrance of the lodge are the point at
which Part III's argument and Part IV's meet.

The provenance is the least disputed thing in Masonic symbolism. 1 Kings 7:21: Hiram of Tyre
cast two bronze pillars and set them up at the porch of the Temple, and he called the right
one Jachin and the left one Boaz. The chapter gives their height, their circumference, their
capitals, their lily-work and pomegranates and chain-work. It is one of the most physically
detailed passages in the Hebrew Bible and it is where the Masonic pillars come from,
directly and without intermediary.[^3]

Two features have been added since and both can be dated.

**The globes.** In most modern workings the pillars are surmounted by spheres, one celestial
and one terrestrial. These are early modern scientific instruments — the sort of object that
appears in portraits of natural philosophers — and they are an eighteenth-century addition.
A pillar of the tenth century BCE did not have a globe on it.

Nobody minded. That is the point worth noticing: the Craft was never attempting historical
reconstruction, and its symbolism absorbed an anachronism without embarrassment because the
globes said something the tradition wanted said — that the Craft's concern is universal.

**The Kabbalistic reading.** The identification of the two pillars with Severity and Mercy,
the outer columns of the Sephirotic Tree, with a middle pillar of equilibrium between them,
is nineteenth century. It comes through Lévi and reaches Masonry substantially via Pike. It
is absent from every eighteenth-century Masonic source. It is now so widespread that many
Masons meet it before they meet 1 Kings.[^4]

---

### What a pair does

The structural observation, which applies whether or not anyone intended it.

A single pillar is a support. **Two pillars with a gap between them are a door.**

That is not a metaphor; it is the architecture. The pillars of the Temple stood at the porch,
which is to say at the threshold, and what they framed was the act of going in.

And the Craft's use of them follows the architecture exactly. The pillars are at the entrance
of the lodge. The candidate passes between them. The second degree, in most workings, makes
that passage explicit and attaches an ascent to it — a winding staircase, a progress, a
movement from outside to inside by way of a gap between two uprights.

So the pillars are not primarily about duality, or polarity, or the two poles of a
metaphysical system, whatever the nineteenth century made of them. They are about
**passage**, and they are the architectural form of the same event that Chapter 4 described
as the knock at the door.

The Craft put its threshold twice: once as an act, and once as a pair of objects standing
either side of the place where the act happens.

---

### The instruments taken together

Part III has been about a set of objects from a workman's bag, and it is worth saying what
the set amounts to before we leave it.

A candidate is shown two stones and told he is one of them. He is handed instruments whose
entire function is to test, and told to apply them to himself. He is told that the standard
is external and does not consult him. He is shown a pair of pillars and walked between them.
He is told that the building is unfinished and that the work is his.

There is no doctrine in any of it. There is no metaphysics, no cosmology, no god beyond the
minimum formula of 1723, and nothing that a Catholic, a Dissenter, a Jew or a deist would
have to refuse.

And it constitutes a reasonably complete moral position: that you are unfinished, that the
work is yours, that there are standards which do not care who you are, that you are being
prepared to fit with others, and that it does not end.

**Whether anybody was changed by being told this is a different question and it is the
question Part XI exists for.** What can be said at the end of Part III is that the Craft
managed to say all of it without a single proposition anyone in the room could dispute — and
did it with six objects you could buy from an ironmonger.

That was not an accident of poverty. It was the only way to say anything at all to a room
assembled on the terms Anderson had set.

Which leaves the question of why the trade the Craft borrowed from was *this* trade. There
were many crafts in eighteenth-century Europe with tools and secrets and apprenticeships.
None of the others produced a philosophy.

Masonry did, and the reason is that masonry alone had a liberal science attached to it.
