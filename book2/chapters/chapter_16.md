# PART IV — GEOMETRY AS LANGUAGE

# CHAPTER SIXTEEN
## Why Geometry?

There were many trades in eighteenth-century Europe with tools, secrets, apprenticeships and
oaths.

The joiners had them. The smiths had them. The weavers, the coopers, the cordwainers, the
goldsmiths, the hatters — all of them had a craft to protect, a period of indenture, customs
of admission, and in many cases ceremonies with more elaborate horseplay than anything in a
Masonic lodge.

None of them produced a philosophy. None of them was adopted by dukes. None of them became
the organisational template for the entire Western esoteric tradition.

The masons did. And the previous volume's answer to why — that masonry was the one trade
with a liberal science attached to it — is correct as far as it goes and stops one step too
soon.

This chapter takes the step. Not *why geometry was available* to the Craft, but **what
geometry was able to do that no other body of knowledge could.**

---

### The inherited answer, briefly

The medieval curriculum divided knowledge into seven liberal sciences: grammar, rhetoric and
logic; then arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy. These were the studies proper to a
free man, as distinct from the mechanical arts of those who worked with their hands.

Masonry sat on the boundary and was the only trade that did. A mason worked with his hands
and was therefore a mechanic. But what he did was *apply geometry*: set out a right angle,
strike an arc, calculate a thrust, raise a vault. The Old Charges, from about 1390, make the
identification explicitly and repeatedly — masonry is geometry, the craft is the science.[^1]

A weaver has no liberal art. A mason has one, and his own founding documents told him so, and
they were read aloud at his admission.

That is the historical explanation and it accounts for the raw material. It does not account
for what was built with it.

---

### What geometry is, as a form of language

Here is the property that matters, and it is not about masonry at all.

**Geometry is the only body of knowledge in the pre-modern European inheritance that
*demonstrates* rather than *asserts*.**

Consider the alternatives available to an educated man in 1720 who wanted to say something
about truth and how it is known.

*Scripture* asserts, and its authority is revelation. You accept it or you do not, and the
century had recently finished killing large numbers of people over which parts to accept.

*Philosophy* argues, and arguments can be answered. Every position had a rival with an equally
long pedigree.

*Natural history* observes, and observation in 1720 was a matter of testimony — did you
believe the traveller who reported the animal?

*Geometry does none of these.* It demonstrates. A proof is not an assertion backed by
authority, not an argument that might be answered, and not a report you must take on trust.
It is a sequence of steps that anyone competent can walk, arriving at a conclusion nobody can
refuse.

And it produces certainty **without requiring anybody to agree about anything else**. A
Catholic and a Calvinist can dispute every proposition in theology and will construct the
same perpendicular.

That is an extraordinary property and it is precisely the property the Craft needed.

---

### The problem Anderson created

Recall what happened in 1723. The *Constitutions* removed doctrinal requirement from Masonic
membership: a Mason must obey the moral law and must not be an atheist or an irreligious
libertine, but as to the particulars, he is to hold "that Religion in which all Men agree,
leaving their particular Opinions to themselves."[^2]

That solved a membership problem and created a content problem.

An institution that will not specify what its members believe has, by that act, disqualified
itself from teaching almost everything. It cannot teach theology. It cannot teach ethics
grounded in theology, which in that century was most ethics. It cannot appeal to any
authority that some members reject.

What is left?

**Only the things that are true regardless of who is asking.** Which, in 1723, meant
geometry and what could be built on it.

So the Craft's adoption of geometry as its sacred science is not a mystical preference. It is
the logically forced consequence of Anderson's religious settlement. Having ruled out
everything men disagree about, the institution was left with the one discipline where
agreement is compelled rather than negotiated — and it made that discipline the centre of its
symbolism, its moral vocabulary and its self-description.

---

### Newton in the room

The dating supports this, and it is worth making the point because the popular picture runs
the other way.

The circle that produced the 1723 *Constitutions* was thick with Fellows of the Royal
Society. John Theophilus Desaguliers, Grand Master in 1719, was a Huguenot refugee, an
experimental philosopher, and the man who demonstrated Newton's mechanics to paying audiences
in London. The historian Ric Berman has traced the network in detail, and it is a Newtonian
and Whig network before it is anything else.[^3]

Newton had shown, within living memory, that the heavens obey mathematics — that the universe
is, literally, a geometrical object with calculable behaviour.

In that world a fraternity organised around geometry was not archaic. It was the most modern
thing available.

This matters because Masonry is habitually read backwards through its nineteenth-century
occult interpreters, who found in the square and compasses a cosmic symbolism of enormous
antiquity. The men of the 1720s were not looking backwards to Egypt. They were looking at the
*Principia*. When they called God the Great Architect of the Universe, the most natural
reading is not that they had recovered a Hermetic doctrine. It is that they had read Newton
and thought the universe looked designed.

---

### The objection, and why it does not hold

The Newtonian reading has an obvious counter and it should be met.

**The objection.** Geometry was not invented in 1687. The Old Charges call Masonry a
geometrical science, and the *Regius* manuscript of the 1390s already makes the
identification. Geometry is therefore the oldest continuous element in the whole tradition,
predating Newton by three centuries, and explaining it by the *Principia* is anachronism of
exactly the kind this book keeps warning against.

**The objection is correct about the fact and wrong about what follows.** Geometry is indeed
the oldest element, and the medieval identification of Masonry with geometry requires no
Newtonian explanation at all: it is the identification a guild of stonemasons would make
because geometry is what stonemasons use. Setting out a foundation, cutting a voussoir,
raising a vault — these are geometrical operations performed by hand, and a craft that does
them daily will call itself a geometrical science without any philosophical prompting.

What changes in the 1720s is not the presence of geometry but **what geometry is taken to
be evidence of.** For the operative craft it was a technique. For Anderson's circle it had
become the language in which the universe was written, and the difference is visible in what
they do with it: the claim that geometry underlies not merely building but all the arts and
sciences, and the elevation of a proposition of Euclid to the foundation of the whole
institution, are not craft statements. They are philosophical ones, and they are made by men
who had watched Desaguliers demonstrate the mechanics of the solar system.

So the two readings are not competitors. **The word was inherited from the operative craft;
its cosmic significance was supplied by the eighteenth century**, and the seam between them
is visible in the 1723 text itself, where a traditional history of the craft sits beside a
claim about the nature of things that no fourteenth-century mason would have made.

This is, incidentally, a model for how a good deal of Masonic material should be read.
Continuity of vocabulary is not continuity of meaning, and the fact that a word survives
from the *Regius* manuscript tells us nothing about what the men using it in 1723 took it to
mean.

---

### Geometry as a moral instrument

And then the transfer, which is the part that is genuinely clever.

The Craft did not teach geometry. There is no evidence that eighteenth-century lodges taught
anybody to construct a perpendicular, and the members did not need it.

What the Craft did was take geometry's *epistemic character* — demonstration, not assertion;
standards that do not consult the observer — and apply it to conduct.

That is the argument of Chapter 14 restated at the level of the whole system. The working
tools are instruments of verification. To act on the square is to conform to a standard that
is external and indifferent. The moral system is *verificationist in form* even though
nothing in it can actually be verified.

Which is either a profound insight or a sleight of hand, and I have genuinely gone back and
forth.

The case for sleight of hand: geometry's certainty comes from its subject matter, and moral
propositions do not have that subject matter. Saying that uprightness is like a plumb-line
borrows the plumb-line's authority without earning it. There is no procedure for testing
whether a man is square, and the analogy conceals the absence of one.

The case for insight: the analogy is not claiming a procedure. It is claiming a *structure* —
that moral standards, like geometrical ones, are not constituted by anyone's preference, and
that the appropriate posture toward them is submission rather than negotiation. That is a
real philosophical position, held by a great many serious people, and expressing it in a
tool a man can hold is a considerable achievement in communication.

I incline to the second, with the first as a standing caution. The Craft's moral vocabulary
is powerful precisely because it feels like measurement, and feeling like measurement is not
the same as being it.

---

### The language without a vocabulary

One last observation, because it sets up the next three chapters.

Geometry as the Craft uses it has almost no content. There are no theorems in Masonic ritual.
There is no construction anybody is taught. The forty-seventh proposition of Euclid is named
— Chapter 18 is about that — but it is named, not proved.

What there is instead is a *vocabulary of shapes*: the point, the line, the circle, the
triangle, the square, the perpendicular. Each is used to mean something, and none is worked
with.

That is worth stating plainly because it is the difference between Freemasonry and the
sacred-geometry literature that has grown up around it. A tradition that actually did
geometry would leave constructions, proofs, ratios and problems. Freemasonry left a set of
figures used as emblems.

Which is not a criticism. Emblems are what the institution needed, and a language of shapes
has a property that a language of words does not: **it crosses every linguistic boundary
without translation.**

A French Mason and a Bengali Mason could not read each other's rituals. They could both look
at a square.
