# CHAPTER SEVENTEEN
## Point, Line, Circle

A point within a circle, with a parallel line on either side, and sometimes a book resting
on the top.

It appears on Masonic tracing boards, in the monitorial literature, and in the explanations
given in lodge, and the interpretations offered for it are so various, so mutually
incompatible, and so obviously improvised that it has become the standard example of a
Masonic symbol nobody can account for.[^1]

The point is the individual brother. Or it is the sun, with the lines as the solstices. Or
it is God. Or the lines are St John the Baptist and St John the Evangelist, whose feast days
fall near the solstices, which is why Masonic lodges met on them. Or the whole figure is a
diagram for finding the meridian, which a mason genuinely would need. Or it is a phallic
emblem of generation, which is a nineteenth-century suggestion made by people who made that
suggestion about a great many things.

I raise it first because it is the honest state of the evidence for Masonic geometrical
symbolism, and this chapter is about what can be said when a symbol has too many meanings
rather than too few.

---

### The minimum vocabulary

Strip Masonic geometry to its elements and there are four: the point, the line, the circle
and the square, with the triangle appearing in the higher degrees and in certain furnishings.

These are not a system. They are the smallest set of figures from which anything can be
constructed, which is why they are the same four that every geometrical tradition in every
culture has used, and why their appearance in two traditions tells you nothing whatever.

**The point.** Position without magnitude, in Euclid's definition. The origin of everything
constructible and itself unconstructible. In Masonic usage: the individual, the center, the
place from which the work proceeds.

**The line.** Extension in one dimension. In Masonic usage: direction, and the connection of
two points.

**The circle.** Everything equidistant from a center. In Masonic usage: boundary, and the
extent within which a man should keep himself.

**The square.** The figure that closes, has four equal angles, and stands.

The instructive thing about that list is how *little* the Craft does with it. These are not
developed. Nothing is derived from anything. There is no construction, no proof, no
relationship demonstrated between one figure and another.

Compare what an actual geometrical tradition looks like — Euclid, or the Islamic
geometrical corpus, or the design practice of medieval masons' lodges, which really did use
constructive procedures to set out buildings — and the difference is total. Those are bodies
of technique. Masonic geometry is a set of nouns.

---

### The point within the circle, examined

Apply the method of Chapter 5 to the figure, because doing so demonstrates something general.

**Provenance.** The figure appears in Masonic sources of the eighteenth and nineteenth
centuries. Its earliest datable Masonic appearance requires work on tracing boards and
monitors that I have not been able to complete, and I am not going to assert a date I cannot
support.

**The competing explanations.** Here is the diagnostic. When a symbol has one documented
meaning, that meaning is probably its meaning. When it has seven, offered by different
authors in different centuries with no agreement between them, the most likely explanation is
that **the figure arrived without an interpretation and the interpretations were supplied
afterward, independently, by people who felt something ought to be said.**

That is not a cynical reading. It is what Chapter 14 described as the ordinary process by
which an undefined symbol in a non-doctrinal institution accumulates sediment. The point
within a circle is the same process caught early, before one reading won.

**The one explanation with a practical basis** deserves noting, because it is the only one
that does not require a symbolic intention. A point, a circle and a pair of tangent verticals
is, in fact, the classic construction for determining a true north–south line by observing
the sun's shadow — you mark where the shadow falls at equal times before and after noon and
bisect. An operative mason setting out a building oriented east–west needed exactly this.

Whether the Masonic figure descends from that practice or merely resembles it is not
established, and I will not pretend otherwise. But it is the only reading with an independent
reason to exist, and in this subject that counts for something.

---

### What a shape can carry

The general question behind this chapter: what *kind* of meaning can a geometrical figure
hold?

Three kinds, and they are worth separating because the literature conflates them
constantly.

**Iconic meaning.** The figure resembles the thing. A circle resembles the sun, an enclosure,
a wheel, a cycle. This is the cheapest kind and the most promiscuous — resemblance is
everywhere and constrains nothing.

**Structural meaning.** The figure has a property, and the property is the meaning. A circle
*has* a boundary and a center, and everything on it is equidistant from that center. If you
use a circle to say something about inclusion or about a standard applying equally, you are
using the structure rather than the appearance, and the meaning is constrained by the
geometry rather than by free association.

**Conventional meaning.** The figure means what a community has agreed it means, and the
agreement has no basis in the figure at all.

Masonic geometrical symbolism operates mostly in the second register, which is why it is
better than it looks. The square means rectitude because a square *tests* for rectitude, not
because it resembles it. The compasses mean limitation because compasses *define* a limit.
The level means equality because it *finds* a horizontal.

**Each symbol's meaning is anchored to what the instrument does.** That is a genuine
constraint, and it is why Craft symbolism has remained comparatively stable while the
speculative readings piled up around it.

And it is exactly what the point within the circle lacks. A point in a circle does not *do*
anything. There is no operation, no test, no function to anchor the meaning — and in the
absence of an anchor, the interpretations multiplied without limit.

Seven readings and no consensus is what happens when structural meaning is unavailable and
only iconic and conventional meaning remain.

---

### The discipline this suggests

A rule falls out of this that I think applies beyond Freemasonry, and I offer it as a working
instrument rather than a finding.

**When a symbol is an instrument, ask what it does. When a symbol is a shape, be careful.**

Instruments constrain their own interpretation. Shapes do not, and a shape can be made to
mean nearly anything by an interpreter of moderate ingenuity — which is the mechanism behind
the entire sacred-geometry literature, and behind a great deal of what Chapter 38 will
examine.

The Craft's own symbolic vocabulary is weighted heavily toward instruments: square, level,
plumb, gauge, gavel, trowel, compasses. The shapes that appear alongside them — the point in
a circle, the blazing star, the triangle — are precisely the ones whose Masonic meanings are
unstable, contested, multiple and late.

That correlation is not a coincidence and I think it is the most useful finding in this
chapter.

---

### The exception

There is one geometrical figure in Freemasonry that is neither an instrument nor a free
shape, and it is the exception that gets its own chapter.

It has a name. It has a documented Masonic appearance in a specific printed book in a
specific year. It is a *theorem* rather than an emblem — a thing with a proof, which can be
right or wrong.

And it was put at the center of Masonic self-description by the man who wrote the
*Constitutions*, in 1723, in a sentence that has been quoted, half-quoted, misquoted and
built upon for three hundred years.
