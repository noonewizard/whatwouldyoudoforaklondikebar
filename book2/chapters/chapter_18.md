# CHAPTER EIGHTEEN
## The 47th Proposition

In an English lodge, the man who has served as Master and stood down wears a jewel, and on
that jewel there is a diagram.

A right-angled triangle, with a square constructed on each of its three sides. The squares on
the two shorter sides, taken together, have the same area as the square on the longest. It
is the theorem every schoolchild learns as Pythagoras's, and it is the forty-seventh
proposition of the first book of Euclid's *Elements*.[^1]

It is the only theorem in Freemasonry.

Everything else in the Craft's symbolic vocabulary is an emblem, an instrument or a shape.
This is a *proposition*: a claim that can be proved, that is either true or false, and that
has been demonstrated in more than three hundred different ways over two and a half
millennia.

And it has been at the center of Masonic self-description since 1723, which makes it both
the best-documented symbol in this book and the one most thoroughly encrusted with claims it
cannot support.

---

### What Anderson wrote

The *Constitutions of the Free-Masons*, published in London in 1723 and compiled by the
Presbyterian minister James Anderson, contains in its long historical section a passage
naming Pythagoras and the forty-seventh proposition, and describing that proposition — in
the formulation universally quoted in Masonic literature — as the foundation of all Masonry,
sacred, civil and military.[^2]

I have to enter a caution here that is more than a formality.

**I have not been able to verify the exact wording, the page, or the precise form of the
passage against the 1723 edition.** The sentence circulates in Masonic writing in several
variants, some of which have plainly been smoothed in transmission, and it is frequently
accompanied by the further claim that the diagram appears on the frontispiece or title page
of the 1723 volume — a claim I have also been unable to verify and which requires a
facsimile to settle.

A book that spends a chapter teaching readers to check things is obliged to say when it has
not been able to check something. This is one of those places. The substance — that Anderson
named Pythagoras and the proposition, and attached great importance to it — is not in
dispute. The wording and the frontispiece are, until somebody looks at the book.

What is not in doubt is the consequence. From 1723 onward, the forty-seventh proposition had
a Masonic charter, in print, in the institution's founding document, under the authority of
its own compiler. Very few Masonic symbols can say as much. Most of the Craft's emblems
appear without explanation in ritual texts of uncertain date; this one was announced.

---

### What the theorem actually is

Worth stating, because the Masonic literature frequently gets it slightly wrong in ways that
matter for what follows.

Euclid I.47 states that in a right-angled triangle, the square on the side subtending the
right angle equals the sum of the squares on the sides containing it. Euclid proves it
geometrically, by areas, in the celebrated construction sometimes called the windmill or the
bride's chair.

**The theorem is about areas, not about the numbers 3, 4 and 5.**

That distinction is the hinge of this entire chapter. The 3-4-5 triangle is one *instance* of
the theorem — the smallest whole-number case, and consequently the one that is useful if you
want to construct a right angle in a field using a knotted cord. There are infinitely many
others: 5-12-13, 8-15-17, and so on. Euclid's proposition is the general statement; the
3-4-5 is a particular that happens to be convenient.

Almost everything claimed about the Masonic 47th Proposition is actually a claim about the
3-4-5, and the substitution is usually made without comment.

---

### The Pythagoras problem

Then there is the attribution.

The theorem was known, as a relation between particular numbers, in Babylonian mathematics
more than a thousand years before Pythagoras. The tablet known as Plimpton 322 records
Pythagorean triples. Whether Pythagoras proved the general theorem, transmitted it, or merely
had it attributed to him by a later tradition that attributed a great deal to him, is not
recoverable — the Pythagorean sources are late, legendary, and written by people with an
interest in the legend.

The story that Pythagoras, on discovering it, sacrificed an ox or a hecatomb is reported by
late authors and is generally treated as legend, not least because Pythagoreans are supposed
to have been vegetarian.[^3]

None of which affected Anderson, and none of it should be treated as an error on his part.
In 1723 the attribution to Pythagoras was simply what an educated man believed, and what the
standard histories of mathematics said.

---

### And then Egypt

Here is where the chapter has to do its work, because there is a large, persistent and
partly correct body of claims attaching the Masonic 47th Proposition to ancient Egypt.

**The claim, in its usual form.** The Egyptians knew the 3-4-5 triangle and used knotted
ropes to lay out right angles for building and for resurveying after the Nile floods; their
surveyors were called *harpedonaptai*, rope-stretchers; and the triangle carried a religious
meaning, with the three sides representing Osiris, Isis and Horus. Freemasonry, being
descended from Egyptian building practice, inherited it.

Take the elements one at a time.

**The Osiris–Isis–Horus reading is genuine — and it is Plutarch's, not Egypt's.** In *On Isis
and Osiris*, Plutarch writes that the Egyptians likened the nature of the universe to the
most beautiful triangle, and assigns the upright of three to Osiris, the base of four to
Isis, and the hypotenuse of five to their offspring Horus.[^4]

That passage is real and it is worth reading. But note what it is: a Greek Platonist of the
late first and early second century CE, writing in Greek, telling a Greek-speaking audience
what he understands the Egyptians to have thought. It is evidence for Plutarch's Egypt —
which is a philosophical construction of the Roman imperial period, the same milieu that
produced the *Corpus Hermeticum* — and it is not evidence for pharaonic doctrine.

**The rope-stretchers are attested; the 3-4-5 is not.** Egyptian surveyors using cords are
documented. That they used a 3-4-5 knotted rope specifically to construct right angles is a
modern reconstruction, widely repeated, for which the Egyptological evidence is considerably
weaker than its popularity suggests. It may be true. It is not established, and historians of
Egyptian mathematics have been notably more cautious about it than the popular literature
is.[^5]

**And the descent claim fails on chronology and transmission, as it always does.** The
previous volume disposed of Masonic Egyptian origins comprehensively, and the route runs the
other way: European Egyptomania, revived by the *Pimander* in 1471 and massively amplified
after Napoleon's expedition of 1798, supplied nineteenth-century Masonic writers with an
Egypt to discover in their own symbols.

**Verdict.** The Masonic 47th Proposition is documented from 1723 as a *geometrical and
Pythagorean* emblem. The Egyptian and Isis–Osiris reading is a nineteenth-century graft onto
an eighteenth-century Masonic symbol, using a genuine classical text about a construction of
Roman-period Platonism.

Every piece of it is real. The assembly is not.

---

### What it does

Having said what it does not mean, the more interesting question: why did Anderson reach for
a theorem at all, and why has it stuck?

I think there are two reasons and they belong to Chapter 16's argument.

**It is the Craft's claim to demonstrate.** Everything else in Masonic symbolism asserts.
Here is one thing that can be *shown*, to anyone, with a certainty that requires no authority
and admits no dissent. An institution whose whole intellectual position is that there are
standards which do not consult you could hardly find a better emblem.

**And it is the point at which the trade and the science coincide exactly.** The theorem is
not an analogy for masonry; it is a working mason's tool. A right angle laid out with a
knotted cord is Euclid I.47 in use, on a building site, by men who could not read Euclid. The
proposition is the precise place where the mechanic art and the liberal science turn out to
be the same thing.

That is what the Old Charges had been claiming since 1390 — that masonry *is* geometry — and
the 47th Proposition is the theorem that makes the claim literally true rather than merely
flattering.

---

### The one Masonic symbol that can be wrong

A closing observation, and it is the reason this chapter sits where it does.

Every other emblem in this book is safe from refutation. You cannot disprove that the square
signifies rectitude; the most you can say is that a given reading is late, or unsupported, or
invented — which is what this book spends its length saying.

**The forty-seventh proposition is different. It is a claim about the world, and it is
either true or it is not.**

It happens to be true. It was true before Euclid wrote it down, it was true in Babylon, it
will be true after every lodge in the world has closed. Nothing about it depends on
tradition, transmission, authority or belief.

And in a symbolic system otherwise composed entirely of things that mean whatever their
holders take them to mean, the Craft placed at its center — and on the jewel of the man who
has completed his term in the chair — the one object in the building that is not a matter of
interpretation at all.

I do not think Anderson planned that. But of all the accidents in this history it is the one
I find hardest to stop thinking about.
