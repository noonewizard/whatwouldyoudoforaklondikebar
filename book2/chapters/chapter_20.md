# PART V — THE LANGUAGE OF LIGHT

# CHAPTER TWENTY
## Fiat Lux

*What do you most desire?*

*Light.*

The exchange is in the eighteenth-century sources and it is the pivot of the entire
ceremony. It is asked of a man who has been blindfolded for the better part of an hour, and
the answer is supplied to him, and then it is given.[^1]

Everything before that moment has been arranged so that it will land. Everything after it
proceeds from what he sees.

---

### The oldest metaphor there is

Light for knowledge is so deeply embedded in every language I know of that it is nearly
impossible to speak about understanding without using it. We say that a matter is clear,
that an argument is lucid, that a person is brilliant, that a problem has been illuminated,
that we see what someone means. The Enlightenment named itself after it. *Fiat lux* is the
fourth verse of Genesis; Plato's cave turns on it; the Johannine prologue has the light
shining in the darkness.

An eighteenth-century candidate did not need this explained. He arrived with the whole
structure installed by every sermon he had ever heard.

Which is exactly why the Craft could use it without a word of theology. The lodge does not
have to tell a man what light signifies. It only has to *take it away and give it back*,
and the meaning assembles itself from material he already possesses.

That is the economy of the whole system in one gesture. Nothing is taught. Conditions are
arranged in which the candidate teaches himself something he already knew, in a form he will
not forget.

---

### The difference restoration makes

Here is the mechanism, and it is worth stating precisely because it is the clearest example
in this book of a ritual converting a condition into an event.

In ordinary experience light is not an event. It is the medium within which events occur —
continuous, unremarked, the background against which we notice things. Nobody experiences
being able to see.

The hoodwink changes the category. For forty minutes, sight becomes something the candidate
does not have. It becomes an object of desire, and then — at a moment chosen by someone else
— an occurrence with a time and a place.

And the ritual places that occurrence at the instant it has something to show him.

I want to draw out what this accomplishes, because it applies far beyond Freemasonry. **You
cannot make a person notice something that is always available.** Attention does not work
that way; the permanently present becomes invisible. The only reliable method of making an
ordinary thing vivid is to remove it and return it.

Every tradition that fasts has discovered this. So has everyone who has been ill and then
well.

The Masonic ceremony applies the principle to vision, deliberately, for an hour, and then
uses the recovered faculty on a book and two tools.

---

### What he is shown

And this is where the design becomes interesting, because the content is so slight.

He opens his eyes on a room lit by candles, with men standing round in aprons, and in front
of him an open volume of sacred law with a square and a pair of compasses laid upon it. He
is told these are the Three Great Lights, and given a brief interpretation of each: the book
to govern his faith, the square to regulate his actions, the compasses to keep him within
bounds.[^2]

That is it. That is what the darkness was for.

A reader arriving from the conspiracy literature expects something else here. There is
nothing else. No revelation of a doctrine, no disclosure of a hidden history, no name of
power. A book and two instruments from a workman's bag, and a piece of moral advice that any
preacher would give for free.

**And the slightness is the point.**

An extravagant revelation invites evaluation. Tell a man he has been brought through
darkness to receive a great secret, then give him a cosmology, and he will assess it — is it
true? do I believe this? — and some proportion of any room will conclude that they do not.

Give him a square instead, and there is nothing to assess. He cannot disagree with a square.
What he can do is *supply* the significance himself, out of the conditions he has just been
through, and what a person constructs for himself is held far more firmly than what he is
handed.

The ceremony spends an hour manufacturing receptivity and then declines to fill it. The
candidate fills it.

I think that is the single cleverest thing in Freemasonry, and I do not believe anyone
planned it.

---

### Lesser lights and the order of illumination

The Craft's light symbolism is graded, which is worth noting because it is a structural
feature rather than an ornament.

There are the Three Great Lights — the volume, the square, the compasses. There are the
lesser lights, which in most workings are the sun, the moon and the Master of the lodge, said
to rule the day, the night and the lodge respectively. There is the light restored at
initiation, and there is further light promised in subsequent degrees: the second degree and
the third are both described as advances in light.[^3]

So illumination is not a single event but a sequence, and the sequence never terminates.
There is always more light available in the next degree, and the degrees eventually run out
without the process being described as complete.

This is the same structure as the unfinished Temple in Chapter 12, and the same structure as
the lost word in Chapter 24. **The Craft repeatedly builds systems that promise completion
and are designed not to deliver it** — and I am increasingly convinced that this is the
system's central mechanism rather than an accident of its assembly.

A tradition that delivered completion would produce completed members, who would have no
further reason to attend.

---

### The claim I am not making

A caution, because this chapter is where a certain kind of writing usually goes wrong.

I have described the restoration of light as a moment of engineered receptivity. I have not
said, and will not say, that it produces an altered state, a mystical experience, or an
encounter with anything.

Chapter 3 set out the evidence on this and it is worth restating here in its proper place.
The conditions the Masonic ceremony imposes are, in other traditions, associated with
dissociation and visionary experience — and **no Masonic source describes anything of the
kind.** Not the exposures, not Preston, not the sermons, not the nineteenth-century
interpreters who had every incentive to claim profundity.

The testimony is uniformly sober. Men record being struck, impressed, moved by the
solemnity. They do not record leaving themselves.

Which means that whatever the restoration of light does, it does it *within* ordinary
consciousness, to a man who is fully present and will be having supper in an hour. That is a
less dramatic claim than the literature usually makes and it is the one the evidence
supports.

---

### The word for the whole thing

One last thing about this moment, and it is a point about vocabulary that will matter in Part
VII.

Masonic sources describe the initiate as having been "brought to light." The formula is
ancient in form, and it is the same formula that the ancient mystery traditions used of
their initiates, and the same one that the nineteenth-century occultists would use of
theirs.

The previous volume established that there is no descent between these traditions. What this
chapter adds is an explanation of why they converged on the same word.

**They converged because they were all doing the same physical thing.** Restricting vision
and then restoring it is not a doctrine that has to be transmitted. It is an available
technique, obvious to anyone who has ever noticed that a lamp brought into a dark room is
more striking than a lamp in a lit one, and any tradition seriously interested in making a
moment memorable will find it.

The word is shared because the method is rediscoverable. That is the argument of this entire
book in miniature, and Part V is where the symbols make it most plainly — because the next
two chapters are about symbols that were shared, that look ancient, and that turn out on
inspection to be nothing of the kind.
