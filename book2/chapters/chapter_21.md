# CHAPTER TWENTY-ONE
## The Blazing Star, the Sun, and the Moon

On a first-degree tracing board there is a star.

It is usually five-pointed, usually in the centre or above, usually radiant. It is called the
Blazing Star, and it is among the most prominent images in Masonic visual culture.

The Craft's own sources cannot agree on what it means.

It has been explained as Providence; as the sun; as the star that guided the Magi; as the
glory at the centre; as prudence; as the Deity. Different eighteenth- and nineteenth-century
authorities give different answers, and several give more than one.[^1]

That inconsistency is not a defect in the record. It is the evidence, and this chapter is
about how to read it.

---

### Internal disagreement as a dating tool

Here is a principle that has been implicit for several chapters and should now be stated.

**When a tradition's own sources disagree about what one of its symbols means, the most
economical explanation is that the symbol arrived without a settled meaning.**

Consider the alternative. If the Blazing Star had entered Masonic use carrying a specific
signification — inherited, taught, transmitted — we would expect the sources to agree, or at
least to disagree along traceable lines of descent, with rival interpretations clustering by
jurisdiction or lineage.

They do not. The explanations are scattered, mutually independent, and each looks like an
author supplying something reasonable to a figure that needed accounting for.

Compare the square. Every source from the 1720s onward says the same thing about the square,
because the square's meaning is anchored to what the instrument does. Nobody had to invent
it, so nobody invented a rival.

**Anchored symbols produce agreement. Unanchored symbols produce literature.**

By that test the Blazing Star arrived as a decorative or compositional element — stars are
what one puts in the upper part of an emblematic design — and acquired meanings afterwards,
in the ordinary way.

---

### Lévi's pentagram

Then, in the 1850s, something is added, and it can be dated to the year.

Éliphas Lévi's *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*, published in Paris in 1854–56, gives the
five-pointed star a systematic occult interpretation and — crucially — introduces the
contrast between its two orientations. Point upward: spirit governing the four elements, the
divine order, man upright. Point downward: the reverse, the goat, the subversion of that
order.[^2]

So far as anyone has been able to trace, **the inverted pentagram as an emblem of evil is
Lévi's invention.** It has no established earlier pedigree. Before the 1850s the five-pointed
star is a star; after Lévi it is a sign with a polarity, and the polarised version conquered
Western culture so completely that it is now impossible to see the figure without it.

This matters for Freemasonry in a specific way. The Blazing Star was in Masonic use for more
than a century before Lévi wrote, with the various vague Providential meanings listed above,
and no orientation doctrine whatever. The pentagram reading arrives afterwards and reaches
Masonry principally through Albert Pike, who took a great deal from Lévi without attribution
and whose *Morals and Dogma* of 1871 was given to Scottish Rite initiates for a century.

A modern reader encountering a Masonic five-pointed star and thinking of occult polarity is
having a nineteenth-century French thought about an eighteenth-century English emblem.

---

### The sun and the moon

The same pattern, in a smaller compass.

The sun and moon appear among the lesser lights, and the Craft's explanation is calendrical
and scriptural: they rule the day and the night as the Master rules the lodge, and the
formula echoes Genesis 1:16 almost verbatim. That is the documented meaning and it is
consistent across the eighteenth-century sources.

The overlay is alchemical. Sol and Luna, king and queen, gold and silver, the active and
receptive principles whose conjunction is the Great Work — this is the standard vocabulary
of the alchemical literature, it maps onto the Masonic pair without resistance, and it has
been applied to it constantly since the nineteenth century.

The mapping is elegant. It is also absent from every eighteenth-century Masonic source I have
examined, which give the Genesis reading and nothing more.

**And the ease of the mapping is itself a warning.** Sun and moon are the two most
over-determined symbols available to any tradition on earth. They are visible from
everywhere, they oppose each other in the obvious ways, and every symbolic system that has
ever existed has used them. Finding sun-and-moon symbolism in two traditions establishes
precisely nothing about their relationship.

This is the parallel-is-not-descent rule in its purest application. There is no plausible
world in which Freemasonry lacks sun and moon imagery, and therefore their presence cannot be
evidence of anything.

---

### What the light symbols are for

Step back from the individual figures and ask what the set does.

The Craft's light imagery has three registers and they operate simultaneously.

**Practical.** Candles in a room, controlling what is visible and when. The lesser lights are
frequently actual lights in actual positions.

**Cosmological.** Sun and moon and stars: the lodge is furnished with the heavens, which
makes it a model of the world as well as of the Temple, and the monitorial literature says so
explicitly — the lodge's covering is a clouded canopy or starry heaven.

**Moral.** Light as knowledge, darkness as ignorance, the movement from one to the other as
the entire arc of the ceremony.

Three registers, one vocabulary, and no theology in any of them. Once again the Craft has
found a way to be cosmic without being sectarian — the heavens belong to everyone.

---

### The honest summary

For the record, because this chapter has been mostly deflationary:

**The Blazing Star.** In Masonic use from the eighteenth century, meaning contested in the
Craft's own sources, given its occult polarity by Lévi in the 1850s and imported into
American Masonry through Pike.

**The sun and moon.** In Masonic use from the eighteenth century, explained scripturally,
with the alchemical reading as a nineteenth-century overlay and no transmission evidence.

**Neither is ancient. Neither is borrowed. Both are ordinary emblematic furniture that
acquired significance by use.**

I am aware that this is not a thrilling result. It is, however, what three centuries of
documents support, and the alternative — that these figures carry an ancient encoded
meaning — has never produced a route, a text or a date.

There remain two symbols in the Craft's visual vocabulary that are more famous than any of
these, that are used by the conspiracy literature as its principal exhibits, and that turn
out to be the most straightforwardly datable objects in the entire system.

One of them is not Masonic at all. The other cannot possibly be old, and the proof takes a
sentence.
