# PART VI — DEATH

# CHAPTER TWENTY-THREE
## Hiram and the Murder

The candidate does not watch the play. He is in it.

This is the fact about the third degree that distinguishes it from everything else in this
book, and it is routinely stated and rarely examined. In the first and second degrees a man
is conducted, questioned and instructed. In the third he is given a part — the part of a man
who is about to be killed — and the ceremony proceeds by doing to him what the story says
was done to the character.

He is the only person in the room who does not know how it ends.

---

### The legend, stated once

The drama first appears complete in Samuel Prichard's *Masonry Dissected*, London, 1730, and
the preceding volume established what can be said about its origins: essentially nothing. It
has no known author, no surviving draft, no datable moment of composition. The two-degree
system of the 1690s and the English exposures of 1723 and 1724 contain no trace of it; seven
years later it is fully formed in a hostile pamphlet that sold three editions in a
fortnight.[^1]

The outline, with the standing caution that details vary enormously by jurisdiction and that
no version here is universal or current:

At the building of Solomon's Temple, three grand masters hold the secrets of the master's
degree. Fifteen fellows of the craft, impatient for those secrets before they have earned
them, conspire to obtain them by force. Twelve repent; three do not. The three accost the
chief architect at the gates of the Temple as he leaves at high twelve, each demanding the
word; he refuses each; each strikes him, and the third blow kills him. They conceal the body
and mark the place. The workmen search, and the grave is discovered. The body is raised. And
because the word died with him, a substitute is adopted in its place.

Its biblical basis is a single craftsman in 1 Kings 7 who casts two bronze pillars, is
described as a widow's son of Tyre, and is never mentioned again. He is not an architect. He
is not murdered. Nothing happens to him.

Everything else — the conspiracy, the three blows, the burial, the search, the raising, the
lost word — is not in the Bible, not in Josephus, and not attested anywhere before 1730.

---

### What it is like to be the corpse

Now the part that matters for this book.

In most workings the candidate, playing the master, is accosted three times. The blows are
symbolic and the third is delivered in a manner that varies by jurisdiction; in many
workings he is then lowered or falls to the floor, and is covered or laid out, and lies
there while the ceremony continues around him.

He is on the floor of a room, in the dark or nearly so, being spoken about in the third
person, while men he knows conduct a search for a body that is his.

I want to be careful about what I claim here, and the claim is deliberately narrow.

**I do not know what that is like, and neither does anybody writing about it.** There is no
adequate first-person account. The few descriptions we have were written afterwards, by men
using the vocabulary the ceremony had just given them, which is the standard problem with
initiatory testimony and is close to insurmountable.

What can be said is structural, and it is enough to be going on with.

**He is not representing a dead man. He is doing what a dead man does**, which is nothing,
while others act. The distinction between playing a part and occupying a position is the
distinction between theatre and ritual, and this ceremony sits on the line: there is a script
and there are roles, and yet the protagonist has not read the script and is not acting.

**The passivity is total and it is the only moment in the ceremony where it is.** Everywhere
else he walks, kneels, speaks, answers, promises. Here he does nothing at all, and the
ceremony proceeds without his participation. For a period of minutes, the lodge continues in
his absence, about him.

**And he cannot leave.** Not because he is restrained — he could stand up — but because
standing up would end the ceremony in front of everyone. The social cost of interrupting is
what holds him there, which is the same mechanism that held him through the obligation.

---

### Why a murder?

The Craft could have staged anything. Why this?

Three answers are usually given and I think only the third survives.

**"It represents the death of the old self."** This is the standard interpretation and it is
almost certainly a back-formation. It is not what the eighteenth-century sources say; it
appears in the developed moral commentary and becomes universal in the nineteenth century.
The earlier texts present the legend as a narrative about fidelity — the master died rather
than betray a trust — and draw the moral accordingly.

**"It descends from the ancient mysteries."** Disposed of comprehensively in the preceding
volume and not re-argued. There is no route, no text, no chronology, and the eighteenth
century had no access to the content of the ancient mysteries, which were genuinely secret
and remain poorly attested.

**"Because the Craft needed a third degree and this is what somebody wrote."** Which sounds
deflationary and is, I think, correct — and becomes interesting the moment you ask what the
anonymous author was solving for.

---

### The design problem

Here is the problem as it would have presented itself in London around 1725.

The Craft has two degrees. There is pressure for a third — ordinary institutional pressure,
the same appetite for advancement that would shortly produce hundreds of high degrees in
France. A third degree needs to do something the first two do not.

The first two convey. The candidate arrives ignorant and is given words, signs, tools and
their meanings. A third degree of the same kind would just be more of the same, and everyone
would notice.

**So the third degree conveys nothing. It takes something away.**

The master's word is lost. It died with the man. The candidate, who has been brought through
darkness and ordeal to the climax of the system, is informed at the summit that the thing he
came for is *not available*, and is handed a substitute which is explicitly described as a
substitute.

That is an extraordinary design decision, and whether or not its author understood what he
had done, it is the reason the degree works and the reason everything in Parts VII and X
happened.

---

### Death and fidelity

One further observation about the content, because the modern reading has almost entirely
displaced the eighteenth-century one.

The legend is, on its face, a story about a man who is killed for refusing to divulge
something before the proper time. The three assassins want the master's word without having
earned it; he will not give it; they kill him; the word is lost for everyone, including
them.

Read plainly, that is a story about the *cost of premature disclosure*, and it is being
performed on a man who has just sworn an elaborate oath of secrecy.

The moral is not subtle and the timing is not accidental. The candidate has promised to
conceal. He is then made to play a man who concealed, at the cost of his life, and whose
murderers gained nothing by the murder because the thing they wanted was destroyed in the
taking.

**The third degree is, among other things, an argument for why the obligation matters.** It
says: this is what is at stake, and here is what happens when someone tries to take it, and
you have just been the person who refused.

Whether that reading exhausts the drama I doubt. But it is what the earliest sources support,
and it has the advantage of being about something the eighteenth century actually cared
about, rather than about a psychology of the self that had not been invented.

---

### What the ceremony does not say

A closing note on an absence.

The candidate is killed and raised, and the ritual does not tell him what the raising means.

It does not say that he has been reborn. It does not say that he has died to sin, or to the
world, or to his former self. It does not promise resurrection, salvation, or any state
beyond the one he was in an hour ago. The moral drawn in the earliest sources is about
fidelity; the moral drawn in the developed lectures is about the certainty of death and the
hope of immortality, expressed in terms so general that no member's religion is engaged.

For a ceremony that stages a death and a raising, that is a remarkable restraint. Every
tradition in this book with a comparable drama attaches a doctrine to it. Baptism means
something specific. The mysteries promised something. Freemasonry stages the most powerful
narrative event available to it and then declines to say what it was for.

Which is the same vacancy as the square, the same vacancy as the Great Architect, and the
same vacancy as the light — and by this point in the book I do not think it is a coincidence.
It is the system's governing principle, applied at its climax.

The lost word is the subject of the next chapter, and it is where the vacancy stops being a
technique and becomes a doctrine.
