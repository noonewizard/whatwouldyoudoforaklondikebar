# CHAPTER TWENTY-FIVE
## Raising the Dead

He is on the floor, and then he is not.

In most workings the raising is accomplished by a grip and a physical lift — the candidate
is taken by the hand and brought from the floor to his feet by the Master, in a manner the
ritual specifies and which is among the things a visiting brother may be examined on.[^1]

It is the last physical event of the ceremony and the one the degree is named for. A Mason is
not initiated into the third degree; he is *raised*.

---

### The most over-subscribed pattern in the world

Death and return is not a Masonic idea, and the difficulty of this chapter is not finding
parallels. It is that there are too many to be informative.

Osiris is dismembered and reassembled. Adonis dies and returns. Persephone descends and
comes back annually. Christ is crucified and rises. Baptism, on Paul's explicit account in
Romans 6, is burial and resurrection. The alchemical work passes through putrefaction. Sufi
*fana'* is annihilation followed by subsistence. Seeds are buried and come up. The sun goes
down and returns.

Given a list like that, the temptation is to conclude that these are variants of one thing,
and that Freemasonry is a late member of the family.

**The list is the reason not to conclude it.**

This is the parallel-is-not-descent rule at its most necessary, and the argument is
statistical rather than historical. If a pattern appears in nearly every tradition that has
ever existed, then its appearance in any two of them carries almost no information about
their relationship. Evidence has to be capable of discriminating. A feature shared by
everything discriminates nothing.

There are only so many available images for fundamental change. A thing can become another
thing gradually, or it can stop being what it was and start being something else — and the
second is death and return, and human beings reach for it because they have watched it
happen to seeds, to the sun, to the seasons and to each other for as long as there have been
human beings.

---

### So what can be said?

Three things, in ascending order of interest.

**First: the Masonic version's materials are traceable and they are local.** The names are
biblical. The setting is the Temple. The vocabulary is architectural. There is no sulphur, no
nigredo, no Osiris, no Persephone, no Egyptian formula. Whoever composed the drama in the
1720s was working with 1 Kings and the craft's own legendary history, and the preceding
volume ran the transmission tests on the alternatives and they failed.

**Second: the Masonic version is unusually light on doctrine.** I noted this at the end of
Chapter 23 and it bears repeating here because the comparison makes it sharp. Christian
baptism means something specific and the liturgy says what. The mysteries promised something,
however vaguely attested. Alchemy's putrefaction is a stage in a process with a defined
outcome.

Masonry stages a death and a raising and then declines to specify what has been
accomplished. The earliest sources draw a moral about fidelity. The developed lectures draw
one about mortality and a general hope of immortality, in terms so unspecific that no
member's actual religion is engaged.

**Third, and this is the one I think matters: the candidate is raised by another man's
hand.**

Not by his own effort. Not by a formula. Not by a god. A man who has been through this
before takes his hand, in a specified grip, and lifts.

I do not want to over-read a stage direction. But the Craft had many options for how to get
a man off the floor, and the one it chose puts the agency in the hands of the fraternity.
The degree that dramatises the loss of the word ends with a demonstration that what remains
available is other people.

Whether the eighteenth century meant that, I cannot say. It is what the ceremony does.

---

### The raising as the system's one positive claim

The third point above deserves more weight than a stage direction usually gets, because it is
the only place in the whole Craft system where something is unambiguously *supplied* rather
than withheld.

Follow the pattern of the degrees. The candidate is told he will receive light, and receives
an ordinary lighted room. He is told of a secret, and finds it is a mode of recognition. He
is brought to the climax of the system and told the essential word is lost. At every point
where a tradition would normally deliver, this one defers.

**And then a man takes his hand and lifts him off the floor.**

That is not deferred, not symbolic in the sense the rest of it is symbolic, and not a
substitute for something absent. It happens, physically, and it is the one event in the
ceremony that requires another person's body to be present. A candidate can be talked to at a
distance; he cannot be raised at one.

It is worth being careful here, because this is the kind of observation that invites more
than it can carry. The grip is a Masonic mode of recognition with an institutional function,
and reading it as a statement about human solidarity is an interpretation, not a finding.
The eighteenth-century sources do not say that this is what it means, and Chapter 21's
disagreement principle applies: had they intended a doctrine, they would probably have
recorded one.

What can be said without interpretation is structural, and the structure is consistent.
**In a system organised around what is not given, the one thing given is another person.**
Whether that was designed, arrived at, or is an artefact of the fact that somebody has to
get the candidate up, the arrangement is what it is — and Chapter 47's argument that the
Craft's measurable effects are the social ones rather than the private ones begins here.

---

### The raising and the obligation

A structural observation connecting this to Chapter 10.

The obligation is a promise made by one man to a room. The raising is an act performed by
the room upon one man.

They are the two poles of the ceremony and they are exact inversions of each other. In the
first he gives and they receive; in the second they give and he receives. Between them lies
the death, in which neither happens and he does nothing at all.

That is a more elegant architecture than an accreted ceremony has any business having, and
I record it with the caution that elegance is exactly the thing a pattern-seeking observer
would find whether or not it was put there. Chapter 38 is about that hazard and I am not
exempt from it.

What can be said without interpretation is that the ceremony's two most physical moments are
a promise given and a hand taken, and that the second is the last thing that happens.

---

### The comparative question, properly posed

There is a legitimate comparative enquiry here and it is worth distinguishing from the
illegitimate one.

**Illegitimate:** Masonry's raising resembles Osiris, therefore Masonry descends from Egypt.
Fails on route, chronology, text and continuity.

**Legitimate:** given that many unrelated traditions stage death and return, what does the
structure *do*, and does the Masonic version do it in a distinctive way?

Posed that way, a real answer is available.

What death-and-return ritual does, across the cases, is mark a change of status as
*discontinuous*. The person who emerges is not a modified version of the person who entered;
the ceremony asserts a break. That is why the pattern is so widespread: societies frequently
need to treat a change as absolute — child to adult, outsider to member, layman to priest —
and gradual imagery cannot do it. You need a break, and death is the break everyone
understands.

The Masonic version is distinctive in one respect and it is the respect this whole part of
the book keeps returning to: **the break is asserted and its content is not specified.** He
died and was raised and is now a Master Mason, and what that means is left to him.

---

### Comparison as illumination, not genealogy

Which is, I think, the right general use of comparison in this subject, and worth stating as
a rule before Part VIII takes it up properly.

Comparative material cannot establish that Freemasonry inherited anything. The preceding
volume demonstrated that at length and this book takes it as settled.

But comparison can do something else, and it is not trivial. It can show which features of a
tradition are *forced* and which are *chosen*.

Death-and-return is forced: any system needing to mark a discontinuity will arrive at
something like it. The Masonic drama's specific materials — the Temple, the architect, the
three blows — are chosen, and their choice is historically explicable.

And the refusal to specify what the death means is *distinctive*, which makes it the most
interesting thing about the ceremony, and which comparison is what reveals.

You cannot see that a tradition is unusual until you have looked at others. That is what
comparison is for, and it is not genealogy.

---

### What remains on the floor

The candidate stands. The ceremony proceeds to its explanations and its charges and
eventually to supper.

Something has been done to him that he will describe, if asked fifty years later, as having
made an impression. The record supports that much: men who left Masonry and men who stayed
both remembered the third degree, and the remembering is documented in a way that nothing
else about this subject is.

What was done was to arrange for him to be, for several minutes, a body on the floor of a
room while other people looked for him.

The next chapter asks what a tradition is doing when it makes its members rehearse their
own deaths — and whether Freemasonry's version of that rehearsal is consolation, or
something considerably colder.
