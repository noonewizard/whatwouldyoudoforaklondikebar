# CHAPTER TWENTY-SIX
## Memento Mori

In certain continental and higher-degree workings the candidate is placed, before the
ceremony, in a small chamber furnished with objects: a skull, a candle, an hourglass, bread
and water, sometimes salt and sulphur, sometimes written sentences on the walls. He is left
there to reflect and to write something — in some versions a testament.

It is called the Chamber of Reflection. **It is not a feature of Craft initiation in the
English constitution and it is not universal**, and it is worth saying so at the outset
because it is the single most frequently misattributed element in popular accounts of
Freemasonry.[^1]

Where it does exist, it is unambiguous. The candidate sits with a skull.

---

### The genre

*Memento mori* — remember that you must die — is a European devotional genre with an
enormous material culture, and Freemasonry's participation in it is unremarkable.

Skulls on tombs. The hourglass and scythe on eighteenth-century gravestones, thousands of
which survive in English and American churchyards. The *vanitas* still life, with its guttering
candle and its bubble and its decaying fruit. The danse macabre. The death's-head ring. The
memorial mourning jewellery of the Georgian period, which was a substantial industry.

A culture with high mortality, public funerals, and a theological framework in which the hour
of death was the decisive moment of a life produced these objects constantly, and
eighteenth-century Freemasons were members of that culture before they were members of
anything else.

So when Masonic symbolism includes a skull, an hourglass, a scythe, a coffin or a sprig of
evergreen, the first question is not what secret doctrine this encodes. It is why anyone
would expect an eighteenth-century fraternity *not* to have these things.

---

### The funeral

The Craft's most public engagement with death is the one least discussed, and it is
documented better than almost anything else in this book.

Masonic funerals were a significant social phenomenon from the eighteenth century onward. A
deceased brother was buried with the ceremonies of the Craft: a procession in regalia, an
address, the deposit of an apron and sometimes a sprig of acacia in the grave, and a form of
words. In America especially these were substantial public events, reported in newspapers,
attended by large numbers.[^2]

Three things follow that bear on this book's argument.

**The ceremony was visible.** A society allegedly devoted to concealment buried its members
in the street, in uniform, in front of the town.

**It supplied something the churches could not.** A Masonic funeral could be held for a man
whose religious affiliation was ambiguous, or whose denomination was locally in the minority,
or who had died away from home among strangers who nevertheless knew him by his membership.
In a mobile century that mattered.

**And it closed the obligation.** The relief of a brother's widow and orphans is the most
concrete undertaking in the Masonic oath, and it is the one that produced accounts. The
funeral is the moment the fraternity's promise becomes payable.

---

### What the Craft says about death

Here the evidence is thin in an interesting way.

The developed Masonic lectures on mortality are *general* — that all men must die, that the
faithful may hope, that the acacia signifies immortality of the soul. The formulae are broad
enough that a Christian, a Jew and a deist could each hear their own belief in them, and
narrow enough to say something.

This is the 1723 settlement operating at a funeral, and it is the hardest place for it to
operate. Anderson's formula was designed for a lodge room where the question of what happens
after death need not arise. At a graveside it arises unavoidably, and the Craft's answer is
a carefully constructed generality.

**I find this more impressive than the criticism it usually attracts.** A fraternity that
buries men of four religions without offending any of them, while saying something rather
than nothing, has solved a problem that European societies had recently been at war over.

---

### The colder reading

But there is a reading of Masonic death symbolism that is not consolatory, and I think it is
closer to what the Craft actually does.

Consider the objects. A skull. An hourglass. A scythe. A coffin. A grave.

None of these is a symbol of *hope*. They are instruments of measurement, in the same family
as the square and the plumb — and what they measure is time remaining.

The hourglass does not say that you will live again. It says that a quantity is running out
and cannot be refilled. The scythe does not say that the harvest is gathered in; it says that
the cut is coming. The twenty-four-inch gauge, which is a working tool rather than a
mortality emblem, is explained as dividing the day into portions for labour, refreshment and
service — which is to say, as an instrument for allocating a resource that is finite.

**Read as a set, the Craft's death symbolism is not about the afterlife. It is about the
present one being limited.**

That is a moral doctrine rather than a theological one, and it is entirely consistent with
Anderson's settlement, because it requires no view whatever about what follows. A materialist
and a Calvinist can both agree that there is a fixed and unknown quantity of time and that
wasting it is a fault.

The Craft found a way to make death morally operative without making it theologically
committal, and the instrument it used was measurement.

---

### The rehearsal

Which returns us to the third degree.

A man is laid on the floor in the character of a dead man and raised by another man's hand.
He is told, in the lectures that follow, that this is the closing of a life and that he
should consider his own.

**What is he rehearsing?**

Not immortality: the ceremony makes no promise. Not judgement: nothing is weighed.

He is rehearsing *being absent while other people continue*. That is what the minutes on the
floor consist of: the lodge proceeding in his absence, about him, searching for him. He hears
it. He is the only person in the room who experiences the ceremony from the position of the
missing.

If Masonic death symbolism has a psychological content — and this is an interpretation, not a
finding, and I mark it as such — it is that one. Not *you will die* as a proposition, which
everyone knows and nobody feels, but a few minutes of lying still while the world audibly
carries on.

The Craft could not have told him that. It could only arrange for him to be there.

---

### What Part VI establishes

Four chapters on death, and the findings are these.

**The legend is undated and undescended.** First in print 1730, no author, biblical
materials, no transmission from any older tradition.

**The candidate is the corpse, not the audience**, and that is the degree's distinguishing
feature.

**The word is lost and the loss is doctrine**, and it is the design decision from which the
whole subsequent history of Masonic and post-Masonic esotericism follows.

**The raising is performed by other people.**

**And the death symbolism measures rather than promises.**

Nothing in any of this requires antiquity, and none of it is magic under any definition
current in 1730.

But it is now impossible to avoid the question that Part VII exists for. We have watched a
ceremony that stages a death, withholds a word, and hands a man a set of instruments for
working on himself — and every one of those elements has a counterpart in the traditions that
the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries called magical.

The counterparts are not descent. The preceding volume settled that.

So what are they?
