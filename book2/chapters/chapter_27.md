# PART VII — THE MAGICAL TRADITION

# CHAPTER TWENTY-SEVEN
## Magic and Transformation

In about the year 300, a Syrian philosopher wrote a long treatise explaining that what he did
was not magic.

Iamblichus was defending ritual practice against his own teacher's school. Porphyry had
argued that the soul ascends by contemplation — by thinking its way up. Iamblichus held that
the soul has come the whole way down into matter and cannot think its way anywhere, and that
what raises it is ritual action performed with material tokens which the gods have placed in
the world for the purpose.

He called this *theourgia*, god-working, and he spent a great deal of the treatise
distinguishing it from *goēteia*, which is sorcery, which is what other people do.[^1]

Fifteen centuries later, a Bordeaux merchant conducting angelic invocations inside a Masonic
structure would insist that his operations were religious and not magical. A century after
him, a French author who had spent his twenties writing socialist pamphlets would
distinguish *haute magie* from the superstition of common sorcerers. Fifty years after him,
an English poet would add a letter to the end of the word to distinguish what he did from
stage conjuring.

**Almost everyone in this book who performs what we would call magic denies that it is
magic.**

That fact is where this chapter starts, because it means the obvious question — did the
Masons think they were doing magic? — is nearly useless, and a better one is available.

---

### Five types, never blurred

Before anything else, a piece of apparatus. The word *magic* appears in five distinct kinds
of statement and conflating them has wrecked more argument in this field than any other
error.

**Type A — the practitioner's definition.** *Agrippa called this* magia *and meant this by
it.* Established from the practitioner's own texts. Carries no implication about whether
anything works.

**Type B — the scholarly historical category.** *Historians of the period use "magic" to name
this body of practice.* Established from named secondary scholarship.

**Type C — the anthropological category.** *Magic as a class of ritual act intended to
produce effects.* Comparative theory, and contested.

**Type D — the psychological claim.** *Practices of this kind may produce this effect in
participants.* Modern literature, hedged, never applied to a named individual.

**Type E — the metaphysical claim.** *A supernatural mechanism objectively operates.*

**Type E is never asserted in this book, in either direction.** Not that spirits exist; not
that they do not; not that the ritual really works; not that it is all psychology. Nothing in
my evidence could settle any of those, and the moment a historian answers them he has changed
profession.

Everything below is Types A and B, with C used for comparison and D reserved for Part XI.

---

### The word does not stay still

Here is the history that makes the question answerable. Track what practitioners meant, in
order, and the word moves under everybody's feet.

**c. 300 — Iamblichus.** *Magic* names what his opponents do. His own ritual work is theurgy
and is categorically other. The founding denial.

**1489 — Ficino.** *Magia naturalis* is medicine. Astral influence drawn down by assembling
materials of the right quality at the right hour. He is careful to say he addresses nobody —
this is the manipulation of properties, not traffic with persons. He was nervous about it
anyway.

**1533 — Agrippa.** Three books: natural, celestial, ceremonial. *Magia* is philosophy
completed — the study of nature in its lower storey, the heavens in its middle, and the
divine in its upper. And Book Three crosses Ficino's line, addressing angels and
intelligences by name.

**c. 1700.** The word has become chiefly a term for what respectable knowledge is not.
Randall Styers's argument is that this is its primary modern function: magic is the negative
space against which religion and science defined themselves.[^2]

**1761 — Martinez de Pasqually.** Founds an order performing theurgic operations inside a
Masonic degree structure, and describes the work as religious. The denial again, in the
eighteenth century, inside the institution this book is about.

**1854 — Éliphas Lévi.** And here the word changes decisively. *Haute magie* is an operation
of the trained will upon a universal medium, in which the ceremonial apparatus exists to
concentrate the operator's imagination and resolve. **The instrument is the operator, and the
object of the operation is substantially the operator.**

**1904 — Crowley.** "Magick is the Science and Art of causing Change to occur in conformity
with Will." And he immediately notes that this includes writing a book and baking a cake, and
he means it.[^3]

---

### The migration, stated

Read that sequence with the dates attached and the movement is unmistakable.

**In 1489 magic is a way of acting on the world by manipulating its hidden properties.**

**By 1854 magic is a way of acting on the operator by means of symbol, ritual, attention and
will.**

The object moved. It used to be outside; it is now inside. And the shift is not a
degeneration into psychology — Lévi would have rejected that description furiously — but a
relocation of where the work is understood to happen.

Which produces the observation that this book has been building toward since its first page,
and which I will state as plainly as I can:

**A Freemasonry that was not magic under the 1489 definition became magic under the 1854
definition without altering a single ritual.**

Nothing changed in the lodge. The ceremony of 1730 is the ceremony of 1870 in all essentials.
What changed is that the word *magic* moved across the territory the ceremony was already
standing on.

---

### What that observation is and is not

It is a fact about the history of a word. It is not, by itself, a fact about Freemasonry, and
the distinction is the whole discipline of this chapter.

If tomorrow the word *medicine* were redefined to mean any activity that makes people feel
better, then conversation with friends would be medicine, and nothing about conversation
would have changed. The redefinition would tell us something about the word's career and
nothing about friendship.

So the question cannot be settled by noticing that the definitions converged. It has to be
asked substantively: **does the Masonic ceremony do the kind of thing the 1854 definition
names?** And that is an empirical question about ritual effects, which is what Part XI is
for and which is deferred to it deliberately.

What can be established here is narrower and still substantial.

**The nineteenth-century occultists who looked at Freemasonry and saw a magical order were
not making a category error.** They were applying a definition current in their own century
to a practice that met it. Whether their history was right — and the preceding volume showed
that it was comprehensively wrong — is a separate matter from whether their classification
was reasonable.

They were wrong about the lineage and possibly right about the mechanism. That is the
book's working hypothesis and it is now properly stated.

---

### Transformation as the shared object

One more thing before the three chapters that follow.

If the 1854 definition locates magic's object in the operator, then the traditions this part
of the book examines — Hermetic correspondence, Kabbalistic structure, alchemy — have a
common feature that has nothing to do with whether they are related.

**All of them are systems in which the practitioner is what is being worked on.**

The alchemist's material is also himself; the texts say so, ambiguously and repeatedly. The
theurgist's operation aims at his own ascent. The Hermetic initiate of the thirteenth
treatise undergoes a regeneration he must also bring about. And the Masonic candidate, as
Chapter 13 set out, is simultaneously the mason and the stone.

The preceding volume established that there is no transmission between these. This book's
proposal — advanced in Chapter 13 and tested in Chapter 48 — is that the convergence is
structural: **any system that sets out to change a person through that person's own
deliberate action will produce this doubling, because the doubling is what deliberate
self-change consists of.**

That is a weaker claim than descent and a more useful one, because it predicts that the
pattern will appear in things with no relation to each other at all — in apprenticeship, in
training, in therapy, in the learning of any difficult skill.

It does.

The next three chapters take the three traditions in turn and ask, of each, a functional
question rather than a historical one. Not where did it come from — the preceding volume
answered that. But: **what kind of instrument is this, and what does it let its user do?**
