# CHAPTER TWENTY-EIGHT
## Hermetic Correspondence

Open Agrippa's *Occult Philosophy* at the second book and you find columns.

For each number from one to twelve, a table: the divine name of that number, the order of
angels, the sphere, the metal, the stone, the animal, the plant, the part of the body, the
human faculty. Jupiter runs to tin, to sapphire, to the eagle, to the liver, to a particular
magic square of sixteen cells, to a named intelligence. Then the next planet, and the
next.[^1]

To a modern eye it looks like arbitrary list-making — the kind of thing a certain sort of
person does with index cards.

It is nothing of the sort, and understanding what it actually is unlocks the whole of
Renaissance magic and, eventually, the Golden Dawn.

---

### Why the tables are technical documents

The tables presuppose a cosmology, and given the cosmology they are not associations but
*specifications*.

The cosmology is the one assembled in late antiquity and inherited by the Renaissance: the
universe emanates from a single source, descending through levels — intellect, soul,
heavens, elements — so that everything at a lower level is a diminished expression of
something at a higher one. Nothing is disconnected from anything. The whole thing is
continuous, like light attenuating with distance rather than like objects placed in a room.

Grant that, and correspondence follows necessarily. If gold, the sun, the lion and the heart
are all expressions of the same principle at different levels of descent, then they are not
*like* each other. They are the same thing, appearing four times.

**Which makes Agrippa's columns a map of where the levels touch.**

That is a technical claim, of the same logical type as a chemist's table of reagents. It is
wrong — the cosmology it depends on is not how the universe is arranged — but it is wrong in
the way a superseded scientific theory is wrong, not in the way an arbitrary list is
meaningless.

---

### What the table lets its user do

Now the functional question, which is what this part of the book is for.

A table of correspondences is a **generative instrument**. It does three things that nothing
else in the Western esoteric inheritance does.

**It converts a purpose into a procedure.** A practitioner with an aim — healing, protection,
an intellectual operation — looks up the corresponding column and finds the hour, the metal,
the plant, the colour, the name. The table tells him what to assemble. Without it he would
have to invent, and invention has no authority.

**It makes rituals composable.** Because every element is indexed to the same structure, a
practitioner can build a ceremony for a purpose no previous practitioner addressed, using
components that are guaranteed to be consistent with one another. This is the property that
matters most, and it is rare. Most ritual traditions transmit *specific* ceremonies. A
correspondence system transmits a grammar.

**And it is a memory system.** This is the property least often noticed and, I suspect, the
most historically important. A table organises an enormous quantity of heterogeneous material
— names, plants, metals, hours, angels — into a small number of columns anchored to figures
everyone already knows. That is exactly the structure of the classical art of memory, and it
is why the material survived: it was storable.

---

### The route, stated briefly

The preceding volume established the transmission and I will not re-argue it: Agrippa
drafted around 1510, published in 1533; an English translation appeared in 1651; the work
remained in the second-hand trade for two centuries; and in the 1880s it was on the shelves
of William Wynn Westcott's Hermetic library of two hundred and eighty-six items, catalogued
by Alison Butler.[^2]

From there it entered the Golden Dawn's system of attributions, and from there Crowley's
*Liber 777* of 1909, which is nothing but tables and is still in use.

**Availability is not transmission**, and the difference matters here in a specific way. The
same books were equally available to Freemasons for the same two centuries. They did not
enter Masonic ritual. The exposures of 1723 to 1762 contain no correspondences, no planetary
hours, no angelic names, no metals, no colours.

Two traditions, one library, and only one of them took the tables.

---

### Why Masonry did not take them

I think the answer is structural and it tells us something about what kind of thing a Masonic
degree is.

**A correspondence system is a technology for operators. Masonic ritual is a technology for
candidates.**

The distinction is not subtle once stated. Agrippa's tables are for somebody who intends to
*do* something — to compose an operation and perform it, with an aim in view. They are
useless to a person who is having something done to him.

Masonic ceremonial has no operators in that sense. The officers are not practitioners; they
are performers of a fixed script, and the script's object is the man in the middle of the
room. Nobody in a lodge is composing anything. Nobody has an aim beyond conducting the
ceremony correctly.

So the Craft had no use for a generative grammar, because it was not generating. It had one
ceremony, performed the same way, on a succession of different men.

**A tradition that repeats one rite does not need a table of correspondences. A tradition
that composes new rites cannot function without one.**

That single distinction accounts for most of the difference between Freemasonry and
ceremonial magic, and it explains why the Golden Dawn — which took the Masonic apparatus and
filled it with Agrippan material — needed both. It borrowed the ceremony from one tradition
and the grammar from another, because it intended to do both things: initiate candidates and
compose operations.

---

### Testing the distinction

The operator/candidate distinction is doing a great deal of work, so it should be checked
against cases it might get wrong.

**A tradition with both.** The Golden Dawn is the obvious test and it passes: it initiated
candidates through graded ceremonies *and* trained its members to compose and perform
operations, and it accordingly required both an initiatory apparatus and a correspondence
system. It took the first from Masonry and the second from the Agrippan tradition, and the
seam between them is visible in its own documents, where the grade rituals and the knowledge
lectures are separate bodies of material serving different purposes.

**A tradition with operators and no initiation.** Much of early modern learned magic is of
this kind — a reader with a book, working alone, composing operations from tables. It has the
grammar and no ceremony at all, which is what the distinction predicts.

**A tradition with initiation and no operators.** This is Masonry, and also a great deal of
religious sacramental practice: a fixed rite, performed on people, by functionaries who are
not composing anything. None of these traditions develops correspondence tables, and where
tables-like material appears in them it is usually classificatory rather than generative —
a list of what things mean, not a grammar for combining them.

**The apparent counter-example.** Ritual magic orders that inherited Masonic structure but
did little operative work — some of the fringe Masonic bodies of the nineteenth century — did
sometimes accumulate correspondence material without using it. This is the case the
distinction does not straightforwardly explain, and the explanation available is sociological
rather than functional: by 1890 a correspondence system had become a marker of seriousness,
something an esoteric order was expected to possess whether or not anyone in it was
composing operations. A grammar can be acquired as a credential.

That last case is worth holding onto, because it is how a great deal of esoteric material
travels. Not because anyone needs it, but because having it signals what kind of body you
are — which is also, precisely, how the Agrippan material came to be attached to
Freemasonry's name in the nineteenth century without ever entering its ritual.

---

### The one correspondence Masonry does have

An exception, and it is instructive.

The Craft does operate one systematic correspondence: **the working tools are mapped to
moral qualities.** Square to rectitude, level to equality, plumb to uprightness, gauge to the
division of time, gavel to the conscience.

That is a table. It is small, it has one column, and it is consistent.

And notice its character. It runs from *physical function* to *moral quality* — the square
means rectitude because a square tests for rectitude — which is the structural anchoring
described in Chapter 17. Agrippa's correspondences run between *levels of a cosmos*, which
requires the cosmos to be granted.

The Masonic mapping needs no metaphysics at all. You can accept that the plumb-line's
indifference is a good image for moral standards while believing nothing whatever about
emanation.

**Which is why it survived the Enlightenment and Agrippa's did not.** The correspondence
tables depend on a cosmology that was dismantled between 1600 and 1700. The Masonic tools
depend on the observation that instruments of measurement do not consult the person being
measured — which remains true, and will go on being true.

The Craft built its one correspondence system out of the only material that could not be
falsified: the behaviour of a carpenter's square.

---

### What survives

So the Hermetic inheritance's contribution to this book is not a doctrine that Masonry
absorbed. It is a *tool* that Masonry declined and that the nineteenth century picked up
instead.

And the tool's real achievement is worth stating without condescension, because the
cosmology's falsity has tended to obscure it.

Somebody, working across fifteen centuries, built a system in which every element of a vast
inherited symbolic vocabulary — gods, plants, metals, hours, numbers, letters, colours,
cards — could be located on a single structure and combined with any other element with
guaranteed consistency.

That is a serious intellectual artefact. It organises more material than any other structure
in Western esotericism, it is learnable, it is generative, and it has been in continuous
practical use for five hundred years.

Whether it corresponds to anything is a question this book does not answer. That it is a
remarkable piece of construction is not in doubt.

The same could be said of the structure it was eventually mapped onto — a diagram of ten
spheres and twenty-two paths that arrived in Western esotericism at four removes from its
origin, and which turned out to have exactly the property that a graded initiatory order
needs.
