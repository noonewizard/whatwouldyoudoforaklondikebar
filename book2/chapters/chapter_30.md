# CHAPTER THIRTY
## Alchemy and the Masonic Question

In the 1650s a young American in London was keeping laboratory notes.

George Starkey recorded quantities, temperatures, the number of hours a vessel was held at a
given heat, what happened, what went wrong, and what he proposed to try next. He worked with
Robert Boyle. He supplied materials and techniques to men whose names are in the history of
chemistry.

He also published, under the name Eirenaeus Philalethes, treatises in the dense allegorical
idiom of the alchemical tradition — dragons, kings, doves, the green lion devouring the sun —
which were read across Europe as mystical texts and which were, as Lawrence Principe and
William Newman have shown, encoded laboratory procedures.[^1]

That double existence is the thing about alchemy that a reader coming from the popular
literature will least expect, and it has to be established before the Masonic question can
be asked properly.

---

### Against the psychological reduction

For most of the twentieth century educated opinion held that alchemy was not really about
matter — that it was a system of spiritual transformation cast in chemical metaphor, the
furnace a symbol of the self, the philosophers' stone a symbol of enlightenment. Jung gave
this its most influential form.

It is largely wrong, and the wrongness has a traceable origin.

Principe and Newman have established, by the unglamorous method of reading the technical
literature and repeating the experiments, that early modern alchemy was a laboratory practice
pursuing material goals with material methods. The allegorical language was *Decknamen* —
cover-names, a professional cipher protecting valuable procedures, exactly as a modern
laboratory protects a process. Principe has taken notoriously obscure passages, decoded them,
performed them, and obtained the described results.

The spiritual reading traces to the nineteenth century: to Mary Anne Atwood's *Suggestive
Inquiry into the Hermetic Mystery* of 1850, and to the occult revival that followed, which
found it congenial. Waite adopted a version; Jung inherited it and gave it a psychological
vocabulary.[^2]

**So the "spiritual alchemy" that popular books present as the tradition's inner meaning is
in fact its Victorian reinterpretation** — which places it, chronologically, in Part X of
this book rather than here.

Two qualifications, because the corrective can be overdone. The practitioners were not
materialists in our sense: they thought matter was alive, that metals grew in the earth, and
that the same principles governed a furnace and a soul. And some alchemical writing genuinely
is devotional — the German Lutheran tradition around Jakob Böhme used alchemical vocabulary
for frankly theological ends. The point is not that spiritual alchemy never existed. It is
that it was one strand among several and the twentieth century mistook it for the whole.

### The parallel, tested

Now the Masonic question, and it is the same one the preceding volume tested and which needs
restating here because Part VII has changed what we can say about it.

Rough ashlar becomes perfect ashlar. *Prima materia* becomes the Stone. The candidate dies
and is raised; the alchemical matter putrefies — goes black, stinks, appears dead — and is
reconstituted. The *Rosarium Philosophorum* of 1550 has a woodcut sequence in which a royal
pair are united, die, lie entombed while a soul departs upward, and are revived.

The resemblance is real and has been noticed by every writer on the subject since the
eighteenth century.

Run the tests.

**Provenance.** No document connects the composition of the third degree to any alchemical
text.

**Chronology.** Permits it easily. The alchemical literature is centuries older. This is the
one test the claim passes and it is the weakest of the six.

**Transmission.** No route. We do not know who composed the third degree — that is one of the
genuine mysteries of Masonic history — and there is no draft, no correspondence, no
attribution.

**Intertextuality.** This is where it dies. The Masonic legend's materials are identifiable
and they are elsewhere: 1 Kings and 2 Chronicles for the craftsman and the Temple, the
medieval Old Charges for the craft's legendary history, and the ordinary dramatic repertoire
of the 1720s. The vocabulary is architectural and biblical throughout. There is no sulphur,
no mercury, no salt, no nigredo, no *prima materia*, no stone in the alchemical sense.

**Institutional continuity.** None in the relevant period. The Gold- und Rosenkreuz is a
genuine institutional link between alchemy and Masonic structure — and it dates from the
1750s onward, *forty years after* the third degree is in print, and the influence runs the
other way: an alchemical order recruiting through Masonry, requiring Master Mason status as
the price of admission.

**Anachronism.** The spiritual-transformation reading of alchemy that makes the parallel look
meaningful is itself a nineteenth-century development. To say that the third degree "is"
alchemical transformation is to read an eighteenth-century ritual through a Victorian
interpretation of a seventeenth-century practice.

**One test passed, four failed, one adverse. The parallel is real; the descent is
unsupported.**

### Why they look alike anyway

Which leaves the interesting question, and Part VII has put us in a position to answer it
better than the preceding volume could.

Two answers, and the second is the one this book is actually arguing.

**The cheap answer: death-and-return is universal.** Chapter 25 made this case. A pattern
present in nearly every tradition that has existed carries almost no information about the
relationship between any two of them. The alchemists reached for it because their matter
literally went black and then changed; the composers of the third degree reached for it
because they were dramatising the loss of a master; Christian culture had been soaked in it
for seventeen centuries.

**The better answer: both are systems in which the operator is the material.**

This is the doubling from Chapter 13, and Chapter 27 set out why it recurs. Any system that
undertakes to change a person through that person's own deliberate action must produce it,
because you cannot be improved without being both the improver and the improved.

Alchemy has it in its most acute form: the texts are deliberately ambiguous about whether the
substance in the vessel is a substance or the man at the furnace, and the ambiguity is not
sloppiness. It is the tradition's central claim.

Masonry has it in its plainest form: here are two stones, you are one of them, here is the
chisel.

**They converge because the structure is a constraint, not a doctrine** — and the evidence
for that is that the same structure appears in apprenticeship, in athletic training, in
therapy and in every attempt anyone has ever made to become better at something. None of
those inherited it from Hermes either.

---

### Where Masonry does intersect ceremonial magic

To close Part VII, the honest map — because a chapter that only demolishes is doing half the
job.

**Craft Freemasonry does not intersect ceremonial magic at all.** No correspondences, no
invocation, no planetary hours, no angelic names used operatively, no circle, no talismans,
no goal beyond the ceremony's own completion. The exposures establish this across forty years
and two countries.

**Certain eighteenth-century high degrees do intersect it, and the date is 1761.** Martinez
de Pasqually's Élus Coëns performed theurgic operations — prepared spaces, astrologically
determined times, invocations of divine and angelic names, with a doctrine of the
reintegration of fallen beings — inside a structure of Masonic degrees. That is ceremonial
magic in a Masonic vessel, and it is documented, and it is late, and its author is known.

**The Gold- und Rosenkreuz intersects it from the 1750s**, teaching alchemy to candidates who
had to be Master Masons first.

**And from 1888 the intersection is total**, in an order that was not Masonic at all but was
built by Freemasons out of Masonic parts.

In every case the direction is the same and the preceding volume established it: **the
content came from outside and the form came from the lodge.**

What Part VII adds is why the form was worth having. A correspondence system is a grammar
without a ceremony. A Masonic degree is a ceremony without a grammar. Each tradition had
exactly what the other lacked, and when they were finally combined — by three Freemasons in
London in 1888, working from a Latin anthology and a second-hand bookshop — the result has
outlasted both.

Which is an account of a historical event. Whether any of it *works*, in any sense a modern
reader would accept, is the question the next two parts exist to approach.
