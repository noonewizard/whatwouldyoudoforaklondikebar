# PART VIII — THE MIND INSIDE THE RITUAL

# CHAPTER THIRTY-ONE
## Liminality and the Comparative Frame

The best-kept secret in the history of the West was kept.

For something like two thousand years, initiates walked the road from Athens to Eleusis,
fasted, drank a preparation called the *kykeon*, entered a windowless hall called the
Telesterion, and saw or heard or underwent something. Thousands of people knew what it was.
Aeschylus, Sophocles, Plato, Cicero. Emperors were initiated. The obligation not to speak of
it held across twenty centuries and every social class, and then the sanctuary was closed at
the end of the fourth century and the knowledge went out of the world.

**We do not know what happened in there.** Not vaguely — at all. The reconstructions on offer
are inference from fragments, and the recent scholarship has been steadily dismantling the
confident ones.[^1]

I begin the comparative chapter with that because it is the control case, and because of the
contrast it sets up.

The Eleusinian secret was kept for two millennia. The Masonic secret was published in a
London newspaper in 1723, for a few pence, and the institution grew.

**And Masonry's is the form that spread across the world.**

---

### The matrix

Set the traditions side by side, as the comparative literature always does, and the first
thing to establish is what the comparison can and cannot show.

| Tradition | Separation | Ordeal | Secrecy | Death/rebirth | Graded | Transmission to Masonry |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Eleusinian Mysteries | Procession, fasting | Night vigil | **Absolute** | Disputed | Lesser/Greater | **None** |
| Mithraic mysteries | Underground | Attested, scale disputed | Yes | Not clearly | **Seven grades** | **None** |
| Christian baptism | Catechumenate | Immersion | Originally restricted | **Explicit** | Catechumen → faithful | Convergent, not derived |
| Monastic profession | Novitiate | Years of testing | Limited | Death to the world, new name | Novice → professed | Cultural background |
| Sufi *bay'a* | Attachment to a shaykh | Discipline, *khalwa* | Partial | *Fana'* | Stations | **None** |
| **Medieval craft guild** | **Indenture** | **Term of years** | **Trade secrets** | **No** | **Apprentice → journeyman → master** | **DIRECT AND DOCUMENTED** |
| Masonic Craft | Preparation room | Hoodwink, cable-tow | Performative | Third degree, from 1730 | Three degrees | — |
| Military initiation | Basic training | Severe, sustained | Low | Symbolic in places | Rank | Independent convergence |

**One documented ancestor: the guild.** Apprentice, journeyman, master is the Masonic degree
sequence, and the route is not in doubt.

**Everything else is convergence.**

---

### What convergence is good for

The standard use of comparative material in this subject is genealogical and it is
worthless. Mithraism had seven grades; Masonry has degrees; therefore. The mysteries staged
death and rebirth; so does the third degree; therefore.

The preceding volume disposed of that reasoning and this book takes it as settled. What I
want to argue is that comparison is nonetheless indispensable, for a reason that has nothing
to do with descent.

**Comparison shows you which features of a tradition are forced and which are chosen.**

You cannot see that a practice is unusual until you have looked at others. Run the matrix and
three things become visible that could not be seen from inside Freemasonry alone.

**Grading is forced.** It appears in Mithraism, the guilds, the military, the professions,
Masonry and every occult order — and it appears because competence has to be taught in
sequence and because organizations need internal differentiation. The Neoplatonic cosmology
of Chapter 29 supplies a *justification* for grading; it does not supply the practice, which
arises wherever anyone teaches anything difficult.

**Death and rebirth is common but not universal.** It is explicit in baptism, in Masonry from
1730, in Sufi *fana'*. It is disputed at Eleusis and unclear in Mithraism. This matters
because the "universal death-and-rebirth initiation" is usually asserted on the authority of
Mircea Eliade, whose universalism the current scholarship does not support, and the matrix
shows the pattern is patchier than the claim.

**And secrecy varies enormously in kind** — which is the finding this chapter opened with and
which turns out to be the most useful thing in the table.

---

### Two kinds of secret

Eleusis kept an informational secret, absolutely, for two thousand years, and the mechanism
was religious dread in a society that took it seriously.

Masonry's secret was informational only in the thinnest sense and was published almost
immediately. What it kept was something else: the *experience*, which cannot be published,
and the *boundary*, which publication does not touch.

**These are not the same phenomenon and treating them as one has confused the entire
literature.**

And note the consequence, which is the reason this contrast earns a chapter.

The tradition that actually transmitted its ritual form across the modern world was the one
that could not keep its contents private. Eleusis kept faith and vanished. Masonry leaked
continuously and propagated to six continents, and was adopted by Rosicrucian orders,
magical orders, friendly societies, trade unions, women's orders, Black fraternal bodies and
the Theosophical movement.

**Leakage may have been the mechanism of transmission rather than a failure of it.** A form
that can be read about is a form that can be imitated, and the nineteenth-century bodies that
adopted Masonic ritual structure mostly learned it from print.

I offer that as a suggestion rather than a finding. But it inverts the usual assumption about
secrecy and survival, and the evidence points that way.

---

### Van Gennep and Turner, properly bounded

Now the theory, and the boundaries have to be drawn before it is used.

**Arnold van Gennep, 1909.** Rites of passage have three phases: separation, a marginal or
liminal phase, and incorporation. He derived this from a comparative survey and observed that
societies mark the middle phase physically — doorways, thresholds, spatial passage.

**Victor Turner, 1969.** The liminal phase elaborated. Persons in it are stripped of status,
levelled with respect to each other — he coined *communitas* for the resulting condition —
and characteristically unusually open to what the ritual supplies. They are, in his phrase,
betwixt and between.[^2]

Applied to Masonic initiation the fit is good. Separation in the preparation room. A liminal
phase in which the candidate is divested, blinded, roped, and neither outside nor in.
Incorporation with the apron, the place, the new form of address.

**And the fit is a fit, not a discovery.**

Van Gennep was generalizing from ethnographic reports that did not include eighteenth-century
England. Turner built his account on Ndembu material in central Africa. Neither had a London
tavern in mind. When their schema describes Masonic initiation well, what has been shown is
that a general pattern accommodates an instance — which is what general patterns are for, and
which is not evidence of a universal, and is certainly not evidence of any historical
relationship.

Catherine Bell's objection from Chapter 6 applies with full force here, and Ronald Grimes's
demand that rites be assessed in their particularity applies double. I use the vocabulary
because it is the best available and because inventing my own would be worse. I do not treat
it as a law.

---

### Where the schema fails

And it does fail, in a specific and instructive place.

**Van Gennep and Turner describe a passage. Masonic initiation is not a passage; it is the
first performance of a sequence the participant will repeat for decades.**

This was flagged in Chapter 6 and it belongs here, in the comparative chapter, because the
comparison makes it visible.

Consider the cases. Eleusinian initiation happened to a person once — twice, for those who
took the Greater Mysteries. Baptism happens once. Monastic profession happens once. Military
initiation happens once.

Masonic initiation happens once *to* the candidate and then perhaps a hundred times *around*
him, with him occupying every position in it in turn until he is the man in the east
conducting it.

**No rite-of-passage model accounts for that**, because in the ethnographic cases the model
was built on, the initiated do not become the officiants in a graded sequence over decades.
Some become elders; that is not the same as performing the identical script from every role.

If Masonic ritual does something durable to the people who undergo it — and Part XI will
examine whether it does — then the mechanism is more likely to lie in the thirty years of
repetition than in the one evening that the ritual-studies literature is equipped to
describe.

Chapter 47 makes that argument. I flag it here because the comparative frame is what
exposes the gap.

---

### The honest comparative result

Freemasonry is not descended from the mysteries, the Templars, the Rosicrucians or anything
else. Its documented ancestor is a building trade.

Its structure resembles a great many unrelated traditions, and the resemblances are
informative about the *problem* those traditions were solving rather than about any contact
between them.

It is distinctive in three respects. Its secret is performative rather than informational and
was published without damage. Its death-and-rebirth drama is unusually light on doctrine. And
its members repeat the ceremony from every position for the rest of their lives.

The first two make it hard to classify. The third makes it hard to study, because the thing
that probably matters most is the thing no model describes.

Which leaves the question of what a symbol actually *is* — how a piece of metal or a
geometric figure or a word comes to carry enough weight that people organize their lives
around it.
