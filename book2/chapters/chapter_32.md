# CHAPTER THIRTY-TWO
## The Magic of the Symbol

A square is a piece of metal bent at ninety degrees.

You can buy one. It costs very little. It has no properties beyond the geometrical, it does
nothing in the absence of a person using it, and if you leave it in a drawer for a century it
will be a piece of metal bent at ninety degrees when you take it out.

And men have organised their lives around it.

This chapter asks how that is possible — what has to be true about an object, and about the
people using it, for a hand tool to become something a person would decline to betray.

---

### Three kinds of meaning

The philosopher Charles Sanders Peirce distinguished three ways a sign can relate to what it
signifies, and the distinction is the most useful instrument available for this question.[^1]

**An icon** signifies by resemblance. A portrait; a map; a diagram of a triangle standing for
triangles.

**An index** signifies by actual connection. Smoke means fire because smoke is *caused by*
fire. A weathervane means wind because the wind is pushing it. The connection is real and
not conventional.

**A symbol** signifies by convention. The word *dog* means dog because English speakers
agree, and there is nothing dog-like about the sequence of letters.

Most discussion of Masonic symbolism assumes the third category and never examines the
others. That assumption is where the analysis goes wrong, because the Craft's most durable
symbols are not conventional at all.

---

### Why the working tools are different

Take the square as the Craft uses it: rectitude of conduct, the standard that does not
consult the person being measured.

Is that resemblance? Partly — there is a metaphor of straightness running through the
language. But the metaphor is not doing the work.

Is it convention? No. Nobody had to agree. The meaning is not arbitrary and could not have
been assigned differently.

**It is closer to an index, and that is the discovery.**

A square *actually tests* for a right angle. That is a fact about the instrument, not a
decision about it. And what the Masonic interpretation does is carry across the *structure*
of that fact — that there is a standard, that the standard is external, that it does not
negotiate, that the thing tested either passes or does not.

The meaning is anchored to a real property of a real object. You cannot make a square mean
mercy, or intuition, or the receptive principle, because the instrument does not do those
things.

**Anchored symbols are constrained. Unanchored symbols are not.**

That single distinction explains the entire pattern this book has documented across Part IV
and Part V. The square, the level, the plumb and the compasses — all anchored to
instrumental function — have stable meanings that barely changed across three centuries. The
Blazing Star, the point within a circle, the letter G and the All-Seeing Eye — unanchored —
accumulated seven readings apiece and a substantial speculative literature.

It also generates the rule I stated at the end of Chapter 22 and can now explain: **the
interpretive literature on a symbol grows in inverse proportion to how much the symbol
originally meant.** Anchoring supplies meaning and forecloses invention. Its absence invites
supply.

---

### Symbolic compression, properly defined

The term is usually used to mean that a symbol contains many meanings, which is an
observation rather than an explanation.

Here is what it actually consists of, using the square's eight layers from Chapter 14.

Compression is not a property an object possesses. It is a **historical deposit** produced by
three conditions.

**A concrete object with a function.** The anchor. Every layer of meaning the square carries
is a variation on *testing*: testing an angle, testing conduct, testing a man against a
standard. Six hundred years of use and the meanings never wandered from the instrument's
behaviour.

**Prolonged use by many people.** Three centuries, millions of members, each encountering the
object in a ceremony designed to make it memorable.

**An institution that declines to fix the meaning.** Anderson removed doctrinal authority in
1723 in order to admit men of different religions, and the consequence — very likely
unintended — was that the Craft could never afterwards adjudicate an interpretation.

Under those three conditions a symbol accumulates and cannot do otherwise. New readings are
added; old ones are almost never removed; and a modern Mason may hold four of the eight
layers simultaneously without confusion, because they are compatible variations on a single
anchored function.

**The compression is real and it is datable.** That is the finding. It is not a mystical
property of symbols in general; it is what happens to a particular kind of object under a
particular institutional regime, and the strata can be separated by ordinary historical work.

---

### The test: what fails to compress

A definition that explains why some symbols accumulate meaning is only useful if it also
predicts which ones will not, so it is worth applying to cases where compression demonstrably
failed.

**Symbols that arrived already explained.** The all-seeing eye entered Masonic use with its
meaning attached: divine observation of human conduct, stated plainly in the sources that
introduce it. It has accumulated almost nothing since. A reader encountering it in 1800 and
one encountering it in 1980 receive the same single reading, because the meaning was fixed at
the point of entry and there was nothing for a later generation to add.

**Symbols with no functional anchor.** The chequered pavement means mingled good and evil.
That is where it began and where it has stayed. It is a pattern rather than an instrument;
nothing can be done with it; and the reading it carries is a straightforward visual analogy
that offers no purchase for a second layer. Compare the square, which a man can pick up, test
an angle with, fail a test against, and discover in his own hands what the instruction means.

**Symbols that were adjudicated.** This is the instructive category, because it is where the
institutional condition shows its force. In the bodies that *did* fix interpretation —
notably the Scottish Rite after Pike, where a substantial authorised commentary exists —
symbols carry the meanings the commentary assigns them, and later accretion is markedly
thinner. An authority that answers the question closes it.

The three failures map exactly onto the three conditions, which is what a working definition
should do. Compression is not what symbols do; it is what happens to an object that is
concrete, usable, and left unexplained, in an institution that lasts a long time and declines
to rule.

**And this has a consequence for the esoteric reading.** If compression is a historical
deposit rather than a property of profound symbols, then the depth a modern Mason finds in
the square is not evidence that a profound meaning was placed there. It is evidence that
many people, over many years, were permitted to put something there — and the fact that no
authority ever removed any of it is a feature of Anderson's 1723 settlement, not of the
Hermetic tradition.

---

### The question of efficacy

Now the harder part, and the one where I have to be most careful about what kind of claim I
am making.

**Why does an anchored symbol, encountered in ritual conditions, acquire weight that the same
object does not have in a drawer?**

Four mechanisms are available and three of them are unobjectionable.

**Association.** The object was present at a significant event. This is ordinary memory, and
it is why people keep objects belonging to the dead.

**Cost.** Access to the object's meaning required something — waiting, payment, an ordeal, an
oath. Things obtained at cost are valued above their content, which is Chapter 10's territory
and carries Chapter 10's caution about how firmly that literature can be relied on.

**Social ratification.** Others treat the object as significant, continuously and visibly.
This is the best-evidenced mechanism in this book because it leaves records: certificates,
regalia, the arrangement on the pedestal, the examination of visitors.

**And completion by the recipient.** The candidate is given a square and a minimal
interpretation and supplies the rest himself, from his own life. What a person constructs is
held more firmly than what he is handed.

None of these requires anything supernatural, and I want to state clearly that I am not
excluding anything either. **A metaphysical claim about symbolic efficacy — that the symbol
participates in what it signifies, or that it channels something — is outside this book's
competence in both directions.** I neither assert nor deny it. What I can describe are
mechanisms that are sufficient to account for the observable effects, and I have described
them.

---

### What material religion adds

One more angle, because the recent scholarship has improved on the older treatment of this
question.

The study of material religion — David Morgan's work, and the broader turn associated with
the journal of that name — has argued against treating religious objects as vehicles for
ideas held elsewhere. On that older account, a symbol is a container: the meaning is in the
mind and the object merely refers to it.

The material-religion argument is that this gets the relation backwards. Objects are not
illustrations of beliefs; they are part of how belief is constituted, sustained and
transmitted. What people do with things — handle them, wear them, arrange them, look at them
— is not secondary to what they think.[^2]

Applied to Freemasonry the argument is unusually well supported, because the Craft's symbolic
system is almost entirely material. Tools you hold. An apron you wear. A board you look at. A
floor you walk. A grip you give.

**There is no Masonic creed. There are Masonic objects and Masonic actions**, and whatever
the Craft transmits is transmitted through them or not at all.

Which is either a profound observation about how meaning works, or simply a description of a
fraternity that could not have a creed because Anderson had forbidden one.

I suspect both are true, and that the second is why the first is so clearly visible here.

---

### The piece of metal

So: how does a hand tool become something a man will not betray?

It does not, by itself. A square in a drawer is a square in a drawer.

**What acquires the weight is not the object but the relation** — between a man, an evening
he will remember for fifty years, a promise made in his own voice, a room of people who
confirm it every month, and a piece of metal that sat on a book while he knelt.

The object is the anchor for all of that, and it is a good anchor precisely because it is
plain. You cannot forget what a square is for. You cannot lose it in interpretation. Every
time he sees one — on a building, on a gravestone, in a workshop — the whole structure is
available again in an instant.

That is not magic under any definition available in 1730. Whether it is magic under the
definition available after 1854 is Chapter 49's question, and the answer will depend on
things this chapter has not established.

What this chapter has established is that the effect requires no mysterious properties. It
requires an object with a function, an institution that refuses to define it, and a very long
time.
