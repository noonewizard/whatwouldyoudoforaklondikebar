# CHAPTER THIRTY-FOUR
## The Magic of Memory

The second Schaw Statute, signed at Holyrood House on 28 December 1599, requires that fellows
of craft be tested in "the art of memory and the science thereof," and that the warden
examine them on it.[^1]

It is the earliest reference in any Masonic document to a cognitive skill, it is four hundred
and twenty-seven years old, and scholars have been arguing about what it means since David
Stevenson drew attention to it in 1988.

The argument is worth rehearsing, because it is the clearest case in this book of a piece of
evidence that will support two readings and cannot decide between them.

---

### The two arts of memory

**The ordinary art.** A technique taught in schools across Europe since antiquity: to
remember a sequence of items, imagine a building you know well, place a vivid image for each
item at a specific point along a route through it, and then walk the route mentally. The
architecture holds the order; the images hold the content. It is described in the *Rhetorica
ad Herennium*, in Cicero and in Quintilian, and it was standard equipment for anyone who had
to speak at length without notes.

**The Renaissance elaboration.** By the sixteenth century the technique had grown enormously
ambitious. Giulio Camillo built a memory theater. Giordano Bruno proposed systems in which
the imagined building was the cosmos and the images were drawn from astrological and magical
correspondence, so that mastering the memory system would be mastering the structure of
reality. Frances Yates traced this development in *The Art of Memory* in 1966, and it is one
of the great works of twentieth-century intellectual history.[^2]

So: when a Scottish statute of 1599 requires masons to be tested in the art of memory, which
art is it?

**Stevenson argued for the second**, or at least for contact with it — that the Scottish
lodge system under William Schaw had absorbed Renaissance esoteric ideas, and that this is
the point at which something more than stonecutting enters the craft.

**I think it is probably the first**, and the preceding volume set out why. The surrounding
clauses of the statute are about apprenticeship terms and fines. The lodge's business was a
trade. And the thing a mason actually needed to hold in his head — the catechism, the
questions and answers by which he could be recognized — is precisely what the ordinary
mnemonic art existed to serve.

**Verdict: plausible, suggestive, unproved**, and it stays unproved because the statute does
not say and nothing else bears on it directly.

---

### What is not in doubt

Set the dispute aside, because underneath it is something more important that nobody
contests.

**In 1599, a Scottish mason's competence was tested on what he could hold in his head.**

Whatever art was meant, the statute establishes that memory was a *qualification* — an
examinable skill on which a man's standing in the craft depended.

And that requirement has never gone away. Masonic ritual is learned by heart, in most
jurisdictions, to this day. A man progressing through the offices of a lodge commits
thousands of words to memory, learns them aurally from another man, and delivers them
standing up in front of people who know when he is wrong.

Four hundred and twenty-seven years of an institution testing its members on recall.

---

### Why this is the durable design decision

Chapter 7 argued that memorized transmission makes the Craft's knowledge constitutively
active — that you cannot be a lapsed holder of it the way you can be a lapsed owner of a
creed. This chapter presses the point further, because the consequences are larger than I
first saw.

**A tradition stored in books requires books.** Which means it requires literacy, money,
printing, transport, storage and the absence of confiscation. Every one of those is a point
of failure and every one of them failed somewhere in this history.

**A tradition stored in bodies requires people.**

Compare the survival records. Masonic ritual crossed to America, India, Africa, the Caribbean
and the Pacific in the heads of soldiers and merchants, at no cost, with no baggage, and was
reconstituted on arrival. It survived prohibition in several twentieth-century states that
seized every document they could find. It was carried into prisoner-of-war camps and worked
there.

Documents can be taken. What is in a man cannot be, and lodges have reconstituted themselves
after decades of suppression out of the memories of survivors.

**That is the single most consequential property of the Masonic apparatus**, and it is a
property of the storage medium rather than of the content.

---

### What kind of memory

The cognitive point, stated at the strength the evidence supports.

Psychologists distinguish between declarative memory — facts and events, available to
conscious recall and to verbal report — and procedural memory, which is the memory of how to
do things and which is characteristically more durable and less accessible to introspection.
You can lose the ability to say what you know long before you lose the ability to perform it.

Masonic ritual is encoded substantially in the second register. It is not a body of facts; it
is a *performance* — words in a fixed order, delivered in a particular rhythm, accompanied by
movements, in a spatial arrangement, with another person supplying the alternate half.

Four features make it exceptionally retrievable.

**It is spatial.** The ceremony happens in a room with fixed positions, and the candidate
learned the room by walking it. This is the art of memory operating whether or not anyone
intended it: the architecture holds the sequence.

**It is rhythmic and formulaic.** Fixed phrases, parallel constructions, set pairs. This is
the machinery of every oral tradition that has ever transmitted material across centuries.

**It is dialogic.** The catechism is questions and answers, so retrieval is cued rather than
free — and cued recall is dramatically better than unprompted recall.

**And it is rehearsed constantly.** Not once, but at every meeting, for decades, from
progressively senior positions.

---

### The thirty-year rehearsal

Which brings this part of the book to the point it has been circling since Chapter 6.

The ritual-studies literature describes initiation as an event. The cognitive literature on
high-arousal ritual — Whitehouse's imagistic mode — describes vivid episodic memories of rare
and intense ceremonies.[^3]

Both are about the evening.

**The evening is not where the memory is.**

A Mason of thirty years' standing has been through that ceremony perhaps a hundred times, in
every role, from every position in the room. He has heard the words hundreds of times. He has
spoken most of them. He has watched the same moment of restored light happen to a hundred
different men, from the east, from the west, from the door.

Whatever is in his memory is not a recollection of one night in 1740. It is something much
closer to a skill — a body of material so thoroughly rehearsed, in so many modalities, that
it is available without effort.

And nobody has studied it, because the models available describe initiations and this is not
an initiation. It is an apprenticeship that never ends.

Chapter 47 takes this up as the central claim of Part XI. Here the point is narrower and
concerns evidence: **if we want to know what Masonic ritual did to people, the evening is the
wrong unit of analysis, and almost the entire literature — Masonic, occult and academic —
has used it.**

---

### The thing that was stored

One last observation, which is not quite an argument.

What the Craft actually preserved by all this — the memorization, the examination, the
four-century insistence on recall — is a catechism about a building that does not exist, a
legend about a man who did not die, and a set of moral interpretations of carpenter's tools.

Considered as information, it is not much. It would fit in a pamphlet, and did, in 1730, for
sixpence.

**What was stored was not the information. It was the capacity to perform it**, in company,
on demand, correctly — which is a different kind of possession and the only kind that
constitutes membership.

A tradition that stores facts can be robbed. A tradition that stores a skill can only be
forgotten, and it is forgotten one person at a time, and every meeting is a rehearsal
against that.
