# PART IX — THE SECRET

# CHAPTER THIRTY-SIX
## What Is Actually Hidden?

In April 1723 a London newspaper printed the Masonic catechism.

*A Mason's Examination* appeared in *The Flying Post*, available to anyone who bought the
paper. The following year a fuller version appeared as a pamphlet. In 1730 Samuel Prichard
published *Masonry Dissected*, which gave the catechisms of all three degrees and the whole
of the Hiramic legend, swore an affidavit before a magistrate that it was accurate, and sold
three editions in a fortnight. In 1742 the Abbé Pérau did the same in French. *Jachin and
Boaz*, in 1762, was still being reprinted in the 1790s.[^1]

Everything was public within seven years of the Grand Lodge's foundation and stayed public.

And Freemasonry grew continuously for the next two centuries.

Any theory of Masonic secrecy has to begin from that fact, and most of them do not.

---

### Four kinds of secret

The word covers at least four distinct things and the failure to separate them has produced
three centuries of argument at cross-purposes.

**Informational secrecy.** A fact concealed because knowing it confers advantage. A
combination, a troop movement, a source. **Destroyed permanently by publication.**

**Functional secrecy.** A fact concealed because the concealment performs a job. The original
Masonic word and grip are of this kind: a man arriving at a strange building site or a strange
town needed to demonstrate that he was what he claimed, and a private token did that.
**Damaged by publication in proportion to how many impostors read the pamphlet** — which, in
the event, was not many, because the impersonation had to survive a live examination by
someone who knew the answers.

**Performative secrecy.** The concealment is not protecting information; it is *doing
something* to the participants. A man told he will learn something, made to wait, made to
swear, and then told it, has had an experience. **Not damaged by publication at all**, because
reading a description is not the same event as undergoing the thing described.

**Boundary secrecy.** Georg Simmel's contribution, in an essay of 1906 that remains the best
short treatment of the subject: the secret society's secret is frequently *the fact of having
one*. The content matters less than the line it draws. A group with a boundary has an inside,
and the people inside are bound to each other by their shared position relative to the
line.[^2]

**Prichard destroyed the first kind and dented the second. He could not touch the third or
fourth, and those were carrying the weight.**

---

### What was in the pamphlet

Worth being concrete, because the modern reader's expectation is calibrated by three
centuries of speculation.

The contents of *Masonry Dissected* are: a set of questions and answers about the lodge, its
form, its officers and furnishings; the words and grips of three degrees; the signs; and the
narrative of the master's murder and raising.

That is the whole of it. No doctrine. No cosmology. No theology beyond the ordinary piety of
the period. Nothing that a modern reader would call esoteric, and — as Part VII established —
no correspondences, no planetary material, no angelic names, no Kabbalah.

A man who read it for sixpence in 1730 knew everything a Master Mason knew, in the
informational sense.

**And a substantial number of men subsequently paid a fee, waited weeks, and underwent the
ceremony anyway.**

That is the experiment, and it ran for two hundred years with a very large sample.

---

### The catechism as procedure

Chapter 9 gave the mechanism and it belongs here in its proper place.

**The Masonic secret is not a fact. It is a two-person procedure.**

A catechism is half questions. A man alone who has memorized the whole thing possesses the
material and cannot perform it, in the way that a person who knows both parts of a duet still
cannot sing a duet. To produce the knowledge, two people must agree to produce it together,
each holding up his half, in the right order.

You cannot steal a procedure by printing it. You can describe a handshake in complete detail
and the description will not shake anyone's hand.

Which means that the exposures, for all their accuracy, did not transfer the thing they were
exposing. They transferred a *description* of it, to readers who had nobody to perform it
with, and the gap between those is the whole of what the Craft was actually keeping.

---

### Secrecy that advertises

Here is the feature that most embarrasses the conspiratorial reading and that the Craft has
never bothered to explain, because from inside it does not look like a contradiction.

**Freemasonry has always advertised.**

It printed its constitutions. It published lists of lodges. It put its emblem on the front of
its buildings — buildings which, from the nineteenth century, were among the largest in the
towns that held them. It processed in the streets in full regalia. It laid the corner stones
of public buildings in public, with the governor watching and the newspapers reporting. It
buried its members in uniform, along the high street, in front of everyone.

An organization conducting a covert operation does not do these things.

And this is not a defense of Freemasonry so much as a description of what kind of object it
is. **The Craft was never hiding its existence, its membership, its premises, its officers,
its finances or its purposes.** All of that was public and much of it was ostentatious. What
it withheld was an hour's ceremony and a set of recognition tokens — and it withheld those
badly, from 1723 onward, without consequence.

---

### The function of a thin secret

So why keep it at all?

Because a thin secret does everything a thick one would, at lower cost.

**It draws the boundary.** Simmel's point. The line is made by the act of crossing, and a
trivial content marks a line as well as a profound one.

**It creates the occasion.** You cannot have a ceremony of revelation without something to
reveal. The content's slightness is, as Chapter 20 argued, an advantage — an extravagant
revelation would invite evaluation, and a modest one invites the recipient to supply the
significance.

**It generates the value.** Anything withheld acquires worth in proportion to the effort of
obtaining it, and a man who has waited, paid and sworn will not readily conclude that what he
received was trivial. Chapter 10 attached a caution to this mechanism and the caution stands.

**And it costs nothing to maintain.** A genuinely valuable secret requires security: vetting,
compartmentalisation, sanctions that are actually applied. The Craft's secret required none
of this, which is why publication did not damage it and why the penal clauses could remain
theatrical for three centuries without anyone noticing that they were never enforced.

---

### The counter-case: where the thin secret fails

The argument of this chapter is that Masonic secrecy works precisely because the withheld
content is slight, and the argument is strong enough that it should be tested where it
predicts failure.

If a thin secret is a functional secret, then organizations whose secrets are *thick* — where
real, consequential, checkable information is withheld — should behave differently, and they
do.

**A thick secret can be destroyed.** When the substance is genuine information, disclosure
ends it. Intelligence services, commercial laboratories and criminal conspiracies all
organize around this fact, and their security practices are correspondingly expensive:
compartmentalisation, vetting, surveillance of members, sanctions that are real rather than
symbolic.

**Masonry does none of this**, and has never needed to. Its penalties are ceremonial and were
recognized as such by the courts and by the members. Its membership is, in most jurisdictions
and most periods, a matter of public record — regalia is worn in processions, lodge buildings
are signposted, obituaries name membership. An organization with something to lose by
disclosure does not advertise its membership on gravestones.

**And the historical test has been run.** Masonic ritual was published in 1730, repeatedly
thereafter, and is now available instantly and free to anyone. If the secret were
informational, the institution should have collapsed three centuries ago. It did not, and
neither did the practice of secrecy within it, which continued unchanged through every wave
of exposure.

The contrast makes the point sharper than the positive argument can. It is not that Masonry
keeps an unimportant secret badly. It is that what Masonry does is not the same operation as
keeping information from people, and it has never been vulnerable to the thing that destroys
information-keeping, because it was never doing it.

---

### The asymmetry

One observation to close, and it is the finding of this chapter.

There is a great deal of genuine concealment in the history this book and its predecessor
cover. Almost none of it is Masonic.

**What was actually hidden was this:** that William Wynn Westcott wrote the letters from the
German adept who did not exist. That Albert Pike took his Kabbalah from a Frenchman he did
not name. That Baron von Hund had no warrant from the superiors he claimed for thirty years.
That Diana Vaughan was an invention and her creator knew it. That the Cipher Manuscripts
were compiled in the nineteenth century. That the tarot-Kabbalah system was assembled in the
1850s.

Every one of those concealments was practiced by somebody claiming *to reveal* Masonic
secrets, or claiming an authority *derived* from Masonic structures.

And every one was eventually exposed by ordinary documentary research — by people asking
where the letter was, what the date was, who had held the manuscript.

**The organization accused of hiding everything hid very little, and did it badly. The people
accusing it, and the people claiming to inherit from it, hid a great deal, and did it well.**

That asymmetry is the most useful thing a reader can take from this book into any other
subject, and the next chapter is about why it recurs so reliably.
