# CHAPTER THIRTY-SEVEN
## Knowledge and Power

Baron Karl Gotthelf von Hund spent thirty years waiting for a letter that never came.

He had been received, he said, into a higher Masonic order in Paris in the early 1740s, in
the presence of persons of great rank, and told that this order was the continuation of the
Knights Templar, preserved in secret since the suppression of 1312. He was given authority
over a German province. He was told that Unknown Superiors directed the order and that he
would be contacted.

He built the Rite of Strict Observance around the claim. It became the dominant form of
high-degree Masonry in the German lands. Princes joined. He spent his own fortune on it.

Nobody ever contacted him, and by every account including hostile ones he believed the story
until he died in 1776. In 1782, at a convention at Wilhelmsbad, the assembled leadership of
European Masonry examined the Templar claim and set it aside for want of evidence.[^1]

The preceding volume told that story as a case of manufactured antiquity. This chapter tells
it as a case of something else, because the same sequence has happened so many times, in so
many organisations, that it is better understood as a *structural property* than as a series
of individual frauds.

---

### The life cycle

Every graded system in this book has followed the same six phases. I set them out as a
sequence because the regularity is the point.

**Phase one. Foundation.** A body is constituted with graded access to withheld material.
Authority flows downward from whoever holds the higher grades. This works, and works well,
and may work for decades.

**Phase two. Ascent.** The organisation grows. Members advance. And some of them reach the
top — where they discover that above the highest grade there is nothing except the people
who issued it.

**Phase three. The question.** *By what authority?* It is always asked from below, and it is
always asked by the most committed members rather than the least, because they are the ones
who got far enough to notice.

**Phase four. The appeal upward.** The leadership answers by pointing above itself. Unknown
Superiors. A German adept. Mahatmas in Tibet. Secret Chiefs.

**Phase five. The demand.** Produce them.

**Phase six. Schism.**

Von Hund: phases one to six, 1750s to 1782. The Golden Dawn: 1888 to 1900, when Mathers,
cornered by the London members, told them that the founding correspondence had been forged by
Westcott — which destroyed the Order's warrant at a stroke and was, incidentally, true.
Blavatsky's Society fought the same battle repeatedly and survived it only by being larger
and more diffuse.

**The manufactured superior is not a moral failing peculiar to charlatans. It is a structural
requirement of graded secrecy, and the demand for it arises from below.**

---

### Why the structure requires it

The logic is worth spelling out, because it explains something that otherwise looks like
mere dishonesty.

A graded system's authority is derived from the grades. The man in the third degree can
instruct the man in the second because he holds something the second does not.

Follow that upward and it terminates. Somebody holds the highest grade, and his authority
cannot come from a higher one, because there isn't a higher one.

**So the ladder must either have a top that is visible — in which case the top is just some
men, and everybody can see it — or a top that is concealed, in which case it cannot be
examined.**

Freemasonry, remarkably, chose the first. A Grand Master is elected, is a known person, holds
office for a term, and claims no supernatural warrant. The Craft's authority is admitted to
be institutional and human.

Every esoteric order built on the Masonic template chose the second, and every one of them
broke on it.

---

### What restriction actually buys

Set aside fraud and ask what a graded system gives an institution that an open one does not.

**Pacing.** Material released in sequence is material the recipient is ready for. This is the
defensible version and it is the one every apprenticeship, curriculum and professional
training in history has relied on. You do not hand a first-year student the final-year
syllabus.

**Retention.** A member who has received everything has no further reason to attend. A member
who has received two-thirds has a reason next month.

**Differentiation.** An organisation needs internal structure, and grades supply it without
requiring anybody to assess merit, which is contentious. Grades can be conferred on time
served.

**And authority without coercion.** If the route to what is withheld runs through a person,
that person has power requiring no enforcement at all.

The first is legitimate and the last is the one that goes wrong.

---

### The Craft's peculiar modesty

Something should be said here that cuts against the grain of this chapter, because the
evidence supports it.

Freemasonry is the case in this history that did *not* manufacture a superior.

It has three degrees and then it stops. Its highest officer is elected annually. Its founding
document is a printed book with an author's name on it. When it needed antiquity it invented
a *history* — Anderson's craft legend, and the preceding volume was unsparing about it — but
it never invented a living authority, and it never told its members that hidden masters were
directing them.

More than that: at the summit of the Craft, the third degree *tells the candidate that the
essential thing is lost.* Chapter 24 argued that this is the system's central design
decision. Read alongside this chapter, it looks like something more specific.

**It is a structural refusal to claim what the institution did not have.**

Every order that came after filled that space. Von Hund filled it with Unknown Superiors,
Blavatsky with Masters, Westcott with Sprengel, Mathers with Secret Chiefs. The Craft left it
empty and said so.

The structure is identical. The honesty is not.

---

### Knowledge that cannot be checked

One final mechanism, and it is the one that makes this whole subject epistemically
treacherous.

A system in which knowledge is released by grade has a property that ordinary institutions do
not: **the members cannot audit it.**

A man in the second degree who suspects that there is nothing in the third cannot establish
it. He is told that his doubt is a symptom of his position — that things look different from
above, that he is not yet ready, that he will understand. And that reply is *always
available*, and it is indistinguishable from the true one.

This is the same structure as the unfalsifiable secret and it will be examined properly in
Chapter 39, where it becomes a philosophical problem rather than an institutional one.

Here I want to note only its practical consequence, which is that graded organisations are
extremely difficult to reform from within and extremely easy to hollow out. The mechanism
that protects a genuine pedagogy from impatience protects an empty one from scrutiny, and
from inside they are the same.

The only reliable test is the one von Hund was asked and could not answer, and it is the same
test this book has been applying since Chapter 5.

**Produce the document.**

Which is a good rule for organisations. It is a much harder rule to apply to a claim about a
pattern — where there is no document, no superior and no organisation, only an arrangement of
things that somebody says was put there on purpose.
