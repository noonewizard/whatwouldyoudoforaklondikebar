# CHAPTER THIRTY-EIGHT
## Pattern and Design

**LEVEL = 56. LIGHT = 56.**

Assign each letter its position in the alphabet — A is 1, B is 2, and so on — and add. The
Masonic working tool and the thing the candidate asks for at the climax of his initiation
produce the same number.

That is a real result. I computed it and you can check it in a minute with a pencil. And it
is exactly the kind of thing on which an enormous literature has been built.

Before we decide what it means, one more calculation.

**KETTLE = 73. SAUSAGE = 73.**

---

### The experiment

Here is what I did, and I am setting it out in full because the method is the point of the
chapter.

I took twenty words central to Masonic symbolism: Boaz, Jachin, Hiram, Solomon, Tubalcain,
acacia, mason, lodge, temple, square, compass, apron, gavel, plumb, level, ashlar, widow,
Tyre, pillar, light.

I took twenty control words with no symbolic content whatever. I chose them to be as
unpromising as possible — household objects and root vegetables: bus, potato, Tesco, carpet,
dustbin, spanner, trousers, omelette, hamster, kettle, parsnip, linoleum, badger, sausage,
turnip, pylon, gutter, biscuit, puddle, drainpipe.

I applied the same procedure to both sets: simple English gematria, A=1 through Z=26.

Then I counted how many pairs within each set share a value.

**Masonic set: three pairs.** APRON = PLUMB = 64. LEVEL = LIGHT = 56. TYRE = PILLAR = 68.

**Control set: three pairs.** TESCO = PUDDLE = 62. KETTLE = SAUSAGE = 73. POTATO = SPANNER =
87.

Identical yield.

I also checked whether either set produced any of the numbers conventionally treated as
significant — 3, 5, 7, 33, 47, 72, 93.

**Masonic set: one hit.** GAVEL = 47, which is the number of the Proposition of Euclid that
Anderson placed at the foundation of all Masonry in 1723.

**Control set: one hit.** PARSNIP = 93, which is the central number of Crowley's Thelemic
system.

---

### What that demonstrates

Nothing about Freemasonry. Everything about method.

**The procedure produces the same density of striking coincidences from root vegetables as
from sacred symbolism**, because the procedure is not detecting anything. It is generating.
Twenty words mapped onto a range of a hundred-odd integers will produce collisions at a
predictable rate, and the words' meanings make no difference to the arithmetic.

LEVEL = LIGHT is not evidence that the Craft encoded a relation between them. It is evidence
that both words have five letters drawn from the middle of the alphabet.

And GAVEL = 47 is not a signature. It is one hit in twenty tries against a list of eight
numbers, which is roughly what chance predicts — as PARSNIP = 93 confirms, since nobody
believes the parsnip is Thelemic.

If this chapter achieves one thing, let it be this: **the striking result and the control
result are the same result, and only one of them gets written up.**

---

### The four questions

From this follows the apparatus I promised in Chapter 5. Six questions handle claims that
something old was *transmitted*. Four more handle claims that a hidden pattern has been
*discovered*.

**7. Was the procedure specified in advance, or fitted to the result?**

This is the decisive one and almost every published claim fails it.

Notice how many procedures were available to me. A=1 or A=0? Include or exclude vowels?
Reverse the alphabet? Sum the letters, or multiply, or take the product of the position
values? Use Hebrew values for transliterated words? Count only consonants? Use the English
spelling or the biblical one — HIRAM or CHIRAM or HURAM?

Each choice is defensible. Each produces different numbers. **A researcher who tries several
and reports the one that worked has not found a pattern; he has selected one**, and the
selection is invisible in the writeup because failed attempts are not published.

**8. What is the search space?**

How many things were available to try? Twenty words against eight significant numbers is a
hundred and sixty opportunities, and one hit is unremarkable. A large body of ritual text
against the whole of Hebrew scripture and the whole of Masonic vocabulary is a search space
in the millions, and in a space that size *something* will always look designed.

This is the multiple comparisons problem and it is the single most common error in this
entire literature.

**9. Does it survive a control?**

Run the identical procedure on matched material that cannot contain the message. That is what
the parsnips were for. If the control yields comparably, the finding is noise.

**10. Does the claim make a prediction that could fail?**

A pattern that accommodates any outcome explains none. If the scheme can absorb whatever the
evidence turns out to be, it is not a finding about the evidence.

---

### The model case

The four questions are not my invention and the demonstration above is a small imitation of a
famous one.

In 1997 Michael Drosnin published *The Bible Code*, claiming that equidistant letter
sequences in the Hebrew text of Genesis encoded modern events including assassinations.

In 1999, Brendan McKay, Dror Bar-Natan, Maya Bar-Hillel and Gil Kalai published a paper in
*Statistical Science* running the control. They applied the same method to other texts. McKay
found assassination "predictions" for Indira Gandhi, Yitzhak Rabin, both Kennedys, Martin
Luther King, Abraham Lincoln and Leon Trotsky — in the English text of *Moby-Dick*.[^1]

The method works. That is precisely what is wrong with it.

### A worked demolition: sacred geometry in a blank grid

The gematria test was arithmetic. Here is the same lesson in two dimensions, because the
geometric version of this error is commoner than the numerical one and harder to see through.

The procedure for discovering sacred geometry in a building goes like this. Obtain a plan.
Identify significant points — corners, column centres, door thresholds, altar position. Draw
lines between some of them. Observe that certain of the lines meet at angles close to
seventy-two degrees, or that certain distances stand close to the golden ratio, or that a
pentagram can be fitted so that its vertices fall near five of the points.

Every step in that procedure is a decision, and none of the decisions is constrained.

**Which points count as significant?** A medieval church offers hundreds — every pier, every
respond, every boss, the corners of every bay, the centre of every window. A modern
supermarket car park offers just as many: light standards, drainage gullies, the corners of
the painted bays, the entrance gates.

**How close is close enough?** Seventy-two degrees, or anything between sixty-nine and
seventy-five? The golden ratio is 1.618; is 1.61 a hit, or 1.65, or 1.58? The literature
almost never says, and when the tolerance is not stated in advance it is set, unconsciously,
by whether the answer came out.

**How many attempts were made?** A pentagram has five vertices and can be rotated through
three hundred and sixty degrees, scaled freely, and placed anywhere on the plan. The number
of positions available is effectively unlimited. Finding one that lands five vertices near
five of several hundred candidate points is not a discovery; it is a certainty.

So I ran it, on a grid that certainly contains no sacred geometry.

I generated a plain rectangular lattice — twelve by eight, ninety-six points, the spacing of
an ordinary street grid or a car park, with a small random jitter added to each point so that
it resembles a surveyed plan rather than a perfect abstraction. Nothing was designed into it.
It is a grid.

I then searched for a regular pentagram whose five vertices fall on lattice points. Four
hundred thousand random placements of centre, radius and rotation were tried, and the best
was refined by local optimisation.

**Result: a pentagram whose five vertices land on five lattice points with a maximum
deviation of 0.20 grid units — 5.7 per cent of its own circumradius.**[^4a]

On a drawing at any normal scale, that is a pentagram. The line weight alone would swallow
most of the error. Reproduced as a figure with the five points marked and the other
ninety-one omitted — which is how such figures are always reproduced — it would look like a
finding.

It is a grid.

The point is not that no building has designed proportions. Buildings certainly do, and where
a designer left drawings or a specification we can say so with confidence. The point is that
the method of *retrospective fitting* cannot distinguish a building with designed proportions
from one without, because it returns a positive result either way. A test that cannot come
back negative is not a test.

### How to teach yourself the false positive

The uncomfortable part of this chapter is that knowing all of the above does not immunise
anyone, including its author. The pull of a striking coincidence is not defeated by
understanding it; it is defeated only by procedure.

So here is the procedure, stated as a practice rather than a principle.

**Write the method down before you run it**, in enough detail that somebody else could
execute it without asking you a question. The moment you find yourself adding a clause to
accommodate a result, you have stopped testing and started fitting.

**Count the attempts, including the ones you abandoned.** A researcher who tried HIRAM,
CHIRAM and HURAM, and reports only the spelling that worked, has made three attempts and
reported one. Keep a log. The log will be embarrassing, which is what it is for.

**Build the control first.** Not afterwards, when the exciting result is already in hand and
the control has become an obstacle to it. Choose the control corpus before you know what the
real corpus will yield. I chose the root vegetables before computing a single value, and I
recommend the practice precisely because I did not enjoy the outcome.

**Say in advance what would make you drop the claim.** If nothing would, the claim is not
about the world.

None of this is sophisticated, and none of it requires statistical training. It requires only
the willingness to run the procedure that might take the discovery away — which is the
single thing the literature under examination in this chapter never does.

### What is actually real

Having run the demolition, the other half of the job — because a chapter that only debunks
teaches nothing, and some patterns genuinely are designed.

**The Masonic cipher is real.** The substitution system usually called the pigpen — letters
placed in a grid and represented by the shape of the cell enclosing them — was genuinely used
by Freemasons in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, for records, certificates and
correspondence.[^2]

It is also trivially breakable, was never intended to be otherwise, and carries no doctrine.
It is a filing convenience, of the same order as shorthand. It exists to keep a minute book
from being read by a curious servant, not to conceal a teaching from posterity.

**Atbash is real.** First letter for last, second for second-last. It is attested in the
Hebrew Bible itself — Sheshach for Babel at Jeremiah 25:26 — and is a genuine technique of
Jewish exegesis.

**Gematria, notarikon and temurah are real**, with centuries of learned practice behind them,
operating within a tradition that had a canon, a community and rabbinic judgement to
constrain results.

**And there is no evidence whatever that any of these carried Masonic doctrine.** The Craft's
secrecy was oral and performative, not cryptographic. The exposures show plain language. A
tradition that could not keep its catechism out of the newspapers for seven years was not
running an encryption programme.

**The rule: the reality of a technique is not evidence for any particular result obtained
with it.** Hammers exist. That does not make every hole a hammer-blow.

---

### Why we are so good at this

I want to end without contempt, because contempt would be both unkind and wrong.

The tendency to find structure in noise has a name — apophenia — and it is not a defect. It is
the ordinary operation of a perceptual system shaped by an environment in which missing a
real pattern was far more expensive than seeing a false one. A rustle that is nothing costs a
startle; a rustle that is something and is missed costs everything.

We are machines for detecting structure, and we detect it whether or not it is there.

**So the person who discovers a hidden geometry in a floor plan, or a name in the initials of
the degrees, or a pentagram in a street grid, is not stupid and is usually not dishonest.**
They are doing what human perception does, enthusiastically, on material that rewards it. I
felt the pull myself when LEVEL came out equal to LIGHT, and I had written the control into
the experiment specifically because I knew I would.

The four questions are not a way of calling such people fools. They are the only method
anyone — including the person doing the discovering — has for telling the two cases apart.

And the two cases do exist. Anderson really did put Euclid's forty-seventh proposition at the
foundation of Masonry in 1723. That is a designed pattern, and we know because he wrote it
down, in a book, with his name on it, which is what designed patterns leave behind.

**The question is never whether there is a pattern. Patterns are free. The question is always
whether somebody put it there — and the way to find out has not changed since Chapter 5.**

Produce the document.
