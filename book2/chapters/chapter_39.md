# CHAPTER THIRTY-NINE
## The Problem of Initiation

Every tradition in this book makes the same claim, and it is the claim I have been circling
since the first chapter.

**Something is conveyed by undergoing the ritual that cannot be conveyed by reading about
it.**

Iamblichus made a version of it in the fourth century: the soul cannot think its way home,
and what raises it is ritual action performed with material tokens. Masonic writers have made
it continuously since the eighteenth century, usually in response to the exposures — yes, the
words are printed, and no, you have not been initiated. The Golden Dawn made it. Crowley made
it.

It is also the perfect defence of an empty secret, and that is the problem.

---

### The case for

Start with the strongest version, because it is genuinely strong and is not a mystical claim
at all.

**Michael Polanyi, 1966: we know more than we can tell.**

Polanyi's argument is that a great deal of human competence is *tacit* — real, reliable,
demonstrable on demand, and not reducible to propositions. His examples are ordinary. A
person who can ride a bicycle cannot state the rule they follow. A doctor who can recognise a
condition from across a room often cannot say what they saw. A wine-taster, a joiner, a
radiographer, a mother who knows her child is ill before any symptom is nameable.

This knowledge is transmitted, and it is transmitted by apprenticeship: by working alongside
someone who has it, doing the thing, having errors corrected, until the competence is in
you.[^1]

**Nothing in that is supernatural, and it is not in serious dispute.**

Apply it to ritual and the claim becomes tractable. A man who reads a description of an
initiation acquires propositions about it. A man who undergoes it acquires — on this account —
something in a different register: a procedural memory, a set of bodily associations, a dated
episode, a relationship with the people who conducted it.

Chapter 7 documented exactly this, and Chapter 34 showed the storage medium. The Masonic
catechism is held the way a skill is held. It can be performed and it decays without use.

**So the claim's minimal version is simply true: reading about a ceremony does not give you
what performing it gives you.**

---

### The case against

And yet.

Notice the shape of the claim once it is generalised. *There is a content that exists, that
cannot be stated, and that is available only to those who have undergone the procedure.*

**Nothing about that structure requires there to be any content.**

An institution with a genuine tacit competence and an institution with nothing whatever make
identical noises. Both say that the outsider cannot understand. Both say that description is
inadequate. Both say that the doubter's doubt is evidence of his position rather than of the
claim. And from outside — and, crucially, from inside — the two are not distinguishable.

The preceding chapter's whole argument applies here. A claim that cannot fail is not a
finding.

And the history in these two volumes is largely a record of the structure being operated
without content. Von Hund's superiors. Westcott's German adept. Mathers's Chiefs. In every
case the unstatable knowledge was defended exactly as Polanyi would defend a genuine tacit
skill, and in every case there was nothing there.

---

### How to tell them apart

I do not think this is hopeless, and the way out is to stop asking about the content and ask
about the *competence*.

**Genuine tacit knowledge has a public face: it produces performances that can be judged.**

The joiner's tacit skill cannot be stated, but the joint either holds or it does not. The
doctor's recognition cannot be articulated, but the diagnosis is right or wrong. The
wine-taster is tested blind. In every real case of tacit knowledge, the inability to *state*
the knowledge coexists with an ability to *demonstrate* it, and the demonstration is
assessable by people who do not have it.

**So: what can a Master Mason do that a man who has read Prichard cannot?**

Two answers are available and their status differs sharply.

**He can perform the ritual.** This is real, assessable and exactly analogous to a craft
skill. He can be examined; he can deliver the ceremony; he can be judged by people who know.
Chapter 34 argued that this is the Craft's actual transmitted competence, and it passes
Polanyi's test completely.

**He has been changed as a person.** This is the claim the Craft makes for itself, it is the
one this book exists to examine, and **it has no public face at all.** There is no
performance to assess, no criterion, no examination, no way of distinguishing a man in whom
the ceremony took from a man in whom it did not.

---

### The honest position

Which leaves the question genuinely open, and I want to state the openness precisely rather
than resolve it rhetorically.

**On the transmission of ritual competence: the claim is true and unmysterious.** Masonic
knowledge is tacit in Polanyi's exact sense, is transmitted by apprenticeship, and cannot be
acquired from print. The exposures proved this by experiment: everything was published and
nothing was transferred.

**On the transmission of anything else: undecided, and possibly undecidable.** There may be
something further. There may not. The structure of the claim is such that its truth and its
falsity produce the same evidence, and no amount of testimony resolves it, because testimony
from initiates is exactly what both cases predict.

I have gone back and forth on this for a long time and I have not found a way through. It is
listed among the book's unresolved questions and it will still be there at the end.

---

### Why it matters beyond Freemasonry

One closing observation, because this problem is not confined to lodges.

The structure recurs wherever expertise is claimed and outcomes are hard to measure. In
psychotherapy. In parts of medicine. In connoisseurship. In management consultancy. In
literary criticism, including this book. Every field in which practitioners claim a
competence that cannot be fully articulated faces the same difficulty: the claim is
sometimes entirely true, it is sometimes entirely empty, and the two are hard to tell apart
from the inside.

**Freemasonry is not a peculiar case. It is an unusually clean one** — because the content
was published in 1730, which lets us see exactly what publication does and does not
transfer.

And the answer there was clear. Publication transferred the words and not the ability to use
them, and not the evening, and not the relationship with the men in the room.

Whether it also failed to transfer something else is the question. I cannot answer it, and I
have come to think that the honest response to a question of this shape is not to answer it
but to say precisely why it resists.

Which is what a historian can do, and where a historian's competence ends — and it ends
exactly here, at the door of the one part of this subject that is neither documentary nor
measurable.

The next part goes to the people who claimed to have walked through that door, and says what
the evidence shows about them.
