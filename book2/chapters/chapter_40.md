# PART X — THE OCCULT MIRROR

# CHAPTER FORTY
## Pike and Lévi

In 1854 a Frenchman who had spent his twenties writing socialist pamphlets and some of his
thirties in prison for them published the first volume of a book about magic.

In 1871 an American lawyer, poet and former Confederate general published a commentary on the
degrees of the Scottish Rite which was given to every initiate in the southern United States
for the following century.

The preceding volume established the relationship between those two books, and I will state
it once. Arturo de Hoyos's annotated edition of *Morals and Dogma*, published by the Scottish
Rite itself in 2011 with several thousand critical notes, demonstrates that large portions of
Albert Pike's book are unattributed transcription, and that the largest single identified
source is Éliphas Lévi.[^1]

That is a finding about plagiarism and provenance and it is settled. This chapter asks a
different question.

**What did these two men think the ritual was doing?**

Because they are the first people in this history to ask Book II's question directly, and
their answers — which are wrong in ways that are precisely datable — are the best evidence we
have that the question was worth asking.

---

### Lévi's answer

Strip away the cosmology and Lévi's account of magical operation has a structure that is
startlingly modern.

There is a universal medium, which he calls the astral light — plastic, pervasive, the
vehicle of imagination and life. There is the operator's **will**, trained by discipline. And
there are the instruments of operation: ceremony, symbol, the pentagram, the circle, the
robes, the hour.

And the instruments do not act on the world. **They act on the operator.**

This is the decisive move and it is what makes Lévi the hinge of everything after him. In the
older grimoire tradition, the operator compels or petitions a spirit, and the apparatus is
addressed outward. In Lévi, the ceremonial exists to concentrate the magician's own
imagination and resolve to a point at which the will can act.

Ceremony is a technology of attention, and the astral light is what it acts through.

**Remove the astral light and you have Chapter 33.**

I want to be careful about that sentence, because it would be easy to read as a claim that
Lévi was secretly a psychologist. He was not, and he would have rejected the description with
some heat; he believed the astral light was real and that the operation reached beyond the
operator. Type E is his, not mine, and I neither endorse nor deny it.

What can be said is narrower and remarkable enough. **Lévi located the mechanism of magic in
the operator's attention, imagination and will, and described the ceremonial apparatus as
existing to organise those.** That is a functional account of ritual efficacy, produced in
1854, by a man with no access to any of the disciplines that would later make such accounts
respectable.

---

### Pike's answer

Pike's project was different and less original and in one respect more interesting.

He inherited a degree system in poor condition — rituals of French origin, variously
transmitted, inconsistently worked, thin in content. He rewrote them. And he wrote *Morals
and Dogma* to say what they meant.

His method is comparative mythology in the Renaissance manner. Take a symbol; pursue it
across Egyptian religion, Zoroastrianism, the Vedas, Greek philosophy, the Hebrew scriptures,
Gnosticism, Kabbalah, Christianity, Islam, alchemy; assemble the parallels; draw a moral
conclusion. The learning is genuine, the range is enormous, and the credulity is
period-standard: his sources for ancient religion were the comparative mythology of the
mid-nineteenth century, which was frequently wrong, and he had no way to know.

His answer to *what does the ritual do* is therefore an answer about *meaning*: the ceremony
works by conveying, in veiled form, a wisdom that all traditions share.

**That answer is wrong, and the way it is wrong is the most useful thing about it.**

It is wrong historically, because the traditions do not share a common wisdom and the
parallels are the artefact of a method that cannot fail — which is Chapter 38's argument
applied to comparative mythology.

And it is wrong functionally, because Chapter 35 established that Masonic ritual does not
transmit a common content at all. The members hold incompatible interpretations. Pike's own
book is proof: it is one man's supplied meaning, and Waite supplied a different one, and
Leadbeater a third.

---

### What Pike could not see

There is a moment of real pathos in Pike's position and I think it deserves stating.

He was working on a system he correctly perceived to be powerful. He had watched the degrees
performed and had been affected by them. He knew — anyone who has been in that room knows —
that something is happening which is not accounted for by the propositional content, because
the propositional content is a book and two tools and some moral advice.

**And the only explanatory vocabulary available to him was antiquity.**

If the ceremony is powerful and its stated content is slight, then in 1871 there were
essentially two available conclusions. Either the power comes from something hidden in the
content — an ancient wisdom, veiled — or there is no power and the people reporting it are
mistaken.

The third option, that the power lies in the *arrangement* rather than the content, required
concepts that did not exist. Van Gennep was thirty-eight years away. Turner was ninety-eight
years away. There was no ritual studies, no cognitive science of religion, no vocabulary for
saying that a ceremony might work by controlling attention rather than by conveying
knowledge.

So Pike did the only thing available and looked for the wisdom. He found it, because his
method could not fail to. And he wrote eight hundred and sixty pages of it and gave it to a
hundred thousand men.

**He was not doing bad psychology. He was doing the best phenomenology possible in 1871** —
reporting, in the only language there was, that the machine does something the machine does
not say it does.

---

### The cost of the answer

And then the consequence, which is the reason Part X sits where it does.

Because Pike's explanation was *antiquity*, and because his book was distributed to an entire
jurisdiction for a century, American Freemasonry acquired an authorised-looking account of
itself in which the ceremony's power derives from hidden ancient wisdom.

Two things followed, and both are still with us.

**Masons came to believe it**, which is why so much of the twentieth-century Masonic
literature is a search for the ancient sources of symbols that Chapter 14 dated to the
eighteenth century.

**And anti-Masons came to believe it**, which is why *Morals and Dogma* is the single most
quarried text in the conspiratorial literature. If the head of the Rite says the degrees
encode the mysteries of antiquity, that is a confession — and the fact that he was describing
an effect and reaching for the wrong explanation is not a distinction that survives being
quoted out of context.

The preceding volume documented what was done with Pike's Lucifer passages by a French
journalist named Léo Taxil, who fabricated a Luciferian Masonry, attributed a forged letter
to Pike, and confessed the whole thing in a hall full of reporters on 19 April 1897 — after
which it was believed for another century.

**Pike supplied the vocabulary. Taxil supplied the crime. Neither was describing
Freemasonry.**

---

### The direction of the debt

One closing point of chronology, because it inverts the popular picture completely.

The sequence is: Lévi publishes in Paris in 1854–56. Pike transcribes him into a Masonic
commentary in Charleston in 1871. American Masons read it for the next hundred years and
conclude that Kabbalah, the astral light and the pentagram's polarity are ancient Masonic
doctrine.

**The material entered the tradition within living memory of the men who first read it.**

Which means that when a twentieth-century writer — devotional or hostile — describes
Freemasonry as a repository of Hermetic and Kabbalistic teaching, the thing being described
is a nineteenth-century French synthesis that reached Masonry in 1871 in a book whose author
did not name his source.

Not a survival. A recent acquisition, with a receipt.

---

### How a recent acquisition comes to look ancient

The mechanism deserves description, because it is not peculiar to Freemasonry and the same
process is at work in a great many traditions that believe themselves older than they are.

**Step one: an authority writes.** Pike was not a fringe figure. He was Sovereign Grand
Commander of the Southern Jurisdiction, and *Morals and Dogma* was distributed to candidates
as a matter of course for the best part of a century. Material arriving by that route does
not arrive as one man's opinion; it arrives as what the institution says.

**Step two: the source is invisible.** Pike did not cite Lévi. This was not, by the standards
of his century and his genre, unusual, and it is not evidence of bad faith. But the effect is
decisive: a reader in 1890 encountering the astral light in a Masonic publication had no way
of knowing it had been in print in French for thirty-four years and in Masonry for nineteen.

**Step three: the material is absorbed into the tradition's self-description.** Later Masonic
writers, reading Pike as a Masonic source, repeat what they find there. Within two
generations the claim is no longer "Pike says" but "Masonry teaches," and the attribution has
dropped out entirely.

**Step four: antiquity is inferred from the absence of a date.** Because nobody can say when
the doctrine entered, it is assumed always to have been there. This is the crucial move and
it is a logical error of a specific and common kind: treating the limit of one's own records
as the limit of the thing recorded.

**Step five: the assumption becomes evidence.** A later writer, seeking to establish that
Masonry preserves ancient Kabbalistic teaching, cites the Masonic literature in which it
appears — which is to say, cites the downstream consequences of Pike's transcription as
independent confirmation of it. This is circulation mistaken for corroboration, and it is the
single most productive error in the whole field.

The chain is only 150 years long and every link is documented. What makes it instructive is
that it is documented *and still generally believed*, which should temper anyone's confidence
about chains of comparable length that happen to fall in a period where the documents did not
survive.

---

### Why Pike is not the villain

It would be easy to end this chapter with Pike as a forger and he was not one, and the
distinction matters for how the book treats the whole nineteenth century.

Pike believed what he wrote. His procedure — assembling comparative religious material from
the best sources available to him and arranging it as commentary — was ordinary
nineteenth-century practice, and the scholarship he drew on was the scholarship of his
period, which was confident about Egyptian and Indian antiquity in ways no longer tenable.
He was reading Lévi as a source of genuine knowledge rather than as a contemporary author
with a system of his own, because that is what Lévi presented himself as.

His error was not deception. It was the absence of a method for distinguishing a tradition
from a synthesis — precisely the method Chapter 5 sets out and which was not generally
available in 1871.

That should produce some humility in the other direction too. The apparatus this book uses
was assembled over the following century and a half, by people working on other problems,
and it is not obvious which of its assumptions will look to a later reader the way Pike's
Egyptology looks to us.
