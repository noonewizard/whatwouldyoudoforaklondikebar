# CHAPTER FORTY-THREE
## The Occult Mason

There is a figure who appears in a great deal of writing about Freemasonry and who has
rarely been examined: the Mason who knows.

He is the member who has penetrated the symbolism, who understands what the ceremonies
actually convey, who holds the interpretation that the ordinary brother recites without
comprehending. In the anti-Masonic literature he is the inner circle. In the occult
literature he is the genuine initiate. In Masonic writing he is the ideal toward which the
studious member aspires.

**Has such a person ever existed?**

The question sounds hostile and is not meant to be. It is the natural terminal question of
this part of the book, and the answer turns out to be interesting in both directions.

---

### The four claimants

Take the men Part X has examined and ask what each actually possessed.

**Lévi was not a Freemason in any meaningful sense.** He had brief and unimportant Masonic
contact, and his system was assembled from books. What he understood about Masonry he
understood from outside.

**Pike was the head of a Masonic jurisdiction and had read enormously — and his
interpretation was substantially transcribed from Lévi without attribution.** He possessed
office, learning and a library. What he did not possess was a Masonic tradition of esoteric
interpretation, because there was none; he was making one, out of French sources.

**Westcott, Mathers and Woodman were all Freemasons**, and the Golden Dawn's apparatus is
Masonic. But its content came from a second-hand book trade, and its warrant was forged by
one of them. They took Masonic *form* and non-Masonic *content*, and they knew exactly what
they were doing, which is why they had to fabricate a German order to explain where it had
come from.

**Leadbeater was a Co-Mason** and his interpretation is Theosophical doctrine applied to a
ceremony.

**In no case does the esoteric content come from Freemasonry.** In every case it is supplied
from outside by a man who had read widely, and who then found it in the Craft.

That is not a coincidence and it is not fraud. It is what Chapter 35 predicted: a ceremony
that produces conviction without specifying content will produce members who supply content,
and the content will be whatever their century has available.

---

### The Mason who actually knew something

So is there anybody in this history who held a genuinely Masonic esoteric tradition?

The best candidates are eighteenth-century and continental, and the preceding volume
examined them.

**Martinez de Pasqually**, who founded the Élus Coëns in 1761 and conducted theurgic
operations inside a structure of Masonic degrees. That is unambiguously operative magic in a
Masonic vessel — and the doctrine was his own, assembled from Christian theosophy and
Kabbalistic sources, and it entered the Masonic structure with him. He did not find it there.

**Jean-Baptiste Willermoz**, who took von Hund's chivalric shell, removed the Templar claim
that had become indefensible, and filled it with Martinez's doctrine of the fall and
reintegration. Again: content supplied, not discovered.

**The Gold- und Rosenkreuz**, which taught alchemy to men who had to be Master Masons first —
and which was teaching them alchemy, not Masonry.

**In every documented case, the esoteric Mason is a man who brought something in.**

I have not found a single instance, in either volume, of a person who discovered an esoteric
content already present in Masonic ritual and transmitted it onward. The direction is always
inward from elsewhere, and then outward again under a Masonic name.

---

### What the ordinary Mason had

Which raises the question the other way round, and it is the one I find more interesting.

If the occult Mason is a myth, what about the man who was not looking for anything — who
joined for the company, attended for forty years, learned the ritual, held the offices, and
never read a word of Pike?

**He had something, and it was not nothing, and it was not what the occult literature
describes.**

He had a body of material memorised so thoroughly he could deliver it at eighty. He had a
dated memory of an evening he would describe as having made an impression. He had a set of
obligations with documented consequences — the charity accounts are real and the sums are
large. He had a form of address, a place in a room, and a burial.

And he had a vocabulary: the rough stone, the standard that does not consult you, the
unfinished building, the work that is never done.

**Whether that vocabulary changed how he lived is Part XI's question and the evidence is
thinner than anyone would like.** But it is the thing the Craft actually transmitted, and it
is available to a man who could not read, which the esoteric interpretations were not.

There is a case — and Waite, of all people, came close to making it — that the man who was
not looking for a secret got more from the system than the men who were.

---

### The most honest thing Pike wrote

A detail that belongs here.

In the preface to *Morals and Dogma*, Albert Pike states that the work was prepared for the
use of the Rite, that everyone is free to reject any of its opinions, and that he did not
intend to impose them.[^1]

Almost nobody quotes that sentence. His enemies do not, because it undercuts the charge that
he was promulgating a doctrine. His admirers do not, because it undercuts the authority of
the book.

But it is the most accurate thing in eight hundred and sixty pages, and it tells us that
Pike understood his own position better than his readers have.

**He knew he was offering an interpretation and not reporting a tradition**, and he said so,
and it was read as a tradition anyway, for a century, by everyone on both sides.

---

### The figure dissolves

So the occult Mason, as an archetype, does not survive examination.

There was no inner circle holding the real meaning. There was no hidden transmission of
esoteric doctrine within Freemasonry. There were men of learning who brought material in
from outside, and who found it in the Craft because the Craft had left room, and whose
findings were then read as discoveries rather than as contributions.

**And the reason the figure is so persistent is that both sides of the argument need him.**

The occult tradition needs him, because without an authentic Masonic esotericism its own
pedigree collapses — the Golden Dawn's claim on Masonic form is more respectable if the form
was already magical.

The conspiratorial tradition needs him more, because without an inner circle that knows the
real meaning, the outer membership's obvious innocence is fatal to the whole thesis. Every
anti-Masonic argument since Barruel has required a layer of members who understand what the
ordinary brother does not.

**Both traditions have an interest in the same fictional person, and neither has produced
him.**

What the record shows instead is a ceremony with no authorised meaning, a membership that
interpreted it in incompatible ways, and a succession of learned men who filled the space and
believed they had found it full.

Which brings us, finally, to the part of this book I have been postponing since Chapter 6.

We know what the ceremony consists of. We know what its participants believed. We know where
the interpretations came from and when.

**We do not yet know whether any of it worked.**
