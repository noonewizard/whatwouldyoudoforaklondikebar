# PART XI — THE TEST

# CHAPTER FORTY-FOUR
## Does Ritual Change People?

Here is the strongest evidence against this book's thesis, and I am putting it first.

Open the minute books and what you find, in lodge after lodge, decade after decade, is
**attrition**. Members proposed, balloted, initiated, and then not present. Summonses
unanswered. Dues unpaid. Names struck off. Committees appointed to inquire into the
circumstances of brethren in arrears.

The pattern is universal enough that it barely registers as remarkable to anyone who works
with the records. A lodge initiates a man and a few years later he has stopped coming, and
in due course he is excluded for non-payment.[^1]

**If Masonic initiation is a technology of transformation, its commonest outcome was a man
who came for a while, enjoyed the dinners, drifted away, and was struck off.**

That is not a marginal exception to be noted and passed over. On any reasonable estimate it
describes a very large proportion — quite possibly a majority — of everyone who ever went
through the ceremony this book has spent forty-three chapters analyzing.

Any honest test has to start there.

---

### What "does it work" would have to mean

Before assessing the evidence, establish what could count as evidence, because most of the
literature never does.

The claim under test is not that Masonic ritual produces *an effect*. Any hour-long ordeal
produces effects. The claim is that it produces the specific effect the Craft advertises:
that a man is improved — made more upright, more charitable, more governed.

To test that we would need, at minimum: a measure of the outcome, a baseline, a comparison
group of similar men who did not undergo the ceremony, and control for the fact that men who
volunteer for a moral fraternity may differ from those who do not.

**We have none of these for any historical period, and we can never have them.** There were
no instruments, no baselines and no controls. The people are dead.

So the question as the Craft asks it — does Masonry make better men? — is not answerable by
historical method, and I am not going to pretend otherwise by assembling testimony.

What *can* be assessed is narrower and is set out below, in descending order of evidential
security.

---

### What is securely evidenced

**1. Memory.** Well evidenced, and the mechanism is unremarkable.

A man who had been through the ceremony could reproduce its content decades later. This is
not inferred; it is documented by the institution's own operations. The examination of
visitors depended on it. Men who had not attended for years were tested and passed. Masonic
biography routinely records members reciting ritual in old age.

The ceremony reliably installed something durable and retrievable. That much is a matter of
record.

**2. Social obligation with behavioral consequences.** Well evidenced, and this is the
strongest finding in the chapter.

Masonic charity is documented in accounts rather than in rhetoric. Relief of distressed
brethren, provision for widows and orphans, the great charitable foundations of the
nineteenth century. The sums are real, they were paid by members, and they were paid on the
strength of an obligation sworn in a room.

**This is a behavioral effect of ritual commitment, measured in money, recorded in ledgers.**
It is the one place where the question *does the ceremony change what people do* has a clean
affirmative answer.

**3. Identity.** Well evidenced, as Chapter 35 set out — a self-description supplied by the
institution, maintained by monthly use, marked by regalia, and carried to the grave.

**4. Group cohesion.** Evidenced by the institution's mere persistence. Voluntary
associations mostly do not last three hundred years across six continents. Whatever holds
members together, the Craft has more of it than almost any comparable body.

---

### What is not evidenced

**Moral improvement.** No data, no controls, no possibility of either. The Craft's central
claim about itself is the one thing the historical record cannot address.

**Altered states.** The conditions are present and the reports are absent, as Chapters 3 and
33 established. Three centuries of testimony, including hostile testimony, describes sober
impressiveness and nothing else.

**And universal or even typical effect.** The attrition data is decisive here. Whatever the
ceremony does, it does not do it to everyone, and quite possibly does not do it to most.

---

### The experimental literature, handled carefully

There is a body of modern research that bears on this and I have to be more careful with it
than most writers on this subject are.

**Dimitris Xygalatas and colleagues** have measured effects of high-ordeal ritual on living
populations — physiological synchrony, prosocial behavior, charitable giving — in fieldwork
including the Thaipusam Kavadi in Mauritius and the Anastenaria fire-walking of northern
Greece. The findings are real and the methods are careful.[^2]

They are also findings about twenty-first-century participants in specific non-Western ritual
contexts undergoing ordeals far more severe than anything in a lodge. **Transferring them to
an eighteenth-century English tradesman is an inference across two centuries, several
cultures and a large difference in intensity.** I make it, because the alternative is
silence, and I mark it.

**Harvey Whitehouse's modes-of-religiosity framework** predicts, for infrequent, high-arousal,
sensorially rich ritual: vivid episodic memory, strong small-group cohesion, transmission by
participation, and interpretive diversity. Masonic practice exhibits all four, and the fourth
— members holding incompatible interpretations of identical ceremonies — is strikingly
confirmed by the Craft's own literature.

A framework built on Melanesian material predicting four independent features of an unrelated
tradition is worth recording. It is not proof of anything, and Whitehouse's framework has
been contested in detail.

**And Aronson and Mills.** The 1959 finding that severity of initiation increases liking for
the group is the single most-cited result in discussions of fraternal initiation, it would
explain a great deal, and **its replication record is mixed.** I set it aside in Chapter 10
and I am setting it aside again here. A book that spends a chapter teaching readers to
distrust a striking result cannot then lean on one because it is convenient.

---

### Three confounds that no historical study can remove

Even the four securely evidenced findings above are weaker than they look, and the reasons
are worth naming because they apply to every claim anyone makes about what initiation does.

**Selection.** Men who present themselves for admission to a moral fraternity are not a
random sample of men. They are, by the act of applying, people already disposed toward
associational life, already possessed of the fee, already known to a member willing to
propose them, and already attracted to whatever the institution appeared to offer. If
Masons were more charitable than their neighbors, the ceremony is one candidate
explanation and prior disposition is another, and nothing in the historical record can
separate them. The same objection applies with equal force to every study of every
voluntary association ever conducted, which is why the honest literature on fraternal
effects is so much smaller than the enthusiastic literature.

**Survivorship in the sources.** The men who wrote about what Masonry did for them are
overwhelmingly the men in whom it took. A member who found the ceremony dull and stopped
attending wrote no memoir about it. The testimonial record is therefore not a sample of
initiates; it is a sample of enthusiasts, and reading it as evidence of typical effect is
the same error as judging a medicine by the letters from people it cured. The attrition
data at the head of this chapter matters precisely because it is the one body of evidence
generated by the indifferent as well as by the devoted — minute books record the men who
stopped coming, because somebody had to strike them off.

**Confounded exposure.** Even among those who stayed, initiation never arrived alone. It
came with dinners, with business contacts, with a weekly or monthly evening away from the
house, with office and its responsibilities, and with the company of men whose good opinion
mattered. A member who became more charitable over twenty years was subject to all of it.
Attributing the change to the ceremony rather than to the company is a choice, not a
finding, and the evidence does not compel it.

None of these three is a reason to abandon the question. All three are reasons to state the
answer more weakly than the literature on either side prefers.

---

### What a real study would look like

It is worth setting out, because saying that something is not known is more useful when
accompanied by what would be required to know it.

A serious contemporary study is possible, and to this study's knowledge has never been
done. Initiation is a scheduled event with a known date; candidates are identified in
advance; the intervention is standardized within a jurisdiction; and — the feature that
makes the design tractable — there is very often a **waiting period** between acceptance and
ceremony, which supplies a natural comparison group of men who have been approved but not
yet initiated. Measure both groups before, and measure again at intervals afterward.

What to measure would have to be decided in advance and pre-registered, which is the point
at which most such proposals collapse, because the Craft's own claim — that its members are
made better men — is not operationalised anywhere in its literature. A study would have to
choose: charitable giving, which is at least countable; some behavioral measure of
cooperation; or self-reported identification, which is easy to collect and measures mainly
whether people say they belong.

The obstacles are real and they are not merely practical. A jurisdiction would have to agree
to it; candidates would have to consent without the consent itself altering the experience;
and no design can blind a participant to whether he has been initiated. Some of what this
chapter wants to know is probably unmeasurable in principle.

But the gap between *unmeasurable in principle* and *nobody has tried* is large, and almost
all of the present uncertainty sits in the second category rather than the first.

---

### The verdict

Assemble it honestly and this is what the evidence supports.

**Masonic ritual reliably produces: durable memory, a maintained self-description, and
obligations with documented behavioral consequences in charity and mutual aid.**

**It does not reliably produce: continued participation. A large proportion of initiates
lapsed.**

**And whether it produces moral improvement is unknown and unknowable by historical
method.**

That is a weaker result than the Craft claims and a stronger one than its critics allow. It
is also, I think, about what one should expect from any institution that operates on
volunteers: a minority in whom it takes deeply, a majority in whom it does not, and a
documentary record that preserves both.

---

### The thing the test misses

One final point, and it is the reason the next three chapters exist.

**Every assessment in this chapter has treated the initiation as the intervention.** That is
what the ritual-studies literature does, what the cognitive literature does, and what this
chapter has done.

It is very probably the wrong unit.

The man who lapsed after two years received one ceremony. The man who attended for forty
years received that ceremony a hundred times, from every position, learning it by heart,
eventually conducting it.

**Those are not the same treatment**, and we have been comparing them as though they were.

If Masonic ritual changes anybody, the evidence in this chapter suggests it is not the
evening that does it. Chapter 47 argues that it is the thirty years — and that no model in
the literature describes them.
