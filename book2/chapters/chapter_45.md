# CHAPTER FORTY-FIVE
## Can Symbols Change Behaviour?

A man is given a square and told that it teaches him to regulate his actions.

Does he then regulate his actions?

This is the narrowest and most testable version of the Craft's claim about itself, and it is
worth pursuing precisely because it is narrow. If a symbolic instruction with no external
criterion can be shown to affect conduct, that is a substantial finding. If it cannot, that
is equally substantial and rather more interesting.

---

### The problem with the question

The difficulty is that behaviour has many causes and symbols are among the weakest.

Consider what else was acting on an eighteenth-century Mason's conduct. Religion, in a
century when almost everyone attended some form of worship. Law. Reputation, in small
communities where it was economically decisive. Family. Trade guild obligations. Debt. The
magistrate.

A moral instruction attached to a hand tool, delivered once in a ceremony, is competing with
all of that, and the null hypothesis — that it made no measurable difference — is not
unreasonable and cannot be dismissed rhetorically.

So the honest approach is to look for places where Masonic obligation would predict behaviour
*different from* what the other pressures predict, and see whether it occurred.

There are three such places and the evidence differs sharply between them.

---

### Case one: charity. The affirmative.

Masonic charitable provision is the strongest evidence in this book that the apparatus
changed what people did, and the reason is that it leaves accounts.

Relief of distressed brethren; provision for widows and orphans; the great institutional
charities of the nineteenth century, with buildings and endowments and annual reports.
Members paid, and the sums were not trivial, and the payments were made on the strength of an
obligation sworn in a room.

**Why this counts as evidence.** Charity in general is over-determined — Christian duty,
social expectation, self-interest, reputation — so simply noting that Masons gave money
proves nothing.

What counts is the *pattern* of giving. Masonic relief was directed preferentially to
brethren and their dependants, including brethren who were strangers, in distant places, with
no relationship to the donor except the shared obligation. A travelling Mason in distress in
a foreign port had a claim on men who had never met him, and the claim was frequently
honoured.

**That is a behaviour the other pressures do not predict.** Religion enjoins charity to the
poor generally, not to a specific class of strangers identified by a recognition system. The
preferential direction is the fingerprint of the obligation, and it is visible in the ledgers.

**Verdict: affirmative, and well evidenced.**

---

### Case two: conduct between members. The mixed result.

Masonic obligation enjoins members not to wrong one another — not to cheat a brother, not to
injure his interests, not to violate his household.

Did they comply?

The evidence here is genuinely mixed and it is instructive that the institution preserved
it.

**Lodge disciplinary records exist.** Charges brought, committees appointed, members
admonished, suspended and excluded. Masons defrauded Masons, quarrelled with Masons, and
were expelled for it.[^1]

That is disconfirming evidence for perfect compliance and confirming evidence for something
else: **the obligation was treated as enforceable, and the enforcement mechanism was social
and institutional rather than penal.** Nobody had his throat cut. He was excluded, and in a
small town in 1790 exclusion from a lodge was a material event.

**Verdict: compliance imperfect; the obligation nonetheless operated as a real constraint
with real consequences.** Which is roughly what one would say about any legal or moral system
that has ever existed.

---

### Case three: the moral instruction proper. No evidence.

And then the claim the Craft actually makes: that the working tools teach a man to square his
conduct, keep his passions within bounds, and act uprightly.

**There is no evidence whatever, in either direction.**

No measure. No baseline. No comparison group. No criterion by which a man could be assessed
as having become more upright. Nobody was ever tested.

And the instruction is unfalsifiable by design. Chapter 12 noted this: a man's inner temple
cannot be inspected, nobody can fail, and there is no point at which anyone is certified as
squared.

I have looked for indirect evidence and not found any that survives. One might hope that
Masonic membership correlates with something measurable — lower rates of prosecution,
different patterns of bequest, distinctive behaviour in business — but the confounds are
overwhelming. Masons were disproportionately drawn from social strata that differ on all
those measures for other reasons.

**Verdict: unknown, and I do not think it is knowable.**

---

### What symbols plausibly do instead

Having failed to establish the strong claim, it is worth asking what the weaker one might be,
because I think the Craft's symbolism does something that is not quite moral improvement.

**It supplies a vocabulary.**

A man who has been given the rough ashlar has a way of describing himself as unfinished. A
man who has been given the level has a way of describing a relationship as equal. A man who
has been given the unfinished Temple has a way of understanding an obligation as ongoing.

None of that makes him behave differently by itself. What it does is make certain
descriptions *available* — and descriptions of one's own situation are not inert. A person
who can say *I am working on something that will not be completed* is in a different relation
to a long obligation than one who cannot.

**This is a real effect and it is not measurable**, and I want to be clear that I am
describing a plausible mechanism rather than reporting a finding. It belongs to the category
of claims this book's framework calls psychological: hedged, general, and never applied to a
named individual.

---

### The asymmetry worth noticing

One closing observation.

The two cases where the evidence is affirmative — charity and enforceable conduct between
members — are both cases where the obligation is **social and specific**. Give relief to a
brother. Do not wrong a brother. Both name a class of persons and an action, and both are
checkable by other people.

The case where there is no evidence is the one where the obligation is **internal and
general**. Be upright. Square your conduct. Keep your passions within bounds.

**The Craft's measurable effects come from the parts that create relationships between
people. Its unmeasurable claims come from the parts that concern a man alone with himself.**

That may simply reflect what leaves records. Or it may be telling us where the machine
actually acts — and if it is, then Freemasonry is a more sociological and less
psychological instrument than either its defenders or its occult interpreters have supposed.

Which leaves one more environment to test, and it is the one Part III argued was doing less
work than anybody thinks.
