# CHAPTER FORTY-SIX
## Can Architecture Change Consciousness?

There is a finding that everybody who writes about sacred space reaches for, and I am going
to state it and then take it apart, because how it is usually used is a good example of
exactly the error this book has spent a chapter warning against.

In 2007 Joan Meyers-Levy and Rui Zhu published a paper in the *Journal of Consumer Research*
reporting that ceiling height influenced the kind of processing participants engaged in.
Higher ceilings were associated with more abstract, relational thinking; lower ceilings with
more concrete, detail-oriented processing. The proposed mechanism was priming: a high ceiling
activates concepts of freedom, a low one of confinement.[^1]

It has been named the cathedral effect, and it is cited constantly in writing about
religious architecture, usually as though it established that Gothic naves induce
transcendence.

**Three cautions before anyone builds on it.**

**It is priming research.** That is the area of social psychology worst affected by the
replication crisis of the last fifteen years. Some priming findings have held; many have
not; and the field's own practitioners now treat the older literature with considerable
reserve. A single paper from 2007 in this genre is not a foundation.

**The effect sizes are modest**, and the difference tested was between eight-foot and ten-foot
ceilings in laboratory rooms — not between a tavern and Chartres.

**And the extrapolation is enormous.** From a measured difference in processing style in a
controlled setting to a claim about religious experience in a cathedral is several steps, and
each one is a place where the inference could fail.

I am not saying the finding is false. I am saying that anybody who cites it to explain what
sacred architecture does to people is doing the thing this book keeps criticising: taking a
striking result and treating its strikingness as evidence.

---

### And the Masonic lodge is not a cathedral anyway

Which is fortunate, because the argument was never available.

For most of the history covered by this book the lodge met in a rented upstairs room in a
public house. Low ceiling. Fireplace. Tables that had to be cleared. It was a dining room
before the meeting and a dining room after it.

Lodges also met, over three centuries, in warehouses, private houses, ships, garrison tents,
schoolrooms, and — documented in several cases — prisoner-of-war camps.

**Whatever the Masonic ceremony does, it did it in those rooms**, and no theory that depends
on architectural grandeur can be right about it.

---

### What the lodge actually does to a room

So the question has to be reframed, and once reframed it has a good answer.

The lodge does not use architecture. **It constructs a space out of an ordinary one**, by
declaration and movement, and the components are cheap and portable.

**An east is declared.** Not surveyed — declared. The Master's chair is placed, and where it
is placed is east. Masonic sources say so plainly. This single act imports the entire
inherited grammar of sunrise, altar and authority, without a word of explanation and without
a compass.

**Stations are assigned.** Three officers at three points converts an empty room into a
diagram with positions that mean something.

**A center is established** — a place where things are done *to* someone, and to which the
candidate is conducted.

**A boundary is guarded.** This is the single most important architectural act in the room,
and it requires one man and a door.

**A design is laid on the floor.** In the eighteenth century, drawn in chalk and charcoal and
mopped away at the end by the newest member. Later a painted cloth, later a board on the
wall.

**Light is controlled.** Candles in positions, and the candidate's sight removed and restored
at a chosen moment.

**And then it reverts.** The chairs go back. The design is erased. The room is a dining room.

### Jonathan Z. Smith's thesis, confirmed

This is where the theoretical framework introduced in Chapter 2 earns its place.

Smith argued in 1987 that sacrality is not a property certain places possess but one that
ritual attention confers — that ritual is the activity which marks a place, and that the
marking is what makes the place sacred rather than the reverse.[^2]

**Freemasonry is very nearly the ideal test case for that thesis**, and I do not think it has
been used as one.

Here is a tradition with the most architectural imagination of any in this book — temples,
pillars, pavements, orientations, the orders — and, for most of its history, no architecture
at all. It had to make the temple out of assertion and movement, every time, in whatever room
was available, and then unmake it before supper.

If sacrality were a property of buildings, Masonic ritual could not have worked in a tavern.
It evidently did, for two hundred years, across six continents.

**The conclusion is not that architecture is irrelevant to ritual. It is that architecture is
one way of achieving something that can be achieved otherwise** — and that the Craft's method,
being portable, turned out to travel better.

### The one thing that might be architectural

Having dismissed grandeur, there is a spatial effect I do think is real, and Chapter 8
established it.

The candidate learns the room blind, by walking it, before he sees it. He is conducted round
a fixed circuit, challenged at fixed points, stopped and moved on, for some minutes.

When the hoodwink comes off, **the room is already familiar** — and the experience of
recognizing a place you have never seen is uncommon enough that I suspect it accounts for a
substantial part of what candidates across three centuries have described as the peculiar
impressiveness of that moment.

That is not an effect of the architecture. It is an effect of *how the architecture was
learned*, which is a different thing and is entirely within the ceremony's control.

A grand building would not produce it. A blindfold does.

---

### The nineteenth-century anomaly, revisited

Chapter 12 raised the question and this is the place to close it.

From the 1850s Freemasonry built monumentally: purpose-built temples, increasingly large,
classical or Egyptian in idiom, culminating in buildings like the House of the Temple in
Washington and the enormous Detroit temple of 1926.

Four explanations were offered there and tested. Affluence and membership growth.
Post-Morgan respectability politics. The staging requirements of the high degrees, which are
theater and need machinery. And the esoteric reinterpretation of Masonic symbolism making
grandeur meaningful.

The first three account for most of the phenomenon. The fourth — the one that would have
supported this book's thesis — accounts for some flagship buildings and should not be
extended.

**And the sequel is worth recording.** The monumental temples arrived as American membership
peaked and outlived it. The buildings remained when the members did not: enormous,
expensive, and increasingly empty. The Detroit Masonic Temple is now substantially a concert
venue.

**The tradition's portable period was its expansive one.** When the Craft needed only a room
and an agreement, it went everywhere. When it acquired buildings, it acquired fixed costs and
fixed locations and an obligation to fill them.

I would not press that into a moral. But it is consistent with everything this chapter has
argued: the apparatus was never architectural, and the century in which Freemasonry forgot
that is the century in which it began to contract.

---

### The answer

**Can architecture change consciousness?**

Probably, at the margins, in ways the current literature has not established as firmly as it
is usually cited for.

**Did Masonic architecture change consciousness?**

Almost certainly not, because for most of the relevant period there was none — and the
ceremony worked anyway, in rooms whose only qualification was that a door could be shut.

**What did the work was the arrangement, not the building.** An east declared, a boundary
guarded, a center, a route walked in the dark.

That is a set of instructions rather than a structure, and instructions fit in a man's head,
which is why they crossed the Atlantic in sea chests while the cathedrals stayed where they
were.
