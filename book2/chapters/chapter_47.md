# CHAPTER FORTY-SEVEN
## Secrecy, Repetition, and Identity

Everything in this book so far has been about an evening.

The ritual-studies literature describes initiation as an event with three phases. The
cognitive literature on high-arousal ritual describes vivid memories of rare, intense
ceremonies. The Masonic literature, devotional and hostile alike, describes what happens to a
man on the night he is made.

**I think they are all looking at the wrong thing, and this chapter is the argument.**

---

### What actually happens to a Freemason

Follow a man who does not lapse.

**Year one.** He is initiated, passed and raised. Three ceremonies, each done to him. He
learns enough to be examined.

**Years two to five.** He attends monthly. He watches the ceremonies performed on other men —
perhaps a dozen times a year, in a small lodge; far more in a large one. He begins to learn
the ritual properly, because he will be asked to take a junior office.

**Years five to fifteen.** He holds offices in succession. Each office has words, positions
and duties, and he learns them by heart and delivers them in front of people who know when he
is wrong. He progresses from the door inward and from the outer stations toward the east.

**Year fifteen or so.** He is Master. He conducts the ceremonies. He is the voice that asks
who comes there, that administers the obligation, that restores the light. He does this to
other men, repeatedly, for a year.

**Years sixteen to fifty.** He is a Past Master. He attends, he prompts, he examines
visitors, he coaches younger men through the parts he learned thirty years ago. He may do
this until he dies.

**The total exposure is not one ceremony. It is one ceremony undergone, and then perhaps a
hundred performed or witnessed, from every position in the room, across half a century.**

---

### Why no model covers this

Go back through the frameworks this book has used and the gap is consistent.

**Van Gennep** describes a passage: separation, liminality, incorporation. It is a model of a
transition, and transitions happen once.

**Turner** elaborates the middle phase. Same assumption.

**Whitehouse's imagistic mode** describes the memory properties of rare, high-arousal
ceremonies. Rarity is definitional to it.

**The experimental work** — Xygalatas and others — measures participants at a ritual event and
in its aftermath.

Every one of these was built on material where the initiated do not subsequently become the
officiants in a graded sequence for thirty years. Some become elders; that is not the same as
performing the identical script from every role, hundreds of times, with the text held by
heart.

**Freemasonry is structurally unusual, and its unusual feature is the one nobody studies.**

---

### What repetition does that initiation cannot

Four things, and they are of different evidential quality.

**It converts an experience into a skill.** Well supported. Chapter 34 set out the mechanism:
the ritual is encoded procedurally, spatially, rhythmically and dialogically, and it is
rehearsed continuously. What a Mason of thirty years holds is not a memory of a night; it is
a competence, of the same kind as a musician's.

**It rotates the perspective.** This is the observation I think is most important and I have
found nothing in the literature that addresses it.

A man experiences the ceremony from inside the hoodwink once. He then experiences it, dozens
of times, from outside — as the one holding the arm, then the one asking the question, then
the one in the east delivering the light.

**He learns what the machine looks like from the operator's side.** He knows where the
candidate is about to be moved, what he is about to be asked, when the hoodwink comes off.
The mystery is entirely dissolved for him, structurally and permanently.

And he goes on attending.

That fact is fatal to any account of Masonic efficacy that depends on the candidate's
ignorance. Whatever holds a Past Master of thirty years, it is not suspense. He knows the
whole thing. He is the one producing it.

**It distributes the obligation over time.** An oath sworn once decays. An oath whose words
you speak, aloud, in the character of the officer administering it, to other men, twice a
year for three decades, is being continuously re-made — and there is good general support for
the proposition that repeated public enactment sustains commitment more effectively than a
single act.

**And it accumulates relationships.** The men he was raised with; the men he raised. A lodge
of long standing is a network of people who have performed this on each other, and the
ceremony is the medium in which the relationships were formed.

---

### Secrecy over time

The same reframing applies to the secret, and it resolves something Chapter 36 left hanging.

If Masonic secrecy were informational, publication would have ended it in 1730 and the
institution would have collapsed. It did not.

If it were purely about the candidate's ignorance, then the Past Master — who knows
everything and has known it for thirty years — would have nothing left, and would leave.

He does not leave.

**Which means the secret, whatever it is, is not a thing he was told. It is something he
participates in producing.**

The catechism exists only between two people, as Chapter 9 argued. The ceremony exists only
when a room of men performs it. A Past Master's relation to the secret is not that of
someone who possesses information but that of someone who *maintains a practice* — and
practices are maintained by doing them, indefinitely, or they stop existing.

That is why the Craft could survive total exposure. You cannot publish a practice. You can
only describe one, and a description does not perform.

---

### Three objections to the career thesis

The claim is strong enough to deserve its strongest counter-arguments stated rather than
implied.

**First: this is selection, not effect.** The men who serve thirty years are the men in whom
it took; the career does not produce the commitment, the commitment produces the career. This
objection is serious and it cannot be dismissed. What can be said against it is that the
progression is not simply a matter of enthusiasm. It is an institutional ladder with places
on it, and lodges have historically had to fill offices from whoever was available. Men
were pressed into the chairs because somebody had to sit in them, and a number of those men
went on to forty-year attendance records. That is not decisive — the pressed man may have
been disposed to stay anyway — but it means the causal arrow is not obviously one-way.

The honest formulation is that career and commitment are mutually reinforcing, which is
harder to study than either alone and is exactly why nobody has.

**Second: this is not distinctively ritual.** Any organisation with a graded office
structure — a choir, a regiment, a trade union — produces long-serving members who know the
procedures from every side, and nobody calls that a technology of transformation. Why should
the lodge be different?

The answer, if there is one, lies in what is being repeated. What a Past Master rehearses is
not a set of administrative procedures but a script about mortality, obligation, and the
insufficiency of what one knows, delivered in the first person to a man who does not yet know
what is coming. Whether that content makes a difference to the effect is an empirical
question this book cannot settle. It is at least a difference in kind from taking the minutes.

**Third: the members themselves do not describe it this way.** Masonic testimony
overwhelmingly foregrounds the night of initiation. If the career were doing the work, one
might expect members to say so.

But one would not, actually, expect that — and the reason is one of the better-established
findings in the psychology this book has used. Vivid, singular, emotionally marked events
are massively over-represented in autobiographical recall relative to their causal weight.
A gradual thirty-year process has no retrieval cue attached to it. The night has one. Asked
what Masonry did to him, a man reports the memory that comes to mind, which is the memory
that was encoded to be memorable. That his testimony centres on the evening is evidence
about memory, not about mechanism.

---

### What the records would show

Because this is offered as a testable claim and not a rhetorical flourish, it is worth
saying what a test would consist of.

The materials exist in very large quantity: minute books recording attendance meeting by
meeting; office rolls recording who held which chair and when; membership registers
recording initiation, progression, resignation, exclusion and death; and, for the nineteenth
century onward, printed lodge histories compiled from all three.

From these one could construct, for thousands of individuals, a career trajectory — date of
initiation, attendance density in each subsequent year, offices held, year of the chair,
duration of post-chair attendance, and mode of exit. That is a dataset, and the questions it
would answer are specific ones.

Does attendance density in years two to five predict retention at year twenty? Does holding
early office predict it better than attendance alone? Is there a threshold — an office, a
year, a number of ceremonies witnessed — after which lapse becomes rare? Do members who
reach the chair differ in their pre-chair records from those who do not, or does the
difference appear only afterwards? Is charitable subscription, which the accounts also
record, related to career stage?

None of these requires a theory of ritual efficacy to ask. They are questions about
trajectories in an administrative record, of a kind historians of other voluntary
associations answer routinely.

What makes them worth asking here is that the answers would discriminate between the two
models. If effect is concentrated in the ceremony, career variables should add little beyond
the initial period. If the career is the intervention, they should dominate — and the shape
of the curve would tell us where.

---

### What this means for the test

Part XI set out to assess whether Masonic ritual changes people, and Chapter 44 gave the
honest answer: memory, identity and obligation are evidenced; moral improvement is not;
and a very large proportion of initiates lapsed.

This chapter adds the qualification that reframes all of it.

**We have been assessing the wrong intervention.**

The man who lapsed received one evening. The man who stayed received an evening plus thirty
years of rehearsed, embodied, socially ratified repetition in a room of people who knew him.

Those are different treatments by any reasonable measure, and the second one has never been
studied — not by ritual theorists, who study initiations; not by cognitive scientists, who
study events; and not by Masonic writers, who have been preoccupied with what the symbols
mean.

**If this book has one finding that I would want tested by somebody better equipped than I
am, it is this one.** The unit of analysis for Masonic ritual is not the ceremony. It is the
career.

And the career is documented — in minute books, office rolls, attendance registers and
membership records, in very large quantities, across three centuries and thousands of lodges.

The evidence exists. Nobody has looked at it with this question in mind.
