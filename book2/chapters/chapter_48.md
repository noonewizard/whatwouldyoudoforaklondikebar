# PART XII — THE RETURN

# CHAPTER FORTY-EIGHT
## The Magic of Transformation

Five transformations, and the question of whether they are one.

Rough stone becomes perfect ashlar. Darkness becomes light. Candidate becomes initiate.
Death becomes life. Chaos becomes order.

The Masonic system asserts all five, in that ceremony, on that evening, and the esoteric
literature has treated them for two centuries as five expressions of a single underlying
process — the Great Work, the transformation of the human being, which alchemy and theurgy
and initiation are all doing in different vocabularies.

**Is that true, or is it a family resemblance dressed as an identity?**

This is the philosophical center of the book and I have not found a clean answer. What I have
found is a way of asking it that makes the alternatives visible.

---

### The case for one process

Set the five side by side and there is a common shape.

**Each has a before and an after separated by a discontinuity.** Not gradual improvement —
a break. The stone is not gradually squared in the imagery; it is worked. The candidate does
not gradually become a Mason; there is a night.

**Each has an agent and a patient, and they are the same person.** This is the doubling of
Chapter 13, and it recurs in every one. The man holds the chisel and is the stone. He seeks
the light and is the one in darkness. He performs the ceremony and is what the ceremony acts
on.

**Each is subtractive rather than additive.** The ashlar is made by removing. Darkness is
dispelled rather than light manufactured. The candidate's old status is stripped before the
new one is given. Order is what remains when disorder is taken away.

**And each is described as incomplete.** The Temple is unfinished. There is always further
light in a succeeding degree. The word is lost and the search continues.

Four structural features shared across five metaphors is not nothing. If these were
independent images we would expect them to differ in these respects, and they do not.

---

### The case against

And yet the argument has a hole in it, and it is the hole this book has been probing since
Chapter 5.

**The features are shared because they are entailed, not because they are transmitted.**

Take them in turn.

*Discontinuity* is required by anything that marks a change of status. A society that needs
to treat someone as definitively different cannot use gradual imagery, because gradual
imagery has no moment at which the change is complete.

*The doubling* is entailed by self-transformation as such. You cannot be improved without
being both the improver and the improved, and any system undertaking deliberate self-change
will produce the structure — in apprenticeship, in athletic training, in therapy, in learning
an instrument. None of those inherited it from Hermes.

*The subtractive form* follows from the doubling. If the agent and the patient are the same,
there is nothing outside to add, and the only available operation is removal.

*And incompleteness* is what any institution requires that needs its members to keep
attending.

**So the five transformations may share a shape because they are five solutions to one
problem, arrived at independently — not five expressions of one doctrine.**

That is the claim this book has made in every part, and here it reaches its most general
form.

---

### What would distinguish the two

The distinction is not idle and it is testable in principle.

**If the five are one transmitted doctrine**, we would expect them to share *arbitrary*
features — details with no independent reason to exist, which could have been otherwise, and
which therefore indicate a common source. That is how textual descent is established: not by
shared sense but by shared accident.

**If they are convergent solutions**, we would expect them to share only *entailed* features
— the ones any solution to the problem would have — and to differ in everything arbitrary.

Apply the test and the result is consistent with convergence.

The Masonic materials are architectural and biblical. The alchemical materials are
metallurgical. The theurgic materials are Neoplatonic and liturgical. The Hermetic materials
are cosmological. **There is no shared arbitrary detail** — no distinctive vocabulary
crossing between them, no peculiar sequence, no idiosyncratic error, none of the fingerprints
that transmission leaves.

They agree on structure and differ in everything that could have been otherwise.

**Verdict: convergence, not descent — which is what the preceding volume established
historically and which this chapter establishes structurally.**

---

### The more interesting question

Which leaves something that I think matters more than the genealogy, and that neither volume
has quite confronted.

**If these are independent solutions to one problem, what is the problem?**

The answer available from this book's evidence: *human beings are extremely resistant to
deliberate change, and ordinary means do not work.*

That is not a mystical proposition. It is close to a commonplace, and it is confirmed by
every honest account of trying to become different — by anyone who has attempted to acquire a
discipline, abandon a habit, or hold to a resolution.

Information does not do it. Telling someone what they should be produces agreement and no
change.

Intention does not do it. Deciding to be different is the thing that reliably fails.

**And what does appear to work, across the cases, is some combination of: sustained
attention, embodied practice, social ratification, an external standard, repetition over
long periods, and a relationship with people doing the same thing.**

Every tradition in this book has that list. They express it differently and explain it
incompatibly and they keep arriving at the same components, because the components are what
the problem requires.

---

### The problem, stated more carefully

That formulation is loose, and the looseness matters, because "human beings are resistant to
deliberate change" is the kind of sentence that sounds profound and explains nothing until it
is made specific.

What the traditions in this book are actually addressing is narrower. It is not that people
cannot change — people change constantly, through age, circumstance, illness, love and
employment. It is that **change which the person themselves intends, in a direction they have
chosen, against an existing disposition, is unusually difficult to bring about.** That is the
problem, and it is a real one with a modern literature of its own: the gap between intention
and behavior is one of the more robust findings in psychology, and the interventions that
close it are known to be few.

Stated that way, the convergence looks less mysterious and more explicable. Any technique
addressing that specific problem is working against the same obstacles — a disposition
already in place, an intention that decays, an environment unchanged by the resolution, and
no external check on whether anything has happened. A solution has to do something about each
of those or it fails.

Look at the six components against that list and the mapping is close to exact. Sustained
attention and embodied practice work on the disposition, because dispositions are habits and
habits answer to repetition rather than to argument. Social ratification and relationship
work on the decay of intention, by attaching the resolution to people who will notice.
An external standard supplies the check that introspection cannot. Repetition over long
periods addresses the fact that none of it happens quickly.

**The six are not a mystical formula. They are the shape of the obstacle, seen from the
other side.** Which is precisely why unrelated traditions arrive at them: they are not
discovering a doctrine, they are running into the same wall and finding the same small number
of ways round it.

---

### What this does not license

Two restrictions, because the argument above is the kind that invites overreach.

**It does not make the traditions equivalent.** Saying that Masonic initiation, alchemical
practice and Neoplatonic theurgy converge on six components is not saying they are the same
thing, that their practitioners wanted the same outcome, or that any of them would have
recognized the description. They differ in what they thought they were doing, in what they
thought was acting, and in what they expected to result. The convergence is at the level of
technique, and technique is the shallowest level at which two practices can agree.

**And it does not establish that the components work.** This chapter has argued that
independent traditions converge on them, and convergence is evidence that people found them
useful, which is not the same as evidence that they are effective. Traditions converge on
ineffective practices too, and the history of medicine is largely a record of it. Bloodletting
was independently invented many times. What Part XI could establish about actual effects is
set out in Chapters 44 to 47, and it is considerably more modest than the elegance of the
six-component scheme would suggest.

The scheme is a description of what these traditions do. Whether doing it accomplishes
anything is a separate question, asked earlier and answered thinly.

---

### The disconfirming instance

I have to record the counter-evidence here, in the chapter that would most like to ignore it.

Chapter 44 established that a very large proportion of Masonic initiates lapsed. They
received the transformation — all five of them, in one evening, exactly as described — and
then stopped attending and were struck off.

**Whatever the five transformations are, they do not reliably transform.**

And Chapter 47 gave the probable reason, which is that the evening was never the mechanism.
The components that the problem requires — sustained attention, repetition over long periods,
social ratification, relationship — are not delivered by a ceremony. They are delivered by
thirty years of them.

Which means the five transformations, taken as an account of what happens on the night, are
**a description of the advertisement rather than the product**.

The ceremony *stages* the transformation. It does not accomplish it. What accomplishes
anything, if anything is accomplished, is the decades — and the ceremony's real function may
be to give those decades a beginning that the participant can point to.

That is a considerably more modest claim than the esoteric tradition has made, and I think it
is better supported.

---

### What survives

So what is left of the Great Work, after this?

**Not a doctrine.** There is no single transmitted teaching about human transformation
running from Egypt through the mysteries to the lodge. The preceding volume showed there was
no route; this chapter shows there is no textual fingerprint either.

**Not a mechanism nobody has noticed.** The components that appear across the traditions —
attention, embodiment, repetition, standard, community — are unremarkable, and their
unremarkableness is the finding.

**What survives is a convergence**, and it is worth respecting rather than deflating.

Many traditions, unconnected, over two thousand years, working independently on the problem
of how a person becomes different, arrived at substantially the same apparatus. They
explained it in incompatible metaphysical terms — astral light, subtle bodies, sulphur and
mercury, the reintegration of fallen beings — and every one of those explanations is either
unverifiable or wrong.

**And the apparatus works, in the limited and measurable senses Part XI established, and it
is the same apparatus.**

That is the most interesting fact in either of these volumes, and it required no secret to
produce. It required only that a great many serious people, over a very long time, paid
attention to what actually changed people and kept what worked.

Which is, in the end, how the violin was arrived at too.
