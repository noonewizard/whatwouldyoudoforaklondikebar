# CHAPTER FORTY-NINE
## What Counts as Magic?

The question the whole book has been walking toward, stated formally:

> **If magic means the deliberate transformation of reality through symbol, ritual, intention
> and action, does Masonic initiation qualify as a form of magic — even when no supernatural
> mechanism is assumed?**

Notice first what kind of question this is. It asks whether a practice satisfies a
definition. Both the practice and the definition are historical objects, documented and
datable. **The question is therefore answerable without any metaphysical commitment
whatever**, which is more than can be said for "is Freemasonry occult?" as ordinarily asked.

Here are the arguments, and then the objections, and then what I think.

---

### The case for

**One: the definition is not mine, and it fits.**

The formulation above is substantially Éliphas Lévi's of 1854 and Aleister Crowley's of 1904.
Lévi located magic in the trained will operating through symbol, ceremony and imagination,
with the operator as both instrument and object. Crowley defined it as causing change in
conformity with will, and explicitly extended it to ordinary purposive activity.

Measured against that, Masonic initiation qualifies without strain. It is deliberate. It uses
symbol, ritual and staged action. It aims at transformation. The operator and the object are
the same person.

**Two: the mechanisms are the same mechanisms.**

Part VIII established what the Masonic apparatus does: confiscates attention, encodes in the
body, exploits controlled information, stages revelation, ratifies socially. Part X showed
that the Golden Dawn's operative work — the ritual of which is agreed by everyone to be magic
— uses the same components, trained more deliberately and practised more often.

If the Golden Dawn's ceremonies are magic and the Masonic ceremony uses the same apparatus
for the same kind of end, the distinction becomes hard to state.

**Three: the practitioners' own testimony points that way.**

Not the Masons, who deny it. But Lévi, Pike, Mathers, Leadbeater and Crowley all looked at
Freemasonry and classified it as belonging to the same family as their own work. They were
wrong about the history, comprehensively. **They may have been right about the
classification**, and they were in an unusually good position to judge, since they had been
in both rooms.

---

### The case against

**One: the intention is absent, and intention may be definitional.**

Crowley's definition turns on *will* — a change deliberately purposed. The Masonic ceremony
is not performed with a specific end in view. The officers are executing a script correctly;
nobody in the lodge is willing an outcome. There is no aim beyond the ceremony's own proper
conduct.

That is a real difference from magical practice, where the operation is *for something*, and
it may be the difference that matters.

**Two: the Craft has no operative dimension at all.**

Part VII established this and it deserves restating. No correspondences. No invocation. No
planetary hours. No circle, no names of power, no talismans. Nothing is being acted upon.
Chapter 28 showed that Masonry declined the table of correspondences that was sitting on the
same shelves for two centuries — and a tradition that had no use for a grammar of operations
is not an operative tradition.

**Three: the definition is too capacious to be useful.**

This is the strongest objection and it comes from Crowley himself, who cheerfully noted that
his definition includes baking a cake.

A definition that includes baking is a definition that will include most human activity, and
a category that includes most human activity is not doing work. If Masonic initiation is
magic under Crowley's definition, so is basic training, so is medical residency, so is
learning an instrument.

**Four: the Masons said no.**

For three hundred years, when asked, Freemasons have denied that what they do is magic — and
Chapter 27 established that self-description is a weak criterion, since almost everybody in
this history denies it. But it is not *no* criterion. The Craft's denial is not evasive: it
denies magic because it understands magic to mean operative spellcraft, and by that
definition it is plainly right.

---

### What I think

I do not think the question has a determinate answer, and I want to explain why that is a
finding rather than an evasion.

**The question is about where a boundary falls, and the boundary was drawn by a word that
moved.**

In 1489 Masonic ritual would not have been magic, because magic then meant the manipulation
of hidden properties in things, and the Craft manipulates nothing.

In 1854 it would have been, because magic then meant the operation of will through symbol
upon the operator, and that is a fair description of what a lodge does.

**The practice did not change. The word moved across it.**

So the honest answer is that Masonic initiation *is* magic under the definitions current
after 1854 and *is not* magic under the definitions current before, and both facts are facts
about the word.

That sounds like a dodge. I do not think it is, because of what it tells us.

---

### The better question

If "is it magic?" is a question about a moving boundary, then the question worth asking is
the one the word's migration was tracking.

**Why did the meaning of *magic* move in that direction, at that time?**

I think the answer is that during the nineteenth century a number of people — Lévi first and
most consequentially — noticed something that had not previously been articulated: that the
ceremonial apparatus which had been understood for centuries as a means of acting on the
world was far more obviously effective at acting on the person performing it.

They did not conclude that magic was therefore psychological; most of them would have
rejected that. They concluded that the operator was the site of the operation, and they
rebuilt the theory around it.

**And once the theory was rebuilt, an enormous quantity of practice that had never called
itself magic suddenly fell inside the definition** — including a fraternal ritual in a tavern
that had been doing exactly this since the 1720s without a theory at all.

The nineteenth-century occultists who looked at Freemasonry and saw a magical order were
therefore doing something more defensible than they are usually credited with. **They were
not misreading Masonry. They were applying a newly available description to a practice that
met it.**

Their error was historical, not classificatory. They assumed that because Masonry fitted the
description, it must have descended from the tradition the description came from.

It did not. It arrived at the same place independently, which is the argument of Chapter 48
and of this entire book.

---

### The residue

So: does Masonic initiation qualify as magic?

**Under the pre-modern definition: no, clearly.**

**Under the post-Lévi definition: yes, and so does a great deal else, which is a problem for
the definition rather than for Masonry.**

**And under any definition requiring a supernatural mechanism: the question cannot be
answered by me, because nothing in my evidence bears on it, and I have declined to assert
anything either way for forty-nine chapters and am not going to start now.**

What I will assert is the thing the evidence does support, which is narrower and I think more
useful than a verdict.

**Freemasonry and ceremonial magic are not related by descent. They are related by having
independently discovered the same set of techniques for working on a human being** — and the
techniques are attention, embodiment, repetition, controlled information, social
ratification and an external standard.

Whether you call that magic is a decision about vocabulary.

That it works, in the limited senses Part XI could establish, is a finding about people.
