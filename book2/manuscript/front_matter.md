## A NOTE ON METHOD, OBLIGATION, AND WHAT THIS BOOK WILL NOT DO

Four things govern the pages that follow.

**On ritual.** Freemasonry is a body of men and women under obligation. I have not
reproduced private ritual material and I have not invented any. Everything described here
comes from sources that have been public for between eighty and three hundred years:
printed constitutions, the eighteenth-century exposures, published catechisms, the lecture
systems of Preston and Webb, and the twentieth-century publications of orders that no longer
exist. Where I describe a ceremony I am describing a documented historical text. **Masonic
ritual varies enormously by jurisdiction, by century and by rite. No working described in
this book is universal, and none is current.** A Mason reading these chapters may recognize
a great deal and may also find things that have never formed part of his experience. Both
reactions would be correct.

**On evidence.** Every claim here is meant to be checkable, and the claims are of different
kinds. Some are historical: people did this, believed that, wrote the other. Some are
sociological: an institution of this sort produces these effects, and the records show it.
Some are psychological, and those are hedged, because the literature they rest on was built
on other populations in another century. And some claims are not made at all. **I will not
tell you that Masonic ritual produces supernatural effects, and I will not tell you that it
does not.** Nothing in my evidence could settle that, and a historian who answers it has
changed profession without telling the reader.

**On borrowed science.** This book uses ritual theory, cognitive science and environmental
psychology to describe a ceremony none of those disciplines was built to describe. Van
Gennep published in 1909; Turner in 1969; the experimental program is younger than most
of its readers. Applying any of it to a London tavern in 1730 is an inference across two
centuries and several cultures. I have labeled every instance. Where a finding is
contested — and two of the most tempting findings in this whole subject are contested — I
say so in the text, not in a note where it can be missed.

**On sympathy.** The people in this book believed something was happening to them. Some of
them spent decades trying to describe it with no vocabulary to hand. Taking their reports
seriously enough to examine is not a form of condescension; it is the only way I know to
find out what actually occurred.
