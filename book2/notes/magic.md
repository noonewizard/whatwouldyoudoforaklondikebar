# NOTE — MAGIC

**The classification is in `research/magic_classification.md`. This note carries the
argument.**

**The migration.** The word did not stay still.

| Date | Meaning | Source |
|---|---|---|
| c. 300 | *Not* magic — theurgy, ritual ascent | Iamblichus |
| 1489 | Natural and astral medicine | Ficino |
| 1533 | Natural + celestial + ceremonial: philosophy completed | Agrippa |
| c. 1700 | What respectable knowledge is not | The period's polemic |
| **1854** | **An inner science of will, imagination and symbol; the operator transformed** | **Lévi** |
| 1904 | "Causing Change to occur in conformity with Will" — includes baking a cake | Crowley |

**The observation the whole book turns on.** A Freemasonry that was not magic under the 1489
definition became magic under the 1854 one **without altering a single ritual.**

That is a fact about the word. Whether it is also a fact about Freemasonry is Chapter 49's
question — and because both the practice and the definition are historical objects, the
question is answerable **without any metaphysical commitment at all.**

**The recurring denial.** Almost everyone in this history who performs what we would call
magic denies that it is magic. Iamblichus insists on theurgy. Martinez insists his
operations are religious. Lévi distinguishes *haute magie* from sorcery. The Golden Dawn
distinguishes itself from charlatans. Crowley respells the word. Freemasonry, accused,
denies it absolutely — and is telling the truth as it understands the term.

**Consequence:** "did they call it magic?" is nearly useless as a criterion, and gets the
history wrong in both directions.
