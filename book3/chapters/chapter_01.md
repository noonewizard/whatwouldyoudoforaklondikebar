# PART I — THE LOST THING

# CHAPTER ONE
## The Word That Was Lost

The sources are less mysterious than the literature about them, and the first job
of this book is to say what they actually contain.

Here is what we have. In the third degree of Craft Freemasonry, as it emerges in
the printed record of the 1720s and 1730s, a legend is enacted. A master builder
engaged in the construction of Solomon's Temple is approached by men who want
something from him. He refuses. They kill him. He is buried, sought, found, and
raised.

The something they wanted was a word.

He would not give it, and he died, and with him it was lost. At the point in the
ceremony where the candidate might expect to receive it, he is told that it cannot
be given, because nobody has it. He is given a substitute instead, and the
substitution is explicit. He is not deceived about what he is holding.[^1]

That is the whole of it. Everything else — the Egyptian derivations, the
Tetragrammaton identifications, the cipher reconstructions — is commentary, and all
of it is later.

---

### What the documents say, and when

The chronology matters more here than anywhere else in the book, so it is worth
being exact.

**The Mason Word exists in Scotland before 1700.** We know this not from ritual
texts, which do not survive from that period, but from references by people outside
the system who found it curious. A word possessed by masons, transmitted privately,
functioning as a mark of membership. No drama attaches to it. Nothing is lost.[^2]

**The earliest recoverable ritual is the Edinburgh Register House manuscript of
1696**, with two close relatives from around 1700 and 1714. These give us a
catechism — a sequence of questions and answers held by heart — together with a
word and a grip. There are two degrees. There is no Hiram, no murder, no loss and
no substitution. The word is simply a word that Masons have and others do not.[^3]

**The third degree appears in the 1720s.** Prichard's *Masonry Dissected* of 1730
is the first full printed account, and it contains the legend in recognisable
form.[^4] Between the catechisms and Prichard lies a gap of roughly three decades
in which something was built.

This is the single most consequential fact in the book and it is worth stating
without ornament: **the loss of the word is younger than the word by at least a
century, and younger than the earliest Masonic documents by three hundred years.**

Any account that derives the Lost Word from Hebrew name-mysticism, Egyptian
mystery religion or a Templar survival has to carry that material across sixteen
centuries or more and deliver it into a London tavern in the 1720s. The carrying is
the part that is never demonstrated. What is offered instead is resemblance — and
Book I of this series spent four hundred pages on why resemblance is not descent.

---

### The shape of the problem

Set the history aside for a moment and look at the structure, because the structure
is what has kept people interested.

A system of initiation exists to confer. That is what it is for. The candidate
arrives without something and leaves with it, and every initiatory tradition in the
world works this way: a name, a status, a knowledge, a mark, a garment. The first
two Masonic degrees do exactly this. The candidate arrives ignorant and is given
words, signs, tools, and instruction in what they mean.

Then the third degree does the opposite.

It brings the candidate to the summit of the system, through the most elaborate
ceremony in the Craft, and at the moment of conferral it withholds — not out of
policy, but out of *incapacity*. The institution does not have the thing. It says
so. It hands over a placeholder with the word "substitute" attached, and it has
gone on doing this, unchanged in essentials, for three centuries.

I have looked for comparable structures and they are rare. Traditions keep sacred
words; traditions restrict them; traditions defer them to further stages. Very few
traditions stage the loss of one and then decline to restore it.

**And Masonry itself did not find this comfortable.** Within about twenty years a
further degree had appeared in which the word is recovered — the Royal Arch, whose
subject is precisely the finding of what the third degree says is gone. The
institution generated its own answer to its own refusal, which tells us that the
refusal was felt as an incompleteness by the people inside it.[^5]

That is what a designed absence looks like from the inside. It itches.

---

### Four questions, and only one of them is good

The literature asks the first three. This book asks the fourth.

**"What was the word?"** — assumes there was one. The evidence does not support
that assumption. There is no eighteenth-century document identifying a word that
was subsequently lost, because the loss is a feature of a *legend*, not a report of
an event. Asking what the word was is like asking what was in the box Pandora
opened. The question has a grammatical form that suggests an answer and no
referent to supply one.

**"Can it be recovered?"** — assumes it went somewhere. Words do not.

**"What does it symbolise?"** — a better question, and the one Masonic commentary
has answered for two hundred years, with answers that multiply rather than converge.
Truth. The divine name. Moral character. Lost innocence. The knowledge of God.
Consciousness. Identity. The proliferation is itself evidence, and Chapter 21 of
this book treats it as such: *when a tradition's own sources cannot agree what a
symbol means, the most economical explanation is that it arrived without a settled
meaning.*

**"What would it mean for a word to be lost?"** — this is the question that goes
somewhere, and it goes somewhere unexpected.

---

### The one place a word really was lost

Before dismissing the category, it is worth looking at the nearest thing to a
genuine case, because it shows what a real loss looks like and how different it is.

**Etruscan.**

The language was spoken in central Italy for centuries, written in an alphabet
derived from Greek, and left thousands of inscriptions. The script is entirely
legible — anyone can transliterate an Etruscan inscription and say what sounds it
records.

**And the language is largely not understood**, because it is an isolate with no
close relative, and the surviving corpus is overwhelmingly short formulaic
material: funerary inscriptions, dedications, names and dates. There is no
literature, no bilingual text of any length, and nothing that would let the
vocabulary be reconstructed.

**That is a genuine loss and note its shape.** Not the letters — those survive. Not
the sounds — the script records them. What is gone is the **connection between the
words and what they meant**, because the community that held that connection ceased
to exist and wrote nothing down that would preserve it.

**And it is unrecoverable by ingenuity.** No transformation applied to Etruscan
inscriptions will yield their meaning. Only new evidence would — a substantial
bilingual text, which is why the discovery of the Pyrgi tablets in 1964, with
parallel Etruscan and Phoenician, was such an event.

**Apply this to the Masonic case and the contrast is total.** The Masonic word is
not an untranslatable string from a dead language. It is a word in a known language,
in continuous use, transmitted without interruption, whose meaning nobody has ever
doubted.

**What the third degree says is missing is not meaning.** It is something else, and
Chapter 18 spends its length on what.

---

### The thing that cannot be lost

A word is not an object. This sounds like a quibble and it is the hinge of the
book.

Objects can be mislaid, destroyed, stolen, buried. Words have none of these
properties. A word is a pattern — of sound, of letters, of use — and patterns are
not located anywhere, which means they cannot be removed from anywhere.

Take the clearest case, which Part III examines at length. The Tetragrammaton, the
four-letter name of God in the Hebrew Bible, is routinely described as lost. It is
not lost. Its four letters — yod, he, waw, he — have been continuously legible in
manuscripts and printed Bibles for as long as either has existed. Any reader of
Hebrew can point to them. Nothing about the string is missing.

What is unavailable is how to pronounce it, and the reason is prosaic: Hebrew was
written without vowels, the oral tradition of pronunciation was deliberately
allowed to lapse as a mark of reverence, and by the time anyone might have wanted
to write the vowels down there was no one authorised to say what they were. This is
not a mystery. It is the ordinary fate of any word in a consonantal script whose
speakers stop saying it.

And behind the pronunciation, something larger went. The Name had a setting: a
Temple, a priesthood, one day in the year, one man permitted to speak it. That
apparatus was destroyed in 70 CE. Even a person who somehow knew the vowels would
not be able to *do* what the High Priest did, because the doing required a building
that is not there and an office that no longer exists.

**The letters survived. The world in which they meant something did not.**

That is the model this book will apply to every case it examines, and it will fit
almost all of them. When a tradition says a word is lost, look for what actually
went missing. It will be the pronunciation, or the authority to use it, or the
context that made it work, or the condition of the person who would speak it.

The word, nine times in ten, is sitting there in plain sight.

---

### What follows

If that is right, then the Masonic drama is not about missing information.

A system whose climax is the announcement that a word is unavailable, followed by
the provision of a substitute that is labelled as one, is a system making a
distinction — between having a word and having whatever else one might have. It is
telling the candidate, in the only vocabulary available to a ritual, that
possession of a string is not the same as possession of what the string stands for.

Whether anyone in the 1720s intended that is a separate question, and Chapter 18
argues that they almost certainly did not. The legend has the shape it has for
reasons that are probably narrative rather than philosophical: a murdered man
cannot pass on what he knew, and the substitution follows from the plot.

But structures outlive their reasons. A thing assembled for one purpose can turn
out to do another, and three centuries of men have taken from this ceremony an idea
that may not have been put into it. That is not a scandal. It is how symbols work,
and Chapter 2 begins with the strangest of them, which is the one people are given
at birth.
