# CHAPTER TWO
## A Name in the Dark

Everyone reading this has a word that belongs to them in a way no other word does.

It was given before you could object. You did not choose it, you cannot easily
change it, and it does not describe you. It is not an adjective. It carries no
information about your height or temperament or competence. If you were entirely
different in every respect, it would still be your name.

And yet hearing it spoken across a crowded room does something that hearing any
other word does not.

---

### Names are not descriptions

For most of the history of philosophy this was thought to be an illusion. The
dominant view, running from the late nineteenth century through the middle of the
twentieth, held that a name is a compressed description — that "Aristotle" means
something like *the pupil of Plato who taught Alexander*, and that the name refers
to whoever satisfies enough of that bundle.

The view is elegant and it is wrong, and the demonstration that it is wrong is
worth a paragraph because it bears directly on what follows.

Suppose everything we believe about Aristotle turned out to be false — the pupil of
Plato was someone else, the tutor of Alexander a third party, and the works
attributed to him written by a committee. On the description theory, "Aristotle"
would then refer to whoever *did* those things. But that is not how the name
behaves. We would say instead: *we were wrong about Aristotle.* The name keeps
pointing at the man even as every description attached to it fails.[^1]

Saul Kripke's account of why runs like this. A name is fixed to its bearer by an
initial act — a baptism, in his term, though it need not be religious — and then
passed from speaker to speaker in a chain of use, each link intending to refer to
whatever the previous link referred to. The name is a **causal chain reaching back
to an act of naming**, not a bundle of facts.

Three consequences follow, and all three matter for a tradition that treats words
as more than information.

**A name can survive the loss of everything known about its bearer.** The chain
holds even when the descriptions are stripped away.

**A name establishes a relation rather than conveying a content.** To know
someone's name is not to know anything *about* them. It is to be connected to them
in a particular way: you can now address them, call them, summon their attention
across a room.

**And the act of naming is irreversible in an odd way.** The chain can be forgotten
but it cannot be undone. Once the link is made, the name points where it points.

---

### Why this matters for sacred words

Now read the magical and religious material with that in hand, because it stops
looking naive.

A tradition that holds a name to confer power over its bearer is not making a
category error. It is generalising, perhaps too far, from something that is
genuinely true of names: **a name is an instrument of address.** To have someone's
name is to be able to call them. In ordinary social life this is unremarkable and
constantly useful — it is why we learn the names of people we want things from,
and why a stranger using your name is faintly unsettling.

Extend that to a cosmos populated by agents — angels, spirits, powers, a God who
answers — and a name becomes exactly what you would need. Not information about the
power, but a handle on it. The grimoire tradition that Part VI examines is built on
this logic, and the logic is internally impeccable. Whether there is anyone at the
other end of the line is a different question, and not one this book will answer.

The point for now is narrower. **A tradition treating names as power is reasoning
from the structure of naming, not from superstition.** Whether the reasoning
generalises is the question, and it is a real question rather than an obvious
mistake.

---

### The theory the ancients already had

The argument was live in antiquity, and Plato staged it.

In the *Cratylus*, two positions are put. Hermogenes holds that names are
conventional — arbitrary tokens agreed upon, with no natural fit between word and
thing. Cratylus holds that names are natural — that a correct name somehow captures
its object, and that language, properly understood, tells you about the world.

Socrates does what Socrates does and makes trouble for both, spending a long
stretch of the dialogue on etymologies that range from the ingenious to the
transparently absurd. Readers have disagreed for two millennia about how much of
that is serious.[^2]

What matters here is that the dispute exists and that it never closes. **Every
tradition in this book takes a position on it, usually without noticing.**

A view that the letters of the Hebrew alphabet are the elements from which the
world was made is a Cratylan view, and a strong one. A view that sacred names are
effective because of what they sound like is Cratylan. So is any claim that a word
transformed by gematria or Atbash reveals a truth about its referent — the whole
apparatus assumes that the letters are not arbitrary, that they carry something,
that the spelling is a fact about the thing rather than a fact about the language.

Against this: the plain observation that Hebrew, Greek and English give different
names to the same objects, that the same object has different numerical values in
each, and that transformations performed in one language do not survive translation
into another. **A cipher that only works in the language you happen to be reading
is telling you about the language, not about the world.**

The reader should keep this distinction in hand for Part IX, where it does most of
its work.

---

### The name you are given and the name you take

A distinction that matters for what Masonry does, and which the philosophical
literature on naming tends to pass over because it is social rather than semantic.

**Most names are imposed.** You did not choose yours. It was conferred by others,
before you could object, and the conferring established a chain that you inherited
rather than joined.

**Some names are assumed.** A religious name taken at profession. A regnal name. A
pen name. A name taken at conversion, or on entering an order.

**The difference is not semantic — both refer perfectly well — but social, and it is
large.** An assumed name marks a discontinuity: the person who bears it is
presenting themselves as, in some respect, not the person who bore the previous one.
Every tradition that renames at initiation is using this.

**Masonry does something in between, and the in-between is interesting.**

A candidate keeps his name. He is not renamed, and the Craft has no tradition of
religious names or motto-names of the kind several other initiatory bodies use.

**But he is given a designation** — Entered Apprentice, Fellow Craft, Master Mason —
which is conferred rather than assumed, applies only within the institution, and
changes as he progresses.

**And he is given words which are neither.** The word of a degree is not a name for
him; it does not designate him, and he does not answer to it. It is something he
*carries*, and its function is to be produced on demand.

**Three different relations to language, in one ceremony**: a personal name he keeps,
a status name he receives, and a token he holds. The commentary tradition has
frequently merged the second and third, treating the Masonic word as though it
described the candidate's spiritual condition.

**It does not.** The degree name classifies him. The word identifies him. Chapter 8
sets out why those are different operations, and keeping them apart resolves a good
deal of confusion about what the Lost Word could possibly have signified.

---

### Address, and the dark

Return to the room.

The candidate at the threshold is in darkness, and the first thing that happens to
him is that he is asked a question. Who comes there. The question presupposes
something — that there is a *who*, that the who can be identified, that
identification is what the door turns on.

He answers with a formula rather than with his name, and this is worth noticing.
The formula asserts that he is a particular kind of person: one who has been
regularly prepared, who comes of his own free will, who seeks something. It is a
description, not a designation.

Then, inside, over the course of the ceremony, he is given words that function
differently. The word of the degree is not a description of him. It is a token he
carries, and it works the way names work: it establishes a relation, it can be
used to gain address, and knowing it connects him to a chain of people who knew it
before.

**The Masonic word is much more like a name than like a statement.** It tells you
nothing. It puts you in a relation.

And the third degree then does the thing this book keeps returning to. It tells him
that the highest of those tokens is unavailable — that the chain, at its far end,
is broken. Someone died holding it, and the causal chain that a name requires was
cut.

That is a coherent thing to say about a name, and it is the only kind of thing that
*can* be lost about one. Not the letters. The chain.

---

### The dark part

One more observation before Part I moves on, and it belongs here rather than
anywhere else.

Names work best in the dark.

This is not mystical. In good light we identify people by face, gait, dress,
posture — a rich channel that needs no words. Remove the light and all of that
goes, and what remains is voice and word. Every system for identifying people in
conditions of poor information converges on the same solution: a challenge and a
response, spoken. Sentries. Passwords. Telephones before caller identification. The
Masonic catechism.

**Darkness is what makes a name necessary**, and a tradition that hoodwinks its
candidate before asking who he is has arranged, whether or not it understood this,
for the question to be asked under exactly the conditions where the answer carries
the whole weight.

He cannot be seen. He can only be heard. What he says is all there is of him.
