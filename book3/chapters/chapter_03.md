# CHAPTER THREE
## The Power of Saying

In 1955 a philosopher at Harvard pointed out something that everybody had always
known and nobody had written down.

J. L. Austin's observation was this. We assume that the job of a sentence is to
describe — to say how things are, truly or falsely. But a great many sentences do
nothing of the kind. *I promise to pay you Tuesday* is not a report about a
promise; it is the promise. *I name this ship Britannia* does not describe a naming;
it performs one. *I do*, said at the right moment in the right building by a person
in the right relation to another, does not report a marriage. It makes one.[^1]

Austin called these **performatives**, and his point was that for such utterances
the question of truth does not arise. A promise is not true or false. It is made or
not made, and whether it is made depends on a set of conditions having nothing to
do with correspondence to facts.

---

### The conditions

What makes a performative work is the part that matters here, because it is exactly
the part that can be lost.

Austin's account requires an accepted conventional procedure, with a conventional
effect. It requires the particular persons and circumstances to be the appropriate
ones. It requires the procedure to be executed correctly and completely. And it
requires, where the procedure involves intentions, that the participants actually
have them.

Fail any of these and the utterance does not fail as a *description*. It fails as
an *act* — it is, in Austin's term, **infelicitous**. It misfires.

This gives us a vocabulary for something the previous chapter reached and could not
name.

Consider a man who says *I now pronounce you husband and wife* in a registry office
where he does not work, to two people who did not ask, having wandered in off the
street. Every word is correct. The sentence is perfectly formed. Nothing has
happened.

He has the words and not the authority, and the distinction between those two things
is the subject of this book.

---

### Four ways to fail

Set out systematically, because they will recur:

**Wrong words.** The formula is misremembered or corrupted. The easiest failure to
detect and the least interesting.

**Wrong speaker.** The words are right; the person is not authorised. This is the
registry office case, and it is the commonest failure in the material this book
examines.

**Wrong setting.** Right words, right person, wrong place or time or circumstance.
A judge pronouncing sentence at a dinner party has not sentenced anyone.

**Wrong condition.** The speaker is not in the state the procedure requires — has
not undergone what must be undergone, does not intend what must be intended, is
not qualified in whatever way the procedure specifies.

**All four are losses, and none of them is the loss of a word.**

That is the observation this chapter exists to make. When we say a word has been
lost, we almost never mean that the letters have gone missing. We mean one of the
other three: the authority has lapsed, the setting has been destroyed, or the
condition can no longer be attained.

---

### The Temple case

The clearest historical instance is the one Part III will treat at length, and it
is worth previewing because it shows all four categories at once.

The High Priest of the Jerusalem Temple, on the Day of Atonement, pronounced the
divine Name. Consider what that act required.

The **words**: the Name, correctly pronounced, which required a living oral
tradition, since the script supplied no vowels.

The **speaker**: the High Priest, holding an office with specific descent
requirements and a specific consecration.

The **setting**: the Holy of Holies, in the Temple, on one day of the year.

The **condition**: ritual purity, attained by a prescribed sequence, without which
the whole thing was void regardless of everything else.

In 70 CE the Temple was destroyed, and with it the office, the day's procedure, and
the setting. The oral tradition of pronunciation, already restricted, lapsed within
a few generations.

Notice that **the letters were never in danger.** They are in every Torah scroll.
The loss was total and it did not touch the word at all.

A modern person who somehow learned the correct vowels would be in the position of
the man in the registry office. He would have the words and nothing else.

---

### The Masonic case, read this way

Now the third degree, with Austin in hand.

What the ceremony stages is a **failure of transmission**. A man who held something
was killed before he could pass it on. The chain broke.

Read as a claim about information, this is odd: information is not usually
destroyed by the death of one person, and a system serious about preserving
something would not vest it in a single life. Read as a claim about **authority**,
it is exactly right. Authority is precisely the sort of thing that *can* die with a
person, because it is not a property of the words but of the relation between a
person and an institution. When the last man authorised to confer something dies
without conferring it, the thing is gone — even though every word involved remains
perfectly available.

And the substitute makes sense in the same frame. A substituted word is a
**provisional performative**: a formula adopted by a body that acknowledges it
lacks the real one, used to carry on the work in the meantime. It is not a lie. It
is an explicit declaration of infelicity, built into the ceremony.

The Craft says, in effect: *we are performing this without the authority that would
make it complete, we know we are, and we are telling you.*

I know of no other initiatory tradition that does this, and it is a remarkably
honest piece of design — whether or not anyone designed it.

---

### What speech can and cannot do

A caution, because the performative material is easily overextended and frequently
is.

Austin's category covers utterances whose effects are **conventional**. The marriage
is real because a society has agreed on a procedure and recognizes its outcome. The
verdict binds because a legal system enforces it. The promise obliges because we
have a practice of holding people to promises. **In every case the power lies in
the convention, not in the sound.**

This is where the performative account parts company with the magical one. A
magical utterance is held to work *on the world* — to alter a substance, compel a
spirit, cause an event — and no convention mediates. The sound itself is supposed
to do it.

Austin's performatives do not support that claim and cannot be used to argue for
it. What they establish is that a certain class of words changes a certain class of
facts: social facts, institutional facts, facts about status and obligation and
relation. That is a real and large domain, and the Masonic word lives entirely
inside it. A man who gives the word correctly is admitted; the fact created is a
social one; the mechanism is convention, and no one in a lodge has ever claimed
otherwise.

**The magical word acts on the world. The performative word acts on a society. The
Masonic word is the second kind, and Part VI will show how clean the distinction
stays under pressure.**

---

### Why the substitute is not a lie

A question that occurs to most people who encounter the third degree, and which the
performative framework answers cleanly.

**If the word is a substitute, is the ceremony deceiving anybody?**

No, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of good intentions.

**Deception requires a false representation.** The candidate is told, explicitly,
that what he is receiving stands in place of something unavailable. The
representation is accurate. He knows what he has and he knows what it is not.

Compare the alternatives the compilers could have taken. They could have given him a
word and called it the Master's Word, which would have been a false representation
and which nobody could have contradicted. They could have promised it in a further
degree and charged for it, which the Continent did on an industrial scale within
twenty years.

**They did neither, and the declaration is in the ceremony.**

**In Austin's terms, the Craft declares its own infelicity.** It performs a rite
while announcing that one of the conditions for the full performance is not met, and
it tells the person being acted upon.

**This is a rarer institutional posture than it sounds.** Most bodies in this
position conceal the gap — the succession is asserted to be unbroken, the authority
to be intact, the transmission to be complete. Declaring an incompleteness invites
exactly the question that institutions least want asked.

**And the preceding volume noted the same restraint elsewhere in the Craft.** At the
summit of the system there are no hidden masters, no unknown superiors, no secret
chiefs. Every order that came after filled that space — von Hund with Unknown
Superiors, Blavatsky with Masters, Mathers with Secret Chiefs. The Craft left it
empty and said so.

**Whether that reflects honesty or merely a lack of opportunity cannot be settled**,
and Chapter 18 declines to settle it. What can be said is that the design held, and
that a ceremony which tells a man the truth about what he is receiving has done
something most ceremonies do not.

---

### The obligation

One last application, and it is the strongest instance in the Craft.

The central speech act of Masonic initiation is not the word. It is the obligation
— a promise, taken aloud, in the presence of witnesses, in a prescribed form, by a
person who has been brought into a condition the procedure specifies.

Every one of Austin's conditions is met, deliberately and with care. There is an
accepted procedure. The persons are appropriate — the candidate has been proposed,
balloted and prepared; the officers hold their offices. The execution is exact, and
lodges take considerable trouble over exactness. And the intention is checked
explicitly: he is asked, more than once, whether he comes of his own free will.

**The result is a real obligation**, in precisely the sense a promise or an oath is
real, and Book II of this series found that it was the one place where Masonic
ritual produces effects visible in the documentary record — in charity accounts, in
disciplinary proceedings, in money actually paid.

A ceremony that stages the loss of a word also contains a word that unambiguously
works. The contrast is not accidental and the book will return to it.
