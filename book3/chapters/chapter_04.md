# CHAPTER FOUR
## Silence

There is a moment in Masonic initiation when nothing is said, and it lasts longer
than a modern person finds comfortable.

The candidate has been brought into the room. He cannot see. He has answered the
questions at the door and he is now somewhere in the middle of a space whose shape
he does not know, surrounded by men whose number he cannot estimate, and for a
period the ceremony simply does not fill the air.

Nothing is happening. Everything is happening.

---

### Silence is not the absence of information

Start with a technical point that will earn its keep later.

In the mathematical theory of communication, information is not content — it is the
reduction of uncertainty. A message carries information to the extent that it rules
out possibilities the receiver was entertaining. A message that tells you what you
already knew carries none at all, however long it is.[^1]

This has a consequence that sounds paradoxical and is not. **A silence can carry
information, if the listener knows that speech was possible.** The sentry who does
not challenge, the witness who does not deny, the friend who does not congratulate:
each silence rules out possibilities, and each is therefore informative in the
strict sense.

The condition is that the silence must be *marked*. It must occur where speech was
expected. Silence in an empty field is nothing. Silence in a courtroom after a
question is data.

Ritual silence is marked silence, and it is marked by the most reliable method
available: it is surrounded by speech. A ceremony that is continuously verbal
except in certain places has made those places carry weight without putting
anything in them.

---

### Four things silence does

**It makes the next thing audible.** The most obvious and the most underrated. A
word spoken into a silence that has lasted thirty seconds is not the same stimulus
as the same word in continuous conversation. The preceding volume of this series
argued that Masonic ritual repeatedly manufactures the deficit it then relieves —
darkness before light, restriction before investiture — and silence before speech
is the same mechanism in the auditory channel.

**It removes the listener's bearings.** Human beings orient in a room partly by
sound. Remove it and you remove a channel. A hoodwinked candidate in a silent room
has been deprived of both of the senses he would ordinarily use to work out where
he is and how many people are present, which leaves him with exactly what the
ceremony intends to give him: the voice, when it comes, and nothing else.

**It signals that a boundary has been crossed.** Ordinary sociability is noisy.
People arriving at a lodge meeting talk; they talk at dinner afterward. The
silence in between is one of the clearest markers that the room has changed status
— which is Jonathan Z. Smith's point about sacred space, that sacrality is a matter
of placement and designation rather than of substance.[^2] Silence is one of the
cheapest and most effective ways of declaring a designation.

**And it withholds.** This is the one this book cares about most.

---

### Withholding as a system

A tradition can control information in exactly two ways. It can restrict who is
told, or it can restrict what is said at all.

The first is the obvious one and it is the one that fails. Restricting who is told
requires that the tellers be reliable, that the told be discreet, and that nobody
write anything down — and Masonic history is a three-hundred-year demonstration
that these conditions cannot be maintained. The ritual was printed in 1730 by a man
who had been through it, reprinted constantly, translated, sold at bookstalls. The
institution did not collapse, which tells us that this was never where the weight
was.

The second is more interesting, because it cannot fail in the same way. **Material
that is never articulated cannot be disclosed.**

And a large part of what Masonic ritual conveys is of this kind. Not concealed —
simply not put into words by anyone, including the people who have it. The manner
of a ceremony. The timing. What the room feels like when it is done well and what
is wrong when it is done badly. The judgment a Past Master exercises when he
prompts a nervous officer. None of this is secret. All of it is unwritten, and most
of it is unwritable.

Michael Polanyi's term for this is **tacit knowledge** — the category of things we
know and cannot state.[^3] His examples are ordinary: recognizing a face,
riding a bicycle, knowing that a chemical reaction has gone wrong from the look of
it. The knowledge is real, it is demonstrable, it is transmissible by
apprenticeship, and it does not survive being written down because it was never in
propositional form.

**A tradition that transmits tacit knowledge is immune to exposure**, and not
because it guards anything. There is nothing to guard. The exposures printed
everything sayable, and everything sayable turned out not to be the point.

---

### The two silences

It is worth separating two things that get merged, because the distinction runs
through the rest of the book.

**Silence about what could be said.** Masonic obligations restrict disclosure of
certain matters. This is a policy, it is enforceable by the institution, and it has
been comprehensively ineffective at keeping the material out of print. It is
secrecy in the ordinary sense: information exists, and people are asked not to
share it.

**Silence about what cannot be said.** The ritual does not explain itself. It
presents images and declines to gloss them. No authority within the Craft
adjudicates what the symbols mean — a consequence, as the preceding volume argued,
of Anderson's 1723 settlement removing doctrinal authority in order to admit men of
different religions.

The second silence is far more consequential than the first, and it is the reason
the interpretive literature is infinite. **A tradition that explains its symbols
produces commentary that converges. A tradition that does not produces commentary
that multiplies**, and every generation adds a layer that nobody has the standing
to remove.

---

### The unsayable and the merely unsaid

A necessary caution, because this is where the material becomes dangerous.

There is an enormous difference between *this cannot be put into words* and *this
has not been put into words*, and mystical literature has traded on the confusion
for centuries.

The first is a real category with real examples. Tacit knowledge is genuinely
tacit. The taste of a particular wine cannot be conveyed to someone who has not
tasted it. These are honest cases of inarticulacy.

The second is often an evasion. A claim that cannot be stated cannot be checked,
and a tradition that says *the real teaching is beyond words* has placed itself
beyond criticism at no cost. The move is available to anybody and the sincere and
the fraudulent make it in identical language.

**How to tell them apart**, and this is the test the book will keep using: genuine
tacit knowledge has a public face. The joiner cannot say how he knows the joint is
right, but the joint either holds or it does not. The wine-taster is tested blind.
In every honest case, the inability to *state* the knowledge coexists with an
ability to *demonstrate* it, assessable by people who do not have it.

Apply this to Masonry and the result is split, cleanly.

**Ritual competence passes.** A Master Mason can perform the ceremony, be examined,
be judged by people who know. This is tacit knowledge in Polanyi's exact sense,
transmitted by apprenticeship, not acquirable from print — and the exposures proved
it by experiment, publishing everything and transferring nothing.

**Transformation of the person does not pass.** There is no performance to assess,
no criterion, no examination. Whether the ceremony changed a man is a claim with no
public face at all, and the honest position, which the preceding volume reached and
this one inherits, is that the question stays open because its truth and its falsity
produce identical evidence.

---

### Silence and the impossibility of checking

One further function, and it is the one with the sharpest implications for this
book's later parts.

**Silence removes the possibility of correction.**

A tradition that states its doctrines can be argued with. Its claims can be compared
across generations, checked against sources, found inconsistent. Statement creates a
surface that criticism can get purchase on.

**A tradition that shows and does not explain offers no such surface.**

This is not an accusation of evasion. Masonic ritual does not explain itself for a
documented institutional reason: Anderson's 1723 settlement removed doctrinal
authority in order to admit men of differing religions, and a body that cannot
adjudicate religion cannot adjudicate the meaning of its own symbols either.

**But the consequence is the same whatever the motive.** Nobody can be wrong about
what the rough ashlar means, because nothing authorises a correction.

**Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions.**

**The tradition became extraordinarily accommodating.** A Christian, a Jew, a deist
and a skeptic can all work the same ceremony and take from it whatever their
convictions allow. This is why Masonry spread across confessional boundaries that
almost nothing else crossed in the eighteenth century, and it is a genuine
achievement.

**And the tradition became extraordinarily vulnerable to annexation.** If no reading
can be ruled out, then the occult reading cannot be ruled out either, and Chapter 35
describes what happened when the nineteenth century arrived with a system looking
for a home.

**The vacancy that made the Craft universal is the vacancy that made it
colonisable.** Those are not two facts about Freemasonry. They are one fact, and
this book will meet it repeatedly.

---

### Back in the room

The candidate is still standing in the dark, and nobody has spoken for a while.

What is being done to him is not concealment. Everything about to happen to him has
been in print for three centuries and he could have read it that afternoon. Many
candidates have.

What is being done is **the manufacture of attention**, by the simple method of
removing everything else. He has no sight and, for the moment, no sound. He has no
task. He cannot orient himself. There is nothing for his mind to do except wait for
whatever comes next, which means that whatever comes next will land on a surface
that has been cleared for it.

Then a voice speaks, and because of the silence it has the whole of him.
