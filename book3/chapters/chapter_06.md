# PART II — WORDS OF CREATION

# CHAPTER SIX
## In the Beginning

The first thing God does in the Hebrew Bible is speak.

Not build, not shape, not beget. *And God said, let there be light.* The verb is
ordinary — *amar*, the same word used when one person says something to another —
and what follows it is the universe.

This is the passage standing behind every claim in this book that words make
things, and it is worth reading carefully, because what it actually says is
stranger and more restrained than what has been built on it.

---

### What Genesis 1 does and does not claim

Read the chapter as a sequence and a pattern emerges. God speaks; the thing comes
into being; God sees that it is good; God names it. Light, then day and night. The
firmament, then heaven. The gathering of waters, then earth and seas.

Three observations that the interpretive tradition tends to skip.

**The speech is not a formula.** There is no incantation, no special language, no
name of power. The sentences are plain declaratives in ordinary Hebrew. Whatever is
doing the work here, it is not the phonetics.

**The speech is not addressed to anything.** God is not commanding an existing
substance to rearrange itself, or summoning a power, or negotiating with a rival.
There is no second party. This is a significant difference from the surrounding
literature, and it is where Genesis is doing something that its neighbours are not.

**And naming follows creation rather than accomplishing it.** God makes the light
and *then* calls it day. The name is a subsequent act, and Genesis 2 will hand that
act to the human being.

The text is doing something quite specific. It is asserting that the world exists
because of an intention expressed, and that the expressing is effortless. No
struggle, no combat, no dismembered corpse of a defeated god. The restraint is the
theology.

---

### The neighbours

Comparison here is legitimate and it is also where a great deal of overreach
begins, so the rules from Chapter 5 apply from the outset.

The **Memphite Theology**, an Egyptian text surviving on the Shabaka Stone, has the
god Ptah bringing things into being through the thought of the heart and the
utterance of the tongue. The parallel to Genesis is genuine and has been noticed
for a century.[^1]

The **Enuma Elish**, the Babylonian creation epic, contains a test of divine power
in which Marduk destroys and restores a garment by command alone — speech
demonstrated as efficacious before the world is made from a defeated body.[^2]

Now the questions. *Chronology*: both predate the final form of Genesis 1, so the
direction is at least possible. *Transmission*: Israel was in contact with both
Egypt and Mesopotamia, extensively and demonstrably, so a route exists. This is one
of the rare cases in this book where the first two tests pass cleanly.

*Intertextuality* is where it gets harder. There is no verbal dependence — no
distinctive shared vocabulary, no quotation, none of the fingerprints that
establish textual descent. What is shared is a **topos**: the idea that divine
speech can be creative.

And the differences are structural rather than decorative. Enuma Elish builds the
world from a corpse after a war; Genesis has no conflict and no raw material
requiring conquest. Ptah's utterance works within a pantheon; Genesis has no one to
speak to.

**The honest verdict is a shared cultural inheritance rather than a borrowing.**
Israel was part of an ancient Near Eastern world in which the notion of creative
divine speech was available, and Genesis 1 uses it while stripping out the elements
it does not want. That is a real historical relation. It is not the "one continuous
secret tradition" that this book's brief warns against, and it will not support the
weight placed on it by anyone wanting to run a line from Egypt to the third degree.

---

### The Psalms, and what the ancient Hebrew "word" is

Two supports worth noting because they specify the idea.

*By the word of the LORD were the heavens made; and all the host of them by the
breath of his mouth* (Ps 33:6). Note the parallelism, which is the fundamental
device of Hebrew poetry: *word* stands beside *breath*, the two halves saying the
same thing twice. Speech and exhalation are the same act, and the Hebrew *ruach*
carries breath, wind and spirit together.

And the term itself. Hebrew ***davar*** — the ordinary word for "word" — also means
*thing*, *matter*, *affair*, *event*. The same noun covers the utterance and what
the utterance is about. When the phrase *the word of the LORD came to* a prophet,
what arrives is not a message in the modern sense but something closer to an event.

**This is the single most useful philological fact in Part II**, and it is worth
holding for the rest of the book. A Hebrew "word" is closer to a *deed* than a
Greek one. A tradition operating in a language where word and thing are one noun
will not find it strange that saying can be doing — and a tradition inheriting that
material in translation, where the two have come apart, may hear a metaphysical
claim where the original had a linguistic convenience.

How much weight to put on this is a real question. There is a well-known temptation
to read the structure of a language as the theology of its speakers, and to argue
from *davar* to a Hebrew mind for which words were forces. That argument has been
made often and it has been criticised sharply, and the criticism is right: the
range of a noun tells us about a language's history, not about what its speakers
believed.[^3] The fact is genuine; the inference is not automatic.

---

### Why this is in a book about Freemasonry

A caution, stated now because Part II runs for four chapters and the reader is
entitled to know where it is going.

**None of this material is a source for Masonic ritual.** The Craft does not quote
Genesis 1. Its scriptural furniture comes from Kings and Chronicles: pillars, a
temple, a builder, measurements. It is architectural, not cosmological, and there
is no Masonic tradition of creation by speech.

What this material is, is **the air the candidate breathed**.

A man in a London lodge in 1730 who heard a ceremony about a lost Word did not
arrive as a blank. He had heard Genesis read aloud since childhood. He had heard
the prologue to John. He knew, without having to be taught, that *the Word* was a
phrase with weight, and the weight attached itself to the Masonic usage whether or
not anyone put it there.

This is the distinction that governs the whole book and it appears here in its
cleanest form. **The meaning is real and the intention is unevidenced.** A candidate
who heard theological resonance in the word "Word" was not misunderstanding the
ceremony. He was doing what any English speaker of his century would do with that
noun. But his hearing is evidence about him, not about the men who wrote the
ritual — and the literature has spent two centuries treating it as though it were
evidence about them.

---

### Speech, and what it does not require

An observation about Genesis 1 that is easy to miss and that bears on everything in
Part VI.

**Divine speech in Genesis requires no technique, no preparation and no
conditions.**

Compare what the magical traditions of Chapter 28 specify. The operator must fast,
wash, abstain, prepare instruments, consecrate a space, observe planetary hours,
pronounce with precision. **Dozens of conditions, any of which may void the
operation.**

Genesis has none. God says, and it is.

**This asymmetry is theologically deliberate and it is worth naming.** A power that
requires technique is a power constrained by something — by the nature of the
materials, by the cooperation of other agents, by the correctness of procedure. The
Genesis account is specifically refusing to describe a constrained power.

**And it has a consequence that runs through this entire book.** The text describes
creative speech and supplies no method, which means that **nothing in it can be
imitated.** There is no formula to learn, no sequence to reproduce, no name to
pronounce.

Contrast the traditions that *do* supply methods. Every one of them, from the Greek
papyri to the grimoires, offers procedures — because a tradition teaching
practitioners must give them something to do.

**Genesis is not teaching practitioners.** It is making a claim about how things came
to be, addressed to a community that is not being invited to try it.

**Which is why the later appropriation had to add so much.** When Hebrew divine
names enter the magical literature, they arrive accompanied by an apparatus the
Hebrew Bible does not contain and would not have countenanced: hours, seals,
circles, compulsions. The names were taken; the restraint was left behind.

That is a datable transformation, and Chapter 28 traces it.

---

### The thing Genesis does not say

One more observation, and it is the one that connects this chapter to the rest.

For all that Genesis 1 makes speech the instrument of creation, it gives no
technique. There is no formula recorded, no instruction, no suggestion that the
words God used are available or repeatable. The text does not supply a *method*.

This is a refusal, and it is consistent. The Hebrew Bible is remarkably
uninterested in transmitting operative verbal power to human beings. Where it
depicts creative speech, the speaker is God. Where humans use words powerfully —
blessing, cursing, prophesying, swearing — the power is conditional on God's
concurrence and is never presented as a technique the speaker controls.

The grimoire traditions of Part VI will supply the technique, at length and in
detail, and they will do so by drawing on Hebrew divine names while discarding
precisely this restraint. That is a real historical development, it is datable, and
it is a departure from the source rather than an extension of it.

**Genesis asserts that words made the world and declines to say how.** Everything
in this book that claims to say how is later, and most of it is very much later.
