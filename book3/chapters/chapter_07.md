# CHAPTER SEVEN
## The Logos

One Greek word carries four different ideas through eight hundred years, and almost
every confusion in this area comes from treating them as one.

*Logos* means: word, speech, account, reason, ratio, argument, principle,
explanation. It is the noun behind *logic* and the suffix behind every discipline
ending in *-ology*. It was already doing heavy work in philosophical Greek before
anyone applied it to God, and when it was applied to God it did not mean the same
thing twice.

This chapter separates the four, because the separation matters for what a Western
reader hears in the phrase *the Word*.

---

### One: Heraclitus

Around 500 BCE, in Ephesus, a philosopher whose surviving work amounts to a few
dozen fragments opens with a complaint about his readers.

Men fail to comprehend the *logos*, he says — the account according to which all
things come to pass — though they encounter it constantly and it is always true.
They hear it and do not understand, like sleepers.[^1]

This *logos* is not a word in the sense of a spoken utterance. It is closer to
**the intelligible order of things**: the pattern the world obeys, which is
available to anyone who attends and which almost nobody does. Heraclitus is
notoriously difficult and the fragments are contested, but the shape of the idea is
clear enough. There is a structure. It can be grasped. Most people do not grasp it.

Nothing personal about it, in either sense. The Heraclitean *logos* is not a being
and does not address anyone.

---

### Two: the Stoics

Three centuries later the Stoics take the term and make it do cosmology.

For them the *logos* is divine reason pervading matter — immanent, active,
identified with God, with fate, with providence, and with the fire from which
everything comes and to which it returns. The *logos spermatikos*, the
"seed-bearing reason," is the principle by which the cosmos organises itself into
the particular things it contains.

Two features are worth marking because they will be inherited. The Stoic *logos* is
**inside** the world rather than outside it. And it is **rational in a way that
obliges us**: because the same reason that orders the cosmos is present in the
human mind, living according to reason and living according to nature are the same
project. This is where Stoic physics becomes Stoic ethics, and it is why the
vocabulary survives into Roman moral philosophy.

Still impersonal, or nearly. One can be in harmony with the Stoic *logos*. One
cannot have a conversation with it.

---

### Three: Philo

Then, in Alexandria, in the first century, a Jewish philosopher with a Greek
education faces a problem that will occupy theology for four hundred years.

Philo holds two commitments. The God of Israel is transcendent, beyond
description, not to be contained by the world. And that same God made the world and
acts in it. These pull against each other. A God wholly beyond cannot touch; a God
who touches is not wholly beyond.

Philo's solution is a mediator, and he calls it the *logos*. It is God's instrument
in creation, the intelligible pattern by which the world is ordered, and the point
at which the transcendent becomes available. He gives it an extraordinary range of
titles — the image of God, the firstborn son, the high priest, the second God, the
place where the divine ideas reside.[^2]

**This is the decisive innovation** and it is easy to underrate because of what
came after. Philo has taken a Greek philosophical term and used it to solve a
Jewish theological problem, and in doing so he has created something with a
peculiar status: not God, not a creature, not quite a person, not quite a
principle. Later readers will find this unstable and will resolve it in
incompatible directions.

Philo did not think he was inventing. He thought he was reading Scripture, and much
of his work is commentary on Genesis. Whether he succeeded in being both a Platonist
and a Jew is a question his readers have disagreed about since.

---

### Four: John

And then, around the end of the first century, a Christian text opens with a
sentence that uses all of this vocabulary and then does something none of the
others did.

*In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.*

The first eighteen verses of the Gospel of John are a hymn, and they are carefully
built. The opening phrase deliberately echoes Genesis 1. The *logos* is pre-existent,
was with God, was God, and was the agent through which all things came to be —
which is recognisably Philonic. Light and darkness are introduced. The *logos* comes
to its own and is not received.

Then verse fourteen.

*And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us.*

**Nothing in the Greek tradition prepares for this.** Heraclitus's order does not
become a man. The Stoic reason does not have a birthplace. Philo's mediator is many
things and is not incarnate, and Philo would have found the claim unintelligible.
The verb John uses — *eskenosen*, literally "pitched his tent" — carries an echo of
the tabernacle, the tent in which the divine presence dwelt among Israel in the
wilderness, and the choice is not accidental.

This is a discontinuity, not a development. A philosophical term has been applied
to a particular human being who was born in a particular place and executed under a
particular Roman governor, and the consequences occupied the next four centuries.

---

### What the Church did with it

Briefly, because the Christological controversies are not this book's subject and
because their shape matters.

The patristic tradition spent three hundred years working out what had been
committed to. Justin Martyr used the *logos* to argue that whatever truth pagan
philosophers had reached was a share in the same reason. Origen developed the
relation between Father and Son in terms the next century found insufficient. The
fourth-century disputes, and the councils of Nicaea and Constantinople, turned on
whether the Word was of the same substance as the Father or merely similar — and
the difference, in Greek, is one letter.

That last detail deserves a moment. **A theological settlement affecting the
practice of half the world came to rest on the presence or absence of an iota**, and
the participants knew it and said so. The Arian controversy is the largest
historical instance of a tradition treating a single letter as carrying the weight
of everything — which is, in a different key, the subject of this book.

---

### The Hebrew side

Two corrections before leaving the material, because the account above is the Greek
half and the Greek half alone is misleading.

**John's prologue owes at least as much to Jewish Wisdom literature as to Greek
philosophy.** Proverbs 8 has Wisdom present at creation, brought forth before the
world, beside God as he establishes the heavens. Read Proverbs 8 and then John 1
and the structural debt is hard to miss. The tradition of a personified divine
attribute active in creation is in the Hebrew material already, and it did not need
Athens.

**And the Targums supply *Memra*.** In the Aramaic paraphrases of Scripture read in
the synagogue, "the Word of the LORD" repeatedly stands where the Hebrew has God
acting directly — a reverential circumlocution, a way of speaking about divine
action without excessive directness.

The relation of *Memra* to the Johannine *Logos* is **disputed and this book does
not resolve it**. An older generation of scholarship treated *Memra* as a genuine
hypostasis and a direct antecedent. Much recent work treats it as a translational
convention with no independent standing, and warns that reading it as a person
imports Christian categories into Jewish texts.[^3] Both positions are held by
competent scholars. The reader should know the dispute exists and should distrust
any account that presents either side as settled.

---

### What the Western reader inherits

The point of this chapter is not that Freemasonry took anything from any of it. It
did not; Chapter 6 said so and it remains true.

The point is that by the eighteenth century, **"the Word" was a phrase in English
that no educated person could hear neutrally.** It carried Genesis, it carried John,
it carried whatever the hearer's church had said about both. It meant divine
speech, creative power, revelation, and — for a Christian — a person.

So when a ceremony in a tavern told a man that a Word had been lost, the phrase
arrived pre-loaded, and it was not in anyone's power to unload it. The compilers
could no more prevent that resonance than they could prevent the word "temple"
sounding like Jerusalem.

**Which is why the interpretive literature turned out the way it did.** Generations
of Masonic commentators, asked what the Lost Word signified, reached for the
theological register because it was the register the phrase came in. They were not
inventing a connection. They were hearing one that the English language had already
made, and then mistaking their hearing for a report about the eighteenth century.
