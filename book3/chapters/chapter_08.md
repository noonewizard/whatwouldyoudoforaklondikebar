# CHAPTER EIGHT
## Naming the World

The second chapter of Genesis contains one of the strangest scenes in the Hebrew
Bible, and it is usually read past on the way to the more famous events that
follow.

God has made the animals. He brings them to the man *to see what he would call
them*, and whatever the man called every living creature, that was its name.

Read it slowly. God does not supply the names. He waits to see what the human being
will produce, and then ratifies the result. Whatever Adam says, stands.

---

### What the scene grants

Three things, and each has a long afterlife.

**Naming is delegated.** The chapter that opens with God naming day, night, heaven
and earth hands the remaining work to a creature. Whatever authority is involved in
naming, it is transferable.

**The names are not corrected.** There is no scene in which God improves on Adam's
choices. The text does not say the man named them rightly; it says he named them,
and that was that. This is a conventionalist detail sitting in a text that has
usually been read by Cratylans.

**And the naming is presented as a form of dominion.** The scene follows the grant
of rule over the animals in chapter 1, and readers from antiquity onward have taken
the connection as deliberate: to name is to have a certain standing toward the
named.

That last reading is where a very large amount of subsequent theory begins, and it
deserves examination rather than repetition.

---

### Naming as classification

Strip away the theology and the scene describes something perfectly ordinary and
genuinely powerful.

To name a set of things is to divide the world. The namer decides what counts as
one kind and what as another — where the boundaries fall, which differences matter
and which are ignored. Every naming system is a theory about what there is.

This is not a mystical claim; it is what taxonomy is, and the history of science is
substantially a history of arguments about it. Is a whale a fish? The question is
not about the animal. It is about which similarities are to be treated as decisive,
and the answer changed not because whales changed but because the criteria did.

**A person who controls the names controls the categories**, and control of
categories is control of a great deal. This is why disputes over terminology in
law, medicine and politics are not merely verbal, and why participants in them are
right to fight. The naming is the thing being fought over.

So the Genesis scene, read soberly, grants the human being something real: the
authority to carve the world up. That is a considerable power and no supernatural
account of it is required.

---

### Naming as address

The other sense, and the one that runs to the magical material in Part VI.

To have a name for something is to be able to *call* it. This is what Chapter 2
established: a name is an instrument of address rather than a description, and the
relation it creates is precisely the relation of being able to summon attention.

In a world understood as populated by agents — spirits, angels, powers, gods — this
capacity is not trivial. If the wind is a being rather than a process, then knowing
what to call it is the difference between hoping and asking. Every system of
invocation in this book rests on that inference, and the inference is not stupid.
It is an application to the cosmos of something that demonstrably works among
persons.

The question is whether the cosmos is the sort of thing the inference applies to,
and that is exactly the question this book does not answer in either direction.

---

### The name-taboo literature, and its limits

A caution here, because the comparative material on this point has been badly used
for a century.

It is often asserted that "primitive" peoples universally conceal their true names,
believing that possession of a name confers power over the bearer, and this claim
is then run together with the Tetragrammaton, with Rumpelstiltskin, with Egyptian
material about the secret name of Ra, and with whatever else is to hand, to produce
a supposed universal human intuition.

Several things are wrong with this.

**The universality is overstated.** Name-avoidance practices are real and
widespread and they are not uniform. Some concern the names of the dead; some the
names of affines; some particular classes of persons at particular times. Treating
them as one phenomenon requires ignoring what they actually regulate, which is
usually a social relationship rather than a metaphysical risk.

**The sources are often Frazer.** *The Golden Bough* assembled an enormous quantity
of material by a method — resemblance as evidence of a common underlying stratum —
that the discipline abandoned a century ago, and much popular writing on names is
still drinking from that well without knowing it.[^1]

**And the Hebrew case does not fit the pattern.** Jewish avoidance of pronouncing
the Tetragrammaton is not an attempt to prevent others from gaining power over God.
That reading gets the theology exactly backwards. It is **reverence**: the Name is
not withheld from enemies, it is withheld from ordinary use, and the substitution
of *Adonai* is an act of honour rather than of security. Chapter 10 treats this
properly.

The half-of-everything rule from the preceding volume applies here with force.
Where a practice has few possible forms — say a name or do not say it — a match
between unconnected traditions carries almost no information. **Half of everything
goes one way.**

### Adam's language, and the men who looked for it

The Genesis scene generated a research programme that ran for fifteen centuries,
and it is worth a paragraph because the programme's failure is instructive.

If Adam named the animals and his names were ratified, then the original language
was presumably one in which names fit their objects — a language in which the word
for a thing tells you what the thing is. This is the Cratylan position with
scriptural backing, and it was enormously attractive.

The search for that language, or for a reconstruction of it, occupied Renaissance
scholars, early modern philosophers, and eventually the inventors of philosophical
languages who hoped to build a notation in which nonsense would be unsayable and
truths would be visible in the form of the words.[^2]

It did not work, and the reason is instructive rather than embarrassing. **Nothing
about the sound or spelling of a word constrains what it means.** A language in
which the names fit would require the world to be divided one way only, and it is
not. Every project of this kind ends up encoding its author's classification and
calling it nature's.

The reader should keep this failure in view when Part IX examines claims that a
Hebrew word, transformed, reveals something true about its referent. Those claims
are the Adamic programme in miniature, and they inherit its problem.

---

### Who is permitted to name

A dimension of the Genesis scene that its theological readings tend to pass over,
and that bears directly on this book's argument about authority.

**Naming rights are distributed and contested in every society that has them.**

Parents name children — but registrars can refuse. Discoverers name species — but
under rules, enforced by bodies, with procedures for invalidation. Scientists name
elements, after a committee has ruled. Monarchs name successors. Conquerors rename
cities, and the renaming is understood by everybody as an assertion of possession.

**What this makes visible is that naming is not one act but two**: the utterance,
and the *standing to make it stick.*

**A name conferred without standing does not take.** A man who declares a new name
for a mountain has said something; the mountain's name has not changed. The same
utterance from a national mapping authority changes the maps.

Which is Chapter 3's distinction arriving in a different domain. **The words are
identical and the authority is not**, and the authority is where the effect lives.

**And it reframes what Genesis 2 is doing.** God does not merely watch the man name
the animals; the text specifies that whatever he called them, *that was its name.*
**The ratification is the grant.** What the human being receives is not the ability to
utter names — anyone can utter — but the standing for his utterances to take effect.

**That is the thing the Masonic third degree says is unavailable.**

Not a string. Not a meaning. The candidate has the room, the office and the
preparation, and is told that the word which would complete the sequence cannot be
supplied — which, read through this chapter, is a statement about where the chain of
ratification stops.

Chapter 18 develops this. It is noted here because Genesis 2 is where the
distinction between saying and being-entitled-to-say first appears in the Western
material, and everything in Part XI depends on it.

---

### The Masonic connection, stated narrowly

A candidate in a Masonic ceremony is not asked to name anything. There is no
Adamic material in the Craft degrees, no doctrine of an original language, no claim
that the words used are natural rather than conventional.

But something does happen to him that belongs in this chapter.

**He is given a name he did not have.** Not a personal name — he keeps that — but a
designation: he becomes an Entered Apprentice, then a Fellow Craft, then a Master
Mason. These are categories, and he has been placed in one. The naming is done *to*
him, by others, and its effect is precisely the classificatory effect this chapter
described: he is now a certain kind of person for certain purposes, and everyone in
the room treats him accordingly.

He is also given words to carry that are not descriptions of anything. They do no
classificatory work. They are tokens, and their function is address and recognition.

**Both senses of naming are present in the ceremony and they are doing different
jobs**, and separating them resolves a confusion that runs through the commentary.
The degree name classifies. The word of the degree identifies. Writers who treat
the Masonic word as though it described a state of the soul have collapsed the
second into the first — and the ritual itself keeps them apart, which is a point in
its favour.
