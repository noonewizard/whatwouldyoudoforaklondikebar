# CHAPTER NINE
## The Name of God

There is no such thing as *the* name of God in the Hebrew Bible, and the
persistence of the phrase has done more damage to clear thinking on this subject
than any other single habit.

There are several names. They have different forms, different histories, different
distributions across the text, and different functions. Merging them makes every
claim about "the Name" ambiguous from the outset — and a great deal of the
literature this book examines depends on that ambiguity to get started.

---

### The names, kept apart

**YHWH** — יהוה. The proper name, appearing some six thousand times. Four
consonants, no vowels supplied by the script. Not pronounced in Jewish practice;
*Adonai* is read in its place in liturgy, *ha-Shem* ("the Name") in ordinary
speech. This is the one Chapter 12 examines at length.

**Elohim** — אלהים. Grammatically plural, syntactically singular when it denotes
Israel's God: the verbs that follow it are singular. It is also a common noun
usable of other gods, and of powerful beings generally, which is why context
decides its reference. Gematria 86.

**Adonai** — אדני. "My Lord." The spoken substitute for YHWH, and eventually a name
in its own right. Gematria 65.

**Ehyeh asher ehyeh** — אהיה אשר אהיה. The response at the burning bush. First
person, imperfect aspect: "I am what I am," or "I will be what I will be," or "I am
the one who is" — the Hebrew does not distinguish these and the translations
diverge accordingly. Treated below.

**El Shaddai** — אל שדי. Rendered "God Almighty" by convention and by the
Septuagint, though the etymology is genuinely uncertain: proposals connect *shaddai*
to mountains, to breasts, to a Akkadian root for wilderness. Nobody knows. Gematria
of *Shaddai* 314.

**YHWH Tzevaot** — יהוה צבאות. "LORD of hosts," an epithet rather than a separate
name, of military resonance.

**These are distinct**, and the critical scholarship of the last century and a half
has spent an enormous effort on why — on which sources of the Pentateuch use which
name, what that tells us about the text's composition, and what the alternation
means. Whatever one thinks of the documentary hypothesis and its successors, the
distribution is a real feature of the text and it is not random.

A claim about "the name of God" that does not specify which one is not yet a claim.

---

### Exodus 3, and the answer that is not one

The single most important passage in this book's Hebrew material is a scene in
which a man asks for a name and does not get one.

Moses, at the burning bush, is sent to Israel. He raises an objection that is
practical rather than pious: they will ask who sent him, and he will need
something to tell them. *What shall I say unto them?*

The reply is **ehyeh asher ehyeh**.

Consider what this is. It is not a noun. It is a verbal clause — a first-person
form of the verb "to be," doubled, with a relative in the middle. As an answer to
"what is your name" it is grammatically peculiar in a way that survives
translation. If a person asked your name and you replied *I am what I am*, you
would not have given them a name. You would have declined, in a manner that also
said something.

The translations diverge because the Hebrew imperfect does not carve time the way
Indo-European tenses do. The Septuagint renders it *ego eimi ho on* — "I am the
being one," which is a substantial philosophical commitment and arguably reads
Greek ontology into a Hebrew verb. The King James has "I AM THAT I AM." Modern
translations increasingly prefer a future or durative sense: "I will be what I will
be."

**That range is not a failure of scholarship.** It is a property of the original,
and any argument that depends on one rendering being correct is building on ground
that will not hold.

What the scene does, in every reading, is put a name-request and a non-answer
together in the founding narrative of Israelite religion. Moses asks for something
to carry. He receives a sentence that mostly refuses to be carried, and is then
given YHWH in the following verse — a name related to the same verbal root, and
supplied after the evasion rather than instead of it.

---

### Not a parallel, and why I am saying so

A tradition whose central name-episode is a non-answer, and a tradition whose
central initiatory episode is a substituted word, are both doing something with the
withholding of a name.

I want to be careful here, because this is exactly the kind of observation that
becomes a chapter in somebody else's book with a line drawn from Sinai to a London
tavern.

**There is no transmission claim available.** The Masonic substitution is
eighteenth-century and its materials are architectural. Nothing connects the two
except that both involve not giving a name, and "not giving a name" is a
low-cardinality feature: there are two options, give it or don't, and half of
everything goes one way.

What can be said is weaker and still worth saying. **The structure is available
because it is a good structure.** A tradition that wants to assert that its object
exceeds what can be handed over has a limited repertoire for saying so, and
withholding the name is the most direct move in that repertoire. It has been
independently reached many times because the problem recurs, not because anyone
carried it.

That is convergence, and it is the finding of all three volumes of this series.

### The Name and its keeping

One further matter, because it is the hinge between this chapter and Part III.

The commandment against taking the Name *lashav* — in vain, to no purpose, falsely
— is usually heard in the modern West as a prohibition on swearing. Its scope in
the Hebrew is considerably wider and considerably more interesting: it concerns
using the Name emptily, including in oaths one does not intend to keep.

**The Name, in other words, is protected principally where it is used to bind
someone.** The context is oath-taking, which is a performative in Austin's exact
sense, and the danger addressed is not blasphemy in the modern sense but the
degradation of the mechanism by which promises are made enforceable.

This is a striking piece of social engineering and it is worth stating plainly.
A society in which contracts are secured by oath has an interest in the oath
retaining force. Restricting the Name's use to occasions that warrant it is how you
keep the currency from inflating.

And it means that the most consequential thing the divine Name does in the Hebrew
Bible is not creative or magical. **It is contractual.** The Name is what a promise
is sworn on, and its power in daily life is the power to make an undertaking
binding.

Which is precisely what the Masonic word is not, and what the Masonic **obligation**
is. Chapter 3 noted that the Craft's one unambiguously effective speech act is the
obligation, sworn aloud, before witnesses, on a volume of sacred law. The candidate
is not doing anything with the word. He is doing something with the oath — and in
that, whether or not anyone designed it so, he is standing in a tradition that runs
back a very long way and that has always understood exactly what it was doing.
