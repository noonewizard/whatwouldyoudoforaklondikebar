# PART III — THE HEBREW PROBLEM

# CHAPTER TEN
## Ineffability

A word stops being spoken. Nobody writes down the moment it happens, because it is
not a moment — it is a drift, a hardening of practice over generations, and by the
time anyone notices it is done, there is no longer anyone who could undo it.

This is the history of the Tetragrammaton in Jewish practice, and it is the closest
thing in the historical record to the situation Masonic ritual dramatises. It is
worth getting right, both because it is interesting and because it is constantly
misdescribed.

---

### What actually happened, as far as we can tell

The evidence is patchy and the reconstruction is contested in its details. What
follows is the broad shape, with the uncertainty marked.

In the earlier biblical material the Name is used freely. People greet each other
with it. It appears in personal names — every Hebrew name ending in *-iah* or
beginning *Jeho-* carries an element of it — and those names went on being given
long after the Name itself stopped being pronounced.

By the later Second Temple period, restriction is visible. The Septuagint, the
Greek translation made from the third century BCE onward, renders YHWH as *kyrios*,
"Lord" — a translation choice that already presupposes the substitution.[^1] Some
Greek manuscripts leave the Hebrew letters standing in the Greek text, untranslated
and unpronounced, which is a striking piece of scribal behaviour: the copyist
writes Greek until he reaches the Name, then writes Hebrew, then resumes.

At Qumran the practice varies and is informative. Some scrolls write the divine Name
in palaeo-Hebrew script — the older, angular letter-forms — inside texts otherwise
written in the square Aramaic script that had become standard. The Name is
typographically quarantined. Some scribes leave blank spaces to be filled later by
someone more qualified. Some substitute four dots.[^2]

**All of this is behaviour around a word that is still perfectly legible.** Nobody
is hiding it. They are marking it, and the marking is reverential.

By the rabbinic period the position is that the Name is pronounced only in the
Temple, and there only by priests — in the priestly blessing, and by the High Priest
on the Day of Atonement. Outside, *Adonai* is read.

Then in 70 CE the Temple is destroyed, and the one setting in which pronunciation
still occurred ceases to exist.

---

### The drift, not the decree

It matters that no one banned it.

There is no council, no edict, no datable prohibition. What there is instead is a
practice that narrows — from common use, to restricted use, to priestly use, to
Temple use, to none — over something like five centuries, with each generation
inheriting a slightly tighter convention than the last and having no reason to
question it.

**This is the ordinary way a pronunciation is lost**, and the ordinariness is the
point. A word in a consonantal script survives as letters; its sound survives only
in mouths. Stop the mouths for three generations and there is nothing to consult.
The script does not record it. That is not a failure of the script; Hebrew writing
was not designed to preserve pronunciation for people who had stopped speaking.

By the time the Masoretes came to fix the text's vocalisation, between roughly the
seventh and tenth centuries CE, there was no living tradition of pronouncing the
Name to record — and, more to the point, no desire to record one. What they did
instead is the subject of Chapter 12, and it is the origin of a very durable
confusion.

---

### Why this is not concealment

The distinction this chapter exists to make.

**Concealment** withholds information from someone who would use it. It implies an
adversary, a risk, and a reason to prevent access. Ciphers are concealment.
Passwords are concealment.

**Reverence** restricts use while leaving access untouched. The letters were never
hidden. They are in every scroll, legible to any reader of Hebrew, and there is no
period in which a Jewish child learning to read was prevented from seeing them. The
restriction is on *saying*, not on *knowing*.

Popular writing collapses these constantly, and the collapse produces the idea that
the Jewish tradition possessed a secret name which it guarded and which might be
recovered by outsiders with the right technique. Every element of that sentence is
wrong. The name was not secret, it was not guarded against outsiders, and there is
nothing to recover that a reader of the Hebrew Bible does not already have in front
of them.

**What was lost was a practice, and practices are lost by not being practised.**

---

### The other losses

Three things went with the pronunciation, and they are the substance of the loss.

**The authority.** Pronouncing the Name in the Temple was an act performed by a
person holding an office, and the office is gone. The priesthood has not functioned
in its Temple capacity since 70 CE. A modern person who somehow knew the vowels
would be in the position Chapter 3 described: possessed of the words and of nothing
else.

**The setting.** The Holy of Holies, on the Day of Atonement. Not a place that can
be improvised.

**The condition.** The purity requirements attaching to the act were elaborate and
non-negotiable. Whatever one thinks about their basis, the system treated them as
constitutive: without them the act did not occur, however correctly it was
performed.

**The letters survived all of it.** They were never at risk. This is the model
Chapter 1 announced and it holds exactly: the word is the part that remains, and
everything that made the word do anything is the part that went.

---

### Ineffability as a theological position

One further reading, and it is not the same as the historical account above.

There is a strand of thought — in Jewish, Christian and Islamic theology alike —
holding that the divine is in principle beyond language, not merely as a matter of
practice but necessarily. The negative theology of Pseudo-Dionysius, the *via
negativa*, Maimonides's insistence that positive attributes cannot be predicated of
God: these argue that every name applied to God is a concession to human weakness,
and that the accurate statements are the denials.

**This is a philosophical claim and it is independent of the historical one.** The
pronunciation of the Tetragrammaton could have been preserved and the theology
would still hold; conversely, the practical loss says nothing about whether God is
in principle nameable.

They are frequently merged, and the merge produces a very attractive and very
unfounded story in which the pronunciation was allowed to lapse *because* the
divine was recognised as ineffable. There is no evidence for that causal claim. The
restriction looks like reverence in practice long before anyone articulates a
doctrine of ineffability, and the doctrine arrives in its developed form from a
philosophical tradition with Greek parentage.

**A historical practice and a metaphysical thesis are different kinds of claim**,
and this book's standing rule applies: they may not be exchanged mid-argument.

---

### The names that survived

A detail that disposes of the "concealment" reading more effectively than any
argument, and which is hiding in plain sight in the biblical text.

**Hebrew personal names are full of the divine Name.**

Every name ending in *-iah* or *-yahu* carries an element of it: Isaiah, Jeremiah,
Obadiah, Zechariah, Hezekiah. Every name beginning *Jeho-* or *Yeho-* does the same:
Jehoshaphat, Jehoiakim, Jehonathan.

**These names went on being given for centuries after the Name itself ceased to be
pronounced**, and they are pronounced daily, by everyone, without difficulty or
anxiety.

**Consider what that means.** If the restriction were about preventing access to a
powerful word — if the concern were that an outsider might obtain the Name and use
it — then names containing it would be a catastrophic leak. They are spoken
constantly, by anybody, in public, and they preserve a substantial part of the
vocalisation.

**Nobody minded, and nobody has ever minded.**

Which tells us that the restriction was never about access. **It was about use.** The
Name is not to be uttered *as the Name*, in the way one utters a designation. Its
presence inside a personal name is a different act, and the tradition has always
treated it as one.

**And it is the principal evidence for the reconstruction discussed above.** The
theophoric names preserve a living vocalisation of the element — *Yeho-* at the
start, *-yahu* at the end — transmitted continuously in a tradition that never
stopped saying them. That is why scholars can say anything at all about how the
Name sounded.

**The thing that was allowed to lapse is precisely the thing nobody needed for any
other purpose.** What was useful — the names of one's ancestors and neighbours —
survived intact, because it was in constant use and because using it was never the
thing that was restricted.

---

### What a Mason inherits

The candidate in the third degree is not being taught any of this. The Craft does
not discuss the Tetragrammaton, and the material examined here has no demonstrable
route into the eighteenth-century ritual.

But the shape is inherited anyway, through the general culture rather than through
a chain.

An educated Englishman in 1730 knew that the Jews did not pronounce the name of
God. It was a commonplace, discussed in sermons and in the Hebraist literature that
Chapter 34 will examine. He knew that a name existed, that it was not said, and
that this was a matter of reverence rather than of secrecy — though he may well
have understood it as secrecy, since Christian writing on the subject was not
always careful.

So a ceremony about a word that cannot be given fell on ears already prepared for
the idea. Whether the compilers knew what they were evoking is not established and
probably not knowable. **The resonance was available whether they reached for it or
not**, which is the finding of Part II restated, and it is the reason this book
spends four chapters on material that is not a source.
