# CHAPTER TWELVE
## The Tetragrammaton

There is a name that millions of people have used in hymns, prayers and sermons for
four hundred years, and it is a mistake.

Not a mistranslation, not a variant, not a dialect form. A mistake of a specific
and recoverable kind: the result of reading one word's consonants with another
word's vowels, made by people who did not understand a scribal convention, and
then set in type and sung until it became familiar.

The name is **Jehovah**, and how it happened is the best worked example in this
book of a phenomenon that will recur in every chapter after it.

---

### The convention

Return to the Masoretes. They are supplying vowel points for a consonantal text
they regard as fixed and will not alter.

They come to יהוה. The consonants must stand; the text is not to be changed. But
the word is not read aloud as written — a reader encountering it says *Adonai*. The
apparatus therefore has to signal a substitution, and the device the Masoretes use
for this is standard and appears throughout the Hebrew Bible wherever the written
and spoken forms diverge.

It is called ***qere*** and ***ketiv***: "what is read" and "what is written." The
consonants of the written word stay on the line. The vowels of the word actually to
be spoken are pointed onto them. The reader, who knows the convention, says the
spoken word.

In the case of the Tetragrammaton this happens so often that no marginal note is
needed. It is a *qere perpetuum* — a permanent substitution, assumed everywhere,
requiring no annotation because every reader knows.[^1]

So יהוה appears with vowel points belonging to *Adonai*.

**The points are not the vowels of the Name. They are an instruction to say a
different word.**

---

### The misreading

Now introduce a reader who does not know the convention.

Christian Hebraists of the Renaissance came to the Hebrew text with enormous
enthusiasm, real learning, and gaps. They saw four consonants with vowel points
attached. They did what any reader does with a pointed word: they pronounced it.

Yod-he-waw-he, with the vowels of *Adonai*, comes out as something close to
*Yehovah* — and, through the conventions of Latin and then European
transliteration, as **Jehovah**.

The form is generally traced to the early sixteenth century and particularly to
Petrus Galatinus, whose *De arcanis catholicae veritatis* of 1518 put it into wide
circulation. Earlier instances have been proposed, and the question of who first
wrote it down is less interesting than the fact that it spread.[^2] It entered the
great vernacular Bibles. It entered hymnody. Four centuries later it is still on
church noticeboards.

**A scribal instruction was read as a word, and the word acquired a life.**

There is no malice in this and not much stupidity either. The misreading is what a
competent reader does when a convention is unfamiliar, and the Hebraists who made
it were doing genuinely difficult work in a language they had learned as adults,
often from teachers who had their own reasons for not explaining everything.

---

### What this case demonstrates

Four things, and each recurs.

**A technically correct operation performed on real data can produce a completely
spurious result.** Nothing about the pronunciation of *Yehovah* is arithmetically
wrong. The consonants are those consonants; the points are those points; the
combination sounds like that. The operation is valid and the output is an artefact.

**The error is invisible from inside the operation.** Nothing in the letters
announces that the vowels belong elsewhere. Detecting the mistake requires
knowledge from outside the transformation — knowledge of a convention, which is to
say, historical and contextual knowledge.

**A result that circulates becomes evidence for itself.** Once *Jehovah* was in
print, later writers found it in their sources and reproduced it, and the
reproduction looked like confirmation. This is the mechanism the preceding volume
identified in a different context: circulation mistaken for corroboration.

**And the error is stable.** It has been understood for a very long time. Scholarly
opinion has been clear since at least the nineteenth century. It has made no
difference to usage, because usage is not driven by scholarship.

**Hold those four points.** They are the anatomy of every failed decipherment in
Part IX, and the *Jehovah* case has the advantage of being one where everyone now
agrees what went wrong.

---

### Why nobody fixed it

A question worth asking, because the answer says something about the whole subject.

The misreading has been understood for a very long time. Hebraists knew by the
seventeenth century; scholarly opinion has been unambiguous since at least the
nineteenth. Reference works say so. It is not controversial and never really was.

**And it has made no difference whatever to usage.**

The form is in hymns that congregations have sung for centuries. It is in the
Authorised Version at four places, and in the Revised Version more widely. It named
a nineteenth-century religious movement. It is on church noticeboards.

Three reasons, and each recurs in this book.

**Usage is not driven by scholarship.** A form that has entered liturgy, hymnody and
devotional habit is maintained by those practices, not by the philological facts.
Nobody singing a hymn is consulting a lexicon.

**The correction has no constituency.** Nothing is gained by anyone in particular
from changing it, and a good deal of familiar material would have to be rewritten.

**And the error had become a name.** This is the interesting one. After four
centuries of use, *Jehovah* refers — people know who is meant, the chain of use is
intact, and by the account of naming in Chapter 2 that is precisely what a name
requires. It is a name whose origin was a misreading and which has, by ordinary
linguistic means, become a real designation.

**So the philological correction and the linguistic fact are both true**, and they
do not conflict. The vowels are wrong. The word works.

That is worth holding onto, because it is a case where "this is a mistake" and
"this functions perfectly well" are simultaneously accurate — and this book has
several more of those coming.

---

### What the Name probably sounded like, and how confident anyone can be

The scholarly reconstruction generally favours something like *Yahweh*, and the
reasoning is worth setting out because it shows the limits.

The evidence is indirect and of three kinds. **Transcriptions into other
alphabets**: Greek writers, particularly patristic authors reporting on Jewish
usage, give forms such as *Iao* and *Iabe*, which are suggestive. **Theophoric
personal names**: the element appears as *Yeho-* at the start of names and *-yahu*
or *-yah* at the end, and these were vocalised in a living tradition that continued
long after the Name itself fell silent. **And the verbal root**: the connection to
*hayah*, "to be," suggested by Exodus 3, supports a form consistent with a
causative or simple imperfect.

Put together, the reconstruction is reasonable and widely held.

**It is also an inference, and it should be labelled as one.** Nobody has heard the
word pronounced by a native speaker in a living liturgical tradition. The Greek
transcriptions are second-hand reports by writers with their own agendas, working
across a phonological gap. The theophoric evidence gives a truncated form, not the
whole. This is a reconstruction of the same general reliability as other
reconstructions in historical linguistics — which is to say, good, and not the same
thing as a recording.

This book will not assert a pronunciation. What it will assert is that **the
question of pronunciation is not the interesting one**, for the reason Chapter 10
gave: a person in possession of the correct vowels would still lack the office, the
setting and the condition, and would therefore be able to do precisely nothing with
them.

### The 26

One arithmetical fact, verified in this book's own computation and reported here
because it will be needed later.

The Tetragrammaton's standard gematria value is **26**. Yod 10, he 5, waw 6, he 5.

It is a small and tidy number, and an enormous amount has been built on it. That
26 is twice 13; that 13 is the value of *echad*, "one," and also of *ahavah*,
"love"; that love plus unity equals the Name.

The arithmetic is exact. This book has run it: *echad* 13, *ahavah* 13, sum 26,
YHWH 26. The figures reproduce.

**What that equality is, and is not**, is the subject of Chapter 14. For now it is
enough to say that it is a genuine observation made within a tradition that
proposed such connections for reflection, that it is not a cipher because nothing
was concealed and nothing recovered, and that its striking quality depends entirely
on not asking how many other pairs of Hebrew words sum to 26.

Chapter 14 asks.
