# CHAPTER FIFTEEN
## Notarikon and Temurah

Gematria has two siblings, and between them the three make up the standard
apparatus of Hebrew letter-interpretation. They work differently, they have
different histories, and they fail differently when removed from the setting that
constrained them.

---

### Notarikon

The name comes from the Latin *notarius*, a shorthand-writer, and the technique is
what a shorthand-writer's habits suggest: a word is read as an abbreviation.

It runs in two directions.

**Expansion.** Each letter of a word is taken as the initial of another word, and
the result is read as a phrase. A four-letter word becomes a four-word sentence,
and the sentence is offered as what the word was concealing.

**Contraction.** A phrase is reduced to the initials of its words, and the
resulting string is read as a word in its own right.

The second direction is thoroughly ordinary. Hebrew literature is full of acronyms
formed this way, and they are not mystical — Rambam for Rabbi Moshe ben Maimon,
Tanakh for Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim. This is abbreviation, the same practice that
gives English *radar* and *laser*, and nobody supposes a concealed doctrine.

**The first direction is where the difficulty lies**, and the difficulty is
arithmetical rather than theological.

Consider a four-letter Hebrew word. Expanding it by notarikon requires choosing,
for each letter, a word beginning with that letter. Hebrew has thousands of words.
Even restricting to a modest vocabulary of, say, five hundred words with reasonably
even distribution across the alphabet, each position offers on the order of twenty
candidates — and four positions give twenty to the fourth power, which is a hundred
and sixty thousand possible expansions.

**Of those, a great many will be grammatical, and a fair number will be
theologically appropriate**, because the interpreter is selecting for
appropriateness and the vocabulary of devotion is not large.

The technique cannot fail. That is its problem. Applied to any word by a
sufficiently fluent practitioner, it will produce a pious sentence, and the
sentence will feel like a discovery because the alternatives were never counted.

### Temurah

Substitution: letters replaced by other letters according to a rule.

Several schemes are traditional. **Atbash** pairs the first letter with the last,
the second with the second-last, and so on — the scheme Chapter 16 examines at
length. **Albam** divides the alphabet in half and pairs the first letter with the
twelfth. **Atbach** works on the numerical values, pairing letters whose values
multiply or sum to a round figure. Others exist, and the tradition treats them as a
family of available operations.

**Note what having several schemes does to the evidential situation.**

A single fixed substitution applied to a word gives one output. Having, say, half a
dozen schemes available gives six outputs, and an interpreter free to choose among
them has six chances to find something usable. Add the option of applying a scheme
twice, or applying one scheme to part of a word, and the space expands further.

This is not a hostile characterization. The tradition genuinely does treat these as
alternative legitimate operations, and there is no rule specifying which applies
where. The freedom is real and it is built in.

---

### What constrained them

Here is the part that the modern appropriations lose, and it is the substance of
this chapter.

All three techniques operated inside a system with three properties, and each
property did real evidential work.

**A fixed canon.** The text being interpreted was bounded. You could not go looking
in an arbitrary corpus for a string that suited you; the material was the Torah,
the Prophets, the Writings, and everyone knew what was in them. This bounds the
search space in the strict sense — and a bounded search space is the precondition
for a result meaning anything.

**A shared language with settled spellings.** Practitioners were working in Hebrew,
about Hebrew words, with orthography agreed within narrow limits. There was little
scope for choosing a convenient spelling, because the spellings were known and a
forced one would be noticed immediately.

**A community with judgment.** This is the strongest constraint and the least
formal. Rabbinic interpretation was conducted in public, among people trained in
the same material, who would say so if a proposal was strained. A notarikon that
struck the room as contrived did not survive the afternoon. The technique had no
authority of its own; what it produced had to persuade a hall full of people
extremely well equipped to be unimpressed.

**Together these three amount to peer review.** Not in the modern institutional
sense, but in the functional one: a proposal was made publicly, to qualified
critics, in a context where being wrong had reputational cost.

---

### What happens when the constraints go

Remove all three and the same operations become something else entirely.

**The canon opens.** Apply the techniques not to the Torah but to Masonic ritual, or
to street plans, or to English words, or to any text at all, and the search space
becomes unbounded. There is always more material.

**The spellings loosen.** Transliterating a non-Hebrew word into Hebrew — *Baphomet*,
say, or a Masonic term — requires choices at every letter, and Chapter 16 will count
them. There is no correct spelling of a word that was never Hebrew.

**And the community goes.** A modern practitioner works alone, publishes to an
audience that mostly shares the conclusion, and faces no critic equipped to say that
a particular expansion is forced. The check that did most of the work is simply
absent.

**The techniques did not get worse. Their environment did.**

This is the finding of the chapter and it is worth stating in a form that
generalizes: *a method's reliability is not a property of the method. It is a
property of the method plus the constraints it runs under.* Gematria inside the
rabbinic academy and gematria on a website are the same arithmetic and different
epistemic situations, and the difference is entirely in the second term.

---

### The temptation to be unfair

A caution, because the argument above can be run too hard and often is.

It would be easy to conclude that rabbinic letter-interpretation was rigorous and
modern letter-interpretation is not, and to leave it there with the ancients
flattered. That is not quite right either.

The rabbinic techniques were not producing knowledge in the sense a modern reader
means. They were producing *derash* — homily, application, the drawing out of a
lesson. The constraints ensured that a proposal was not laughable; they did not
ensure that it was true, and the tradition did not claim they did. A notarikon
expansion that survived the room was a good expansion, not a demonstrated fact
about the text's composition.

**So the comparison is not rigor versus sloppiness. It is one register mistaken
for another.**

The modern error is a category error rather than a competence error. Somebody takes
a device that was always understood as interpretive and treats its output as
evidential, and the reason this is so easy to do is that nothing in the output
announces which register it belongs to. A striking equivalence looks the same
whether it is offered as a homily or as a proof. The difference is in the claim
being made, and the claim is made by the person, not the arithmetic.

### The techniques as a diagnostic

A practical use for the material in this chapter that has nothing to do with
Freemasonry, and which is worth setting out because it generalizes.

**The presence of these techniques in a body of material is a dating signal.**

Gematria, notarikon and temurah have a documented history. They appear in rabbinic
literature from roughly the second century, develop in the medieval Kabbalistic
tradition, and pass into Christian use from Pico in 1486 and into occultism from
Lévi in the 1850s.

**So a text using them belongs to one of those streams, and which stream it belongs
to is usually recoverable from how it uses them.**

**Rabbinic usage** is bounded, homiletic, and applied to the Hebrew canon. It
proposes rather than proves, and it operates within a community that can reject a
strained reading.

**Christian-Kabbalistic usage** applies the same techniques to the same canon and
extracts Christian conclusions. The technique is inherited; the target is new. Pico
finding the Trinity in *bereshit* is a recognizable move with a recognizable date.

**Occult usage** applies them to anything — to non-Hebrew words, to proper names, to
modern material — and the target lexicon expands to whatever the interpreter
accepts. **This is the stage at which the constraints are gone entirely**, and it
begins in the nineteenth century.

**Which gives a rough but serviceable test.** A claim applying Hebrew
letter-technique to a non-Hebrew word almost certainly belongs to the third stream,
and therefore postdates 1850, whatever antiquity it asserts for itself.

**The Atbash-on-Baphomet claim of the next chapter is a textbook instance**, and its
actual date — 1984 — is exactly what the test predicts.

This is not a substitute for checking. It is a prior, and a well-founded one: **the
degradation of these techniques from bounded exegesis to universal solvent is a
historical process with a direction and a timetable**, and where a claim sits in
that process is usually visible from the claim itself.

---

### Forward to Masonry

None of these techniques appears in Masonic ritual.

Not gematria, not notarikon, not temurah. The Craft degrees contain no numerical
interpretation of words, no acronymic expansion, no letter substitution. The
exposures, written by hostile parties looking for anything discreditable, record
none. This is a negative finding and it is a clean one.

What *does* exist, in abundance, is modern application of these techniques *to*
Masonic material by people outside the system — and that is a fact about the
twentieth and twenty-first centuries, which Chapter 43 examines on its own terms.

The next chapter takes the most famous instance, runs it properly, and reports what
happened when I did.
