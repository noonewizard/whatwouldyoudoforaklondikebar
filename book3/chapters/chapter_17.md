# CHAPTER SEVENTEEN
## Hiram

A man is building a temple. Three of his workmen want something he has and will not
give. They confront him at the three entrances in turn; he refuses each; the third
kills him. His body is concealed, searched for, found, and raised.

That is the legend, in the barest form that most jurisdictions would recognise, and
it is worth noticing how little of it is in the Bible.

---

### What scripture supplies

Hiram appears in the Hebrew Bible, and so does a second Hiram, and the relation
between them has occupied Masonic commentators for two centuries.

**Hiram, king of Tyre**, is a political figure: an ally of David and Solomon who
supplies cedar, timber and craftsmen for the Temple. He has no craft role himself.

**Hiram the craftsman** — called *Huram-abi* in Chronicles — is a worker in bronze,
the son of a widow of the tribe of Naphtali and a man of Tyre. He casts the pillars,
the sea, the vessels. He is described with real admiration: filled with wisdom and
understanding and cunning to work in brass.[^1]

**Neither of them dies.**

There is no murder in the biblical account. No conspiracy, no refusal, no burial in
a shallow grave, no search, no raising. The craftsman completes his work and the
narrative moves on. Whatever happened to him, scripture does not say, because
scripture is not interested.

**The entire dramatic structure of the third degree is supplied from outside the
Bible**, and there is no source text for it. This is the central fact of the
chapter and it constrains everything that follows.

---

### When it appears

The chronology has been set out already and is worth restating here in its
narrowest form.

The Scottish catechisms of 1696 to about 1714 have two degrees and no Hiram. The
third degree with the legend appears in the printed record in the 1720s, and
Prichard's *Masonry Dissected* of 1730 gives it in recognisable form.[^2]

So the legend is composed, or assembled, or stabilised — the sources do not let us
say which — somewhere in the 1720s, in England, by persons unknown.

Nobody claims authorship. No document records a decision. There is no committee
minute, no correspondence, no draft. The degree appears in the record already
formed, which is what eighteenth-century ritual development generally looks like:
cumulative, anonymous, and undocumented.

---

### The comparisons, and the rule

Hiram has been identified with a considerable number of figures, and the list is
worth giving because its length is itself informative.

Osiris, dismembered and reassembled. Christ, killed and raised. Tammuz and Adonis,
dying and returning vegetation gods. Mithras. Attis. Baldr. Orpheus. Jacques de
Molay, the last Grand Master of the Templars, burned in 1314. Charles I, executed
in 1649 — a reading that had some currency among those who wanted Masonry to be a
Jacobite vehicle.

**The brief for this book instructs that Hiram not be automatically identified with
any of them, and that where scholars make such comparisons the comparisons be
attributed and examined.** That is the right instruction and here is what
examination yields.

Apply the six transmission questions from Chapter 5.

*Chronology* passes for most candidates; they are all older, some by millennia.
*Transmission* fails universally: no route has been documented from any of these
traditions to the London of the 1720s. *Intertextuality* fails: there is no verbal
dependence, no shared distinctive vocabulary, no quotation, none of the fingerprints
by which textual descent is established. *Institutional continuity* fails: no
organisation persisted. *Anachronism* is a problem for several, since the
scholarship on Osiris, Tammuz and Attis available to eighteenth-century readers was
thin and much of what is now asserted about them post-dates the ritual by a century
or more.

And the comparison itself is weak on its own terms, which is the point the
literature misses.

**A dying-and-rising figure is not a distinctive feature.** It is one of the
commonest narrative shapes in the world's stories, and the anthropological
literature that assembled the pattern — Frazer above all — did so by a method the
discipline has abandoned, and by collapsing cases that differ in nearly every
particular. Several of the standard "dying and rising gods" have been argued not to
die, or not to rise, or to do neither in the sources rather than in the
summaries.[^3]

**The rule from the preceding volume applies with force: before being impressed by
a parallel, count the alternatives.** A narrative about a craftsman can have him
live or die. Half of everything goes one way.

---

### What the legend is made of

Set the comparisons aside and look at the materials actually used, because they are
local and traceable.

**The names are biblical.** Hiram, Solomon, Tyre, the widow's son — all from Kings
and Chronicles, all available to any reader of an English Bible.

**The setting is the Temple**, which was already the organising image of Masonic
symbolism before the third degree existed.

**The vocabulary is architectural.** Builders, workmen, entrances, tools, wages,
the completion of a work. There is no sulphur, no *nigredo*, no Osirian formula, no
Egyptian anything. Whoever assembled this was working with the materials already in
the room.

**And the plot devices are ordinary.** A conspiracy of three. A refusal. A
confrontation at each of three doors. A concealment and a search. A discovery. These
are the furniture of popular narrative in any century, and an eighteenth-century
Englishman assembling a dramatic episode would reach for them without needing a
mystery religion.

**The most economical account is that the legend was made, in the 1720s, from
biblical names and ordinary narrative materials, by people who needed a third
degree and had a Temple to set it in.**

That is not a deflationary reading offered to spite the tradition. It is what the
evidence supports, and it leaves the interesting question untouched — which is why
*this* plot, of all the plots available.

---

### Why a murder

Because something had to be lost, and this book's argument is that the loss is the
degree's actual content.

Chapter 18 makes the case properly. Here it is enough to observe the structural
problem the compilers faced. Two degrees existed that *conferred*. A third degree
doing more of the same would have been redundant and everyone would have noticed.
A third degree that *withheld* was a different kind of thing.

And once you decide that something is to be unavailable, you need a reason within
the story for its unavailability. **A murdered man cannot pass on what he knew.**
That is the cheapest and most complete explanation a narrative can supply: not
withheld, not forgotten, not misplaced, but ended with a person.

Which suggests that the murder is there **because the loss required it**, rather
than the loss being a consequence of a murder imported from elsewhere. The plot
serves the structure, not the other way round.

Whether anyone thought it through in these terms is doubtful. Chapter 18 argues
that the degree was very probably arrived at rather than designed — that a legend
was assembled, that it worked, and that the philosophical reading accumulated
afterwards as generations of members made sense of what they had been handed.

**A structure can be coherent without having been designed coherent.** A cathedral
whose proportions were reached by trial still stands up.

---

### The part that is not invented

One closing observation, since this chapter has been largely negative.

Whatever its sources, the legend works. It has been performed hundreds of thousands
of times across three centuries, in several languages, on six continents, by
amateurs in upstairs rooms, and men who went through it have described it as the
most affecting hour of their lives.

That is an empirical fact about the drama and it is not explained by pointing out
that Hiram does not die in the Bible. The preceding volume spent a hundred pages on
what makes it work — the staged death, the embodied participation, the images
delivered at the point of maximum attention — and found the mechanisms ordinary and
the effect real.

**The legend is eighteenth-century and it is not therefore shallow.** Being able to
date something is not the same as having explained it away, and the temptation to
think otherwise is one this series has tried throughout to resist.
