# CHAPTER EIGHTEEN
## The Lost Word

This is the chapter the book has been walking towards, and its conclusion is
negative in a way that took me some time to accept.

**There is no lost word.**

Not: there is a lost word and we cannot find it. Not: there was one and it has been
destroyed. There is no particular string of letters that Masonic tradition once
possessed and subsequently mislaid, and the evidence for this is the absence of any
document in which such a word is identified, used, or reported as having been
available.

The loss is a feature of a story. It is not a report of an event.

---

### What would count as evidence

State the standard first, so the negative finding can be assessed.

If a word had genuinely been lost, we would expect one or more of the following.

**A text using it.** Some document from before the loss in which the word appears,
so that we can see what was subsequently unavailable.

**A report of the loss as an event.** Minutes, correspondence, a complaint — some
record of a body noticing that something it used to have was gone.

**Disagreement about the substitute.** If a real word had dropped out and a
placeholder been adopted, we would expect argument about whether the placeholder
was right, proposals for recovery, factions.

**Or continuity of the claim across the break.** Some indication that the
eighteenth-century Masons believed themselves to be missing something their
predecessors had.

**None of these exists.**

The Scottish catechisms of 1696 to 1714 have a word, and it is not lost; there is
no sense in those documents that anything is missing. The third degree appears in
the 1720s with the loss already built into it. Between the two there is no
transitional document in which a word is present and then goes.

What we have is a system that acquired a legend, and the legend contains a loss.

---

### The narrative account

The most economical explanation is the one Chapter 17 reached, and it has two
steps.

**A third degree was needed.** Institutional pressure for advancement, the same
appetite that would shortly produce hundreds of higher degrees in France. Two
degrees were not enough to sustain a graded system.

**A third degree conferring more of the same would have been visibly redundant.**
The first two give words, signs, tools, meanings. A third of that kind is an
anticlimax and everyone in the room would feel it.

So the third degree withholds, and once it withholds it needs a reason, and a
murdered master supplies the cheapest complete one. **The substitution follows from
the plot.**

That is very probably how it happened, and it is worth saying plainly that it makes
the whole thing sound smaller than the literature does.

---

### The objection that matters

Except that the narrative account does not finish the job, and this is where I
think most debunking treatments of this material stop too early.

**Granting that the loss is a narrative necessity, why did this version survive?**

A legend in which the word is recovered was equally constructible. The plot could
have had the workmen fail, or the word be written down, or the search succeed, or
the dying man gasp it out. Any of these is as easy to write as what we have, and
each would have given a more conventional and more satisfying degree.

More than that: **Masonry built a recovery version and did not replace the Craft
one.** The Royal Arch, appearing within about twenty years, supplies precisely the
recovery the third degree withholds. The materials for a satisfying ending were
available and were used — in a *supplementary* body, leaving the Craft degrees
exactly as they were.[^1]

So the withholding version persisted, unchanged in essentials, through three
centuries of revision, schism, union and export, in jurisdictions that agree about
very little else.

**Things that persist under selection are usually doing something.** The question is
what.

---

### What the substitution actually does

Here is the answer this book proposes, offered as an interpretation and tagged as
one.

The ceremony makes a distinction that is otherwise very hard to make.

A candidate who is simply given a word learns that he now has it. A candidate who
is given a word *and told it is a substitute for one that is unavailable* learns
something else: that possessing the string is not the same as possessing whatever
the string stands for.

He is handed a token with its status stamped on its face. He is not deceived. He is
not told there is nothing. He is told, in the only vocabulary a ritual has, that
**what he holds and what he came for are different things.**

Chapter 3 supplied the framework. A word is inert without the authority to use it,
the setting in which it counts, and the condition of the speaker. Chapter 13 showed
the historical case: the High Priest had all four and lost three, and the one he
kept was worthless alone. The Masonic candidate is the inverse — he has the lodge,
the office, the preparation, and the ceremony tells him that the word is the piece
he does not get.

**The degree separates the word from everything that makes a word work, and then
gives him the wrong half on purpose.**

That is a coherent and rather sophisticated thing for a ceremony to do, and I do
not believe anyone in the 1720s intended it.

---

### Coherent without being designed

The distinction matters enough to state carefully, because it is this book's
position and it is easy to caricature in either direction.

**I am not claiming that the compilers encoded a philosophy of knowledge.** There is
no evidence for that and the probabilities are against it. They were assembling a
degree, under ordinary institutional pressure, from materials to hand, and the
loss was almost certainly a plot device before it was anything else.

**I am claiming that what they assembled has a property**, and that the property is
real whether or not it was intended, and that it is very likely why the thing
survived.

This is ordinary cultural selection and it needs no mystery. Variants that work get
kept; variants that do not, do not. A ceremony that gives men a token and tells them
it is not the thing produces a particular effect on the men who receive it — an
itch, a sense that something remains outstanding — and a ceremony that neatly
resolves does not. Over three centuries, across thousands of lodges, the version
that leaves something open is the version still being performed.

**The Royal Arch is the evidence that the itch is real.** An institution that felt
no incompleteness would not have generated a recovery narrative within twenty
years.

---

### The comparison that makes the case

There is one piece of evidence for the "designed absence" reading that is stronger
than anything else available, and it comes from what happened next in France.

**Between roughly 1740 and 1780, French Masonry generated hundreds of higher
degrees.**

The scale is genuinely remarkable. Rites of twenty-five degrees, of thirty-three, of
ninety and more. Systems proliferated, competed, merged, collapsed. The Rite of
Perfection, the Strict Observance, the Élus Coëns, Memphis-Misraïm and dozens of
smaller bodies now known only from their own paperwork.

**And what nearly all of them did was supply.**

Higher degrees offered more: more knowledge, more titles, more elaborate
ceremonies, and — repeatedly — the recovery of what the Craft had withheld. Secrets
promised, lineages asserted, unknown superiors invoked. Von Hund's Strict Observance
was organised entirely around the claim that hidden masters were directing it, and
he could never produce one.

**The market wanted delivery, and the market was served.**

Against that background the Craft's refusal is not a default. It is a **choice that
was made repeatedly and could have been unmade at any point**, in a period when
every commercial and institutional pressure ran the other way, and when the people
making the choice could see their competitors making the opposite one.

**English Craft Masonry kept three degrees and a substituted word while the
Continent was selling ninety degrees and the secret of the ages.**

I do not think anyone articulated a reason. What I think is that the version that
kept the space empty went on working, and the versions that filled it mostly did
not — Wilhelmsbad investigated the Strict Observance's Templar claim in 1782 and
rejected it, and that body did not survive the rejection.

**A claim that cannot be checked will eventually be checked.** A declared absence
cannot be falsified, because it asserts nothing.

That is a selective advantage, and three centuries is long enough for selection to
operate.

---

### Two readings I am not endorsing

**That the lost word is the Tetragrammaton.** Widely asserted and unevidenced for
the Craft degrees. The Tetragrammaton is not lost, as Chapters 10 and 12
established; its letters are fully available and what went was its pronunciation
and its setting. Where later Masonic bodies make this identification explicitly, it
is nineteenth-century and attributable, and Chapter 35 handles it.

**That the lost word is recoverable by cipher.** Chapter 16 ran the best-known
attempt on related material and Appendix F sets out why the substituted word cannot
be a cipher in any case: it varies between jurisdictions and has changed over time.
**A cipher that its own transmitters do not transmit stably is not a cipher.**

---

### What is actually lost

If the reader takes one sentence from this chapter, it should be this.

**What the third degree stages as the loss of a word is the loss of the conditions
under which a word means anything** — and those conditions are exactly what an
initiatory system is otherwise in the business of supplying.

The candidate has been given a place, a company, an office structure, a set of
obligations, and a room in which these things hold. He has everything a word would
need in order to work.

And then he is told the word is gone.

It is the most elegant thing in the Craft, and the likeliest explanation is that
nobody put it there on purpose.
