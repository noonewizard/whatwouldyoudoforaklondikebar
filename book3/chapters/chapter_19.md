# CHAPTER NINETEEN
## Substituted Words

The Craft says the word is lost and hands over a substitute. Within about twenty
years, another body was saying it had been found.

This chapter is about what substitution means historically, about the degree that
undid the refusal, and about the one place in this book where the evidence does not
go the way the rest of the argument goes.

---

### Substitution as an ordinary procedure

Begin with the unmysterious version, because it is the commonest and it explains
most of the cases.

Substitution is what a community does when a required element is unavailable and
the practice must continue. It is everywhere in religious and legal life, and it is
almost always explicit rather than concealed.

*Adonai* for the Tetragrammaton: a substitution, openly known, universally
practiced, and never mistaken by anyone inside the tradition for the thing itself.

Prayer for sacrifice in rabbinic Judaism after 70 CE: a substitution on a much
larger scale, articulated and defended.

A deputy for an absent officer; an acting appointment; a caretaker government. In
every case the substitute does the work and declares that it is standing in.

**The declaration is the important part.** A substitution that announces itself is
a different institutional act from a replacement that conceals the change — and the
Masonic case is emphatically the first kind. The candidate is not told he has
received the Master's Word. He is told he has received something to use instead.

---

### What varies

A fact that disposes of a good deal of speculation, and which should be stated
before anything is built on the substituted word.

**It is not the same everywhere.** The substituted word differs between
constitutions, and the forms in use have changed over time. English practice,
Scottish practice, Irish practice and the many American jurisdictions do not all
transmit the same thing, and none of them regards the others as having got it
wrong.

Two consequences follow immediately.

**It cannot be a cipher.** A cipher's whole point is that a determinate output can
be recovered by rule. A token that its own transmitting institutions do not
transmit identically is not carrying encoded information, because there is no
stable ciphertext to decode.

**Its function does not depend on its letters.** If the letters mattered, variation
would be a problem, and somebody would have tried to fix it. Nobody has, because
the token's job — to be conspicuously *not* the thing sought — works regardless of
what it is. That is precisely why the letters were free to drift.

---

### The Royal Arch

And then the degree that answers the question the Craft refuses.

The Holy Royal Arch appears in the record in the 1740s. Its early history is
genuinely contested and this book will not pretend otherwise: who developed it,
where, and out of what, are open questions on which competent scholars differ.

Its relation to the Craft differs by constitution, and the differences are not
cosmetic. In England it is treated as the completion of the third degree. In Ireland
and Scotland the arrangements differ. In much of the United States it sits within
the Chapter as part of a different grouping altogether.

Its subject is the **recovery of a concealed divine Name.** A vault is opened.
Something is found. What the third degree declared unavailable is supplied.[^1]

---

### The impossible sentence

The Union of 1813, which ended sixty years of schism between the Antients and the
Moderns in England, produced one of the strangest formulas in the history of
institutional religion.

The Articles of Union declared that pure Antient Masonry consists of three degrees
and no more — **including** the Supreme Order of the Holy Royal Arch.[^2]

Read it twice. It affirms the number three and admits a fourth thing in the same
breath. It is grammatically impossible and institutionally shrewd: two bodies with
incompatible practices needed a formula that let both continue, and this was it.

The sentence is worth dwelling on because of what it reveals. **An institution that
had to choose between its doctrine and its practice chose a form of words that
preserved both**, and then lived inside the contradiction for two centuries without
apparent discomfort. That is a fact about how traditions actually work, and it is
worth more than any amount of speculation about hidden intentions.

---

### The one open question

Now the part where this book's general argument meets an exception, and I want to
handle it carefully because the temptation to smooth it over is considerable.

**The general argument** of this volume, and of the two before it, is that Hebraic
and Kabbalistic readings of Masonry are nineteenth-century overlays. The evidence
for this is strong and consistent: the vocabulary enters with Lévi in the 1850s and
reaches Masonry through Pike in 1871; the Craft degrees contain no gematria, no
correspondences, no sefirotic structure; everything traceable is late.

**The Royal Arch does not fit.**

Its subject is the concealment and recovery of a divine Name. That is not a generic
biblical borrowing of the kind the Craft degrees make — pillars, a temple, a
builder. The concealment and recovery of the Name is a specific preoccupation with
a long history in Jewish tradition, and here it is in a Masonic context in the
1740s, a century before Lévi, with no obvious route.

**And the route is not the only thing missing. So is the demonstration that a route
is needed.**

The material is constructible from sources any educated Protestant of the 1740s
could reach. The Temple, the Name, the priesthood, the destruction, the loss: all of
it is in the Bible and in the Christian Hebraist literature that had been in print
since the sixteenth century. Knorr von Rosenroth's *Kabbala Denudata* had been
available in Latin since 1677, and men who read Latin could read it — which is
availability, not transmission, and Chapter 34 sets out the difference.

Against a Jewish-mystical source: the degree's apparatus is architectural and
vault-centerd, not sefirotic. There is no Tree, no emanation, no letter-cosmology.
If Kabbalah were the source, one would expect some of its distinctive machinery to
have come along, and none of it did.

**Verdict: genuinely disputed. E4 in this book's scheme.** Not established in either
direction.

---

### Why the exception is reported rather than resolved

Two of the three volumes of this series have now reached this point and left it
open, and a reader entitled to a conclusion may find that unsatisfying. The reason
is worth giving.

An argument with no exceptions should be distrusted. This book's general thesis
about Hebraic readings of Masonry is well supported across a wide range of cases,
and that support would be worth less, not more, if I could also make the one
awkward case come out right. The Royal Arch is the place where the thesis is
weakest, and saying so is a condition of the thesis being worth anything elsewhere.

**What would settle it** is archival: evidence connecting the degree's
eighteenth-century compilers to Christian-Hebraist reading. A library list, a
correspondence, a subscription, a marginal note. That work has not been done, this
study is not equipped to do it, and it is named in the research gaps as the single
most valuable piece of research anyone could undertake on this subject.

Until it is done, the honest position is that a Masonic degree of the 1740s treats
a Jewish theme with no demonstrated connection to Jewish sources, that it could
have been assembled from Christian materials, and that nobody has shown which.

---

### A note on what jurisdictional variation means

This book has repeatedly said that Masonic practice varies between constitutions,
and it is worth pausing on what that implies, because it is treated in the popular
literature as a minor administrative fact and it is not.

**There is no such thing as Masonic ritual.** There are Masonic rituals, plural,
which differ in wording, in staging, in the number and function of officers, in
lectures, in what is explained and what is left alone — and which differ enough that
a visitor from one constitution attending another will notice constantly.

**Three consequences bear on everything in this book.**

**Any claim about "the Masonic word" must specify whose.** A great deal of writing
on this subject quotes a form, from an unstated jurisdiction, at an unstated date,
and proceeds as though it were universal. The form is then subjected to
transformation, and the transformation's output depends entirely on the choice of
form.

**Variation is itself evidence about origins.** A text transmitted from a single
authoritative source and guarded would not diverge this way. What diverges like this
is material transmitted orally, locally, by memory and apprenticeship, with
periodic local revision — which is exactly what the documentary record shows
happening.

**And variation constrains what can be carrying information.** Chapter 38's test
applies: a feature that differs between jurisdictions is not stable, and an unstable
feature cannot be encoding a doctrine. The substituted word varies; the structural
fact of substitution does not.

**What survives divergence is what nobody was willing to change**, and across three
centuries and six continents the Craft has been willing to change nearly everything
except the shape of the thing: a word, a manner of exchanging it, a loss at the
summit, and a substitute declared as one.

---

### What substitution teaches

Return to the Craft, and to the difference between the two degrees.

**The Royal Arch terminates.** A word is sought, a word is found, the seeking is
over. Whatever the recovered Name means, its function as an engine is spent: the
candidate now has it.

**The Craft's substitution does not terminate.** The search remains open for the
length of a member's life, and it remains open in a form that does not depend on
his ever being given anything further.

Both are legitimate designs and they do different work. But if the unit of analysis
is the career rather than the ceremony — which is the finding of the preceding
volume — then the deferring version is the one still operating thirty years later,
and the recovering version has finished its job on the evening it is conferred.

The Craft built the engine that does not stop. Very probably by accident.
