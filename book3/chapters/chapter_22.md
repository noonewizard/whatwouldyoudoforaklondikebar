# PART V — THE SECRET LANGUAGE

# CHAPTER TWENTY-TWO
## Words That Open Doors

There is a phrase in the Masonic literature that has generated more modern cipher
theory than anything else in the ritual, and it almost certainly means something
quite ordinary.

The instruction is that a word is not to be given at length. It is to be
**lettered, or halved**.

---

### What the phrase describes

In the printed exposures and in ritual editions, the communication of a word
between two Masons is not a matter of one party pronouncing it. It is an exchange.
The word is spelled rather than spoken, or it is divided between the two
participants — one giving a portion, the other supplying the next, alternating —
so that neither utters the whole of it.[^1]

The formulae vary. The wording varies between jurisdictions and between periods,
and this book reproduces none of it. The *procedure* is what matters and the
procedure is public: a word co-produced by two people rather than delivered by one.

### Why it is not a cipher

Start with what a cipher is, since Chapter 23 will formalise it.

A cipher transforms a plaintext into a ciphertext by a rule, such that applying the
rule in reverse recovers the original. The defining feature is **substitution or
transposition of the message itself**: what is transmitted is not what was meant,
and a key or method is required to get from one to the other.

Nothing of the kind happens here.

The letters of the word are the letters of the word. Nothing is substituted,
transposed or transformed. What changes is the *division of labour between two
speakers* — who says which part — and that is a fact about the performance, not
about the text.

**Division of delivery is not encoding.** The plaintext passes intact; it is merely
passed in pieces, by two mouths instead of one.

A modern reader encountering "halve it" without the context reaches for the
familiar category, and the familiar category is wrong. Chapter 43 examines the
constructions built on this misreading; what matters here is why the misreading is
so natural, and the answer is that the word "halve" sounds like an operation, and
operations are what ciphers have.

---

### What it actually does

Three functions, and the first is the one nobody notices.

**It makes the exchange unforgeable by an eavesdropper.**

This is the important point and it repays being spelled out. Suppose an impostor
overhears a word given in full. He now has it, and he can produce it on demand.

Now suppose the word is exchanged by alternation. The challenger gives the first
letter; the respondent must supply the second; the challenger the third, and so on.
An impostor who has heard the whole exchange once still has the word — but an
impostor who has heard *nothing*, and is attempting to bluff, cannot follow. He
cannot wait to hear what is said and repeat it. **He must know what comes next, and
the challenger controls when to stop and let him supply it.**

This is a challenge-response protocol, arrived at by people with a practical problem
and no theory. It is the same structural insight that modern authentication rests
on, and Chapter 20 set out why the convergence is unsurprising.

**It marks the word as not ordinary.** A thing that may not be said whole is
thereby declared to be of a different order from things that may. The practice
performs the word's status every time it is used, which is more effective than
asserting it.

**And it requires two.** Like the catechism, the exchange cannot be performed
alone. A man practising in front of a mirror cannot do it. The word, in its proper
form, *only exists between people* — which is the observation the preceding volume
built its account of Masonic secrecy on, and which explains why publication of the
letters changed so little.

---

### The reverential reading, and its limits

There is a second explanation available and the sources incline toward it.

Where the printed literature explains the practice at all, it explains it in terms
of the word's dignity — the manner proper to communicating something of that
standing — rather than in terms of security against a third party.

This is the same gesture as the Jewish substitution of *Adonai* for the
Tetragrammaton: the word is acknowledged and not uttered, and the not-uttering is an
act of honour.

**Whether Masonic practice borrowed this or arrived at it independently is not
established and I do not think it can be.** The parallel is real. The route is not
documented. And the half-of-everything rule applies with force — a word may be
spoken whole or not spoken whole, and traditions that want to mark a word as
special have very few moves available.

The honest position is that the security function and the reverential function are
both present, that the sources emphasise the second, that the first works whether
or not anyone intended it, and that the two are not in competition. A practice can
do two things.

---

### How an instruction becomes a cipher

The mechanism by which "letter and halve it" turned into a decipherment key has four
steps, and they recur throughout this book.

**A technical term is met without its context.** Someone reads "halve it" who has
never seen the exchange performed and does not know it describes two people taking
turns.

**A familiar category is supplied.** The reader knows what ciphers are. "Halve"
sounds like an operation. The category is assumed rather than argued for.

**A rule is invented to fit.** Since no rule is stated in any source, the
interpreter must supply one — split the word at the midpoint, interleave the halves,
reverse one half, take alternate letters. Nothing constrains the choice.

**And the output is read as confirmation.** Any rule applied to a short word
produces *something*, and something was what was being looked for.

**The diagnostic is simple and it is the most useful sentence in this chapter.** A
genuine cipher has a rule that its users state and share. If the rule has to be
reconstructed by the person claiming the decipherment — and if two such people
reconstruct it differently, which they do — then there is no cipher. There is a
text and several readers.

---

### The general lesson

This case is small and it is the book's best illustration of something large.

Ritual instructions are **procedural**. They tell participants what to do. "Letter
and halve it" belongs with "advance by three steps" and "place the hand thus" — a
direction for performance, meaningless outside the performance, and not a claim
about anything.

Read as a text, a procedural instruction looks like a statement and invites
interpretation. Read as what it is, it is closer to a stage direction.

**A great deal of esoteric reading of ritual consists in treating stage directions
as doctrine**, and the mistake is almost impossible to avoid if you have only the
text. The exposures are transcriptions of a performance by people who had seen it;
the modern reader has the transcription and not the performance, and fills the gap
with the categories to hand.

Which is a problem with no general solution, only a general caution: **when a
ritual text seems to be saying something cryptic, ask first whether it is telling
somebody to do something.**
