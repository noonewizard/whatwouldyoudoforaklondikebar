# CHAPTER TWENTY-FOUR
## The Alphabet as Symbol

A letter is a mark that stands for a sound. That is the whole of its job, and it is
one of the most consequential inventions in human history precisely because the job
is so narrow.

But letters do not stay narrow. Every alphabetic culture has eventually asked what
the letters *mean*, and the asking has produced some of the most elaborate
intellectual structures in this book.

---

### What an alphabet is for

The invention worth appreciating before it is embellished.

Earlier writing systems represented things and ideas — a picture for an ox, a sign
for a city. They required hundreds or thousands of signs and years of training, and
literacy was a profession.

An alphabet represents **sounds**, and since any language uses only a few dozen
distinctive sounds, a couple of dozen signs suffice. The consequence is enormous:
writing becomes learnable in months rather than years, and by people who are not
professional scribes.

The trade is precision for generality. A pictogram of an ox tells you about an ox.
The letter that descends from it tells you about a sound, and can therefore be used
to write anything — including words for things nobody has ever drawn.

**An alphabet is a general-purpose technology and its power comes from its
emptiness.** The signs mean nothing in themselves, which is exactly why they can be
combined to mean anything.

That emptiness is what the symbolic traditions fill.

---

### How letters acquire meaning

Four routes, and it is useful to keep them apart because they have different
evidential status.

**Pictographic residue.** The oldest and the best grounded. Hebrew *alef* connects
to the word for ox, *bet* to house, *dalet* to door, *ayin* to eye, *pe* to mouth,
*resh* to head. The signs did descend from pictures and the names preserve the
memory. **This is philology and it is solid.**

**Numerical value.** Where the letters serve as numerals, each carries a number,
and the number can be interpreted. Chapter 14 dealt with this at length: the
numerical role is a practical consequence of having no separate numerals, and the
mystical reading is an inference laid on top.

**Position and shape.** Some letters are first, some last, some in the middle; some
are closed forms, some open; some descend below the line. Any of these can be
made to signify, and in Kabbalistic literature all of them have been.

**And theological assignment.** A tradition simply declares that a letter stands for
something — that *bet*, the first letter of the Torah, is the letter of blessing and
of dwelling, and that the Torah begins with it for a reason.

**The first route is a historical fact. The other three are interpretations**, and
interpretations of an object that was designed to be empty.

---

### The Greek case, which nobody calls mystical

A useful control, because it shows how much of the Hebrew material is a property of
alphabets rather than of Hebrew.

Greek letters also served as numerals. Greek also developed number-word play —
*isopsephy*, from *isos* (equal) and *psephos* (pebble, hence count). Greek
isopsephy produces exactly the same kind of striking equivalence that Hebrew
gematria does, and it was used in antiquity for graffiti, for oracles, for riddles,
and for at least one famous passage of the New Testament.

Alpha and omega, first and last, become a designation of divinity in Revelation —
which is letter-symbolism of the purest kind, in a Greek text, requiring no Hebrew
input whatever.

**Two unconnected traditions, both with alphabets doing double duty as numerals,
both developing number-word interpretation.** That is convergence from a shared
practical constraint. It is not evidence of a common esoteric inheritance, and any
argument that treats the Hebrew material as uniquely significant has to explain why
the Greek material, which behaves identically, is not usually regarded as an
occult system.

The answer, historically, is that the Renaissance found Hebrew exotic and Greek
familiar. That is a fact about Renaissance Europe, not about the alphabets.

### When the letters become the world

The strongest version of the position deserves stating properly, because this book
will spend a chapter on the text that states it.

*Sefer Yetzirah*, the "Book of Formation," presents the twenty-two letters not as
signs for things but as the **elements from which things are made**. God is
described as having engraved and carved and permuted them, and the world follows
from the operation. Letters are assigned to elements, to directions, to months, to
organs of the body.[^1]

This is not a claim that letters *describe* the world. It is a claim that they
*constitute* it, and Chapter 31 treats the text and the dating problem it presents.

Note what the position requires. If the letters are the elements of reality, then
operations on letters are operations on reality, and the techniques of Chapters 14
and 15 stop being interpretive devices and become something closer to engineering.
This is the premise that makes permutational practice — Abulafia's, above all —
intelligible as more than word games.

**It is also the Cratylan position in its most uncompromising form**, and it
inherits the Cratylan problem that Chapter 2 set out: the world is not divided one
way only, and a different language cuts it differently. If the Hebrew letters are
the elements of creation, then Greek, Latin and English are describing the world in
counterfeit currency — which is a position one can hold, and which several
traditions have held, and which requires holding that most of humanity has been
speaking nonsense.

### The magical alphabets

One further category, which Part VI treats at length and which belongs here for
its structure.

From the Renaissance onward, European magical literature circulated alphabets that
were not the alphabets of any spoken language: Celestial, Malachim, Passing the
River, Theban. Agrippa printed several in 1533; Theban is attributed to
Trithemius.[^2]

Their structure is worth noting. They are **substitution alphabets** — each sign
corresponds to a letter of the Latin alphabet, and a text written in them is
ordinary Latin or vernacular text in disguise. They are not languages. They have no
vocabulary and no grammar of their own.

Which means they are, technically, ciphers with a key space of one, exactly like
pigpen, and they concealed nothing from anyone who had seen the table.

**Their function was not secrecy. It was marking.** A text written in Celestial
script announces what kind of text it is. It looks like something from elsewhere,
and the looking is the point.

**None of them appears in Craft ritual in any period.** They were adopted
enthusiastically by the Golden Dawn from 1888, which is where most modern readers
have encountered them, and the adoption is a fact about the late nineteenth century.

---

### The Masonic letters

Masonry has exactly one letter-symbol of importance, and its history illustrates
this chapter's argument neatly.

The letter **G** is suspended in the East in many American workings and in some
others, and is absent elsewhere. Where it is explained, it is explained as
Geometry — and, in most sources, also as God.

**Two explanations, offered simultaneously, by authorities who did not regard the
matter as settled.**

By the disagreement principle, that pattern indicates a symbol that arrived without
a fixed meaning and acquired readings afterwards. And there is a further
consideration that decides it: **the G is an English letter.** It works in English,
where God and Geometry both begin with it. It does not work in French, or in
Hebrew, or in Latin.

A symbol whose significance depends on the initial letter of two English words is
not an inheritance from antiquity. It is a piece of eighteenth-century English
wordplay, and the fact that it has been given a cosmic reading is a fact about the
readers.
