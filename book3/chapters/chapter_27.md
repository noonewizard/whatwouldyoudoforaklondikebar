# PART VI — MAGIC AND LANGUAGE

# CHAPTER TWENTY-SEVEN
## The Magical Word

On a papyrus from Egypt, written in Greek somewhere in the third or fourth century,
a practitioner is instructed to say this:

*ablanathanalba*

It is not a word in Greek. It is not a word in Egyptian, Hebrew, Aramaic, Coptic or
any other language of the region. It reads the same backwards as forwards, which is
clearly deliberate, and it appears across a wide range of texts and amulets, which
means it was transmitted rather than improvised.

It means nothing, and it was used for centuries.

---

### Voces magicae

The Greek Magical Papyri — a corpus assembled from Egyptian finds and spanning
roughly the second century BCE to the fifth CE — are full of these. *Voces magicae*,
"magical words": strings of syllables to be pronounced exactly, often at length,
often with instructions about breath and vowel sequences.

Some look as though they might once have been something. Fragments of Egyptian
divine names, Hebrew names in transcription, Aramaic. Others are clearly
constructed for shape: palindromes, wing-formations where a word is repeated with
one letter removed each line, sequences running through the seven Greek vowels.[^1]

Scholars have spent a long time trying to identify the originals and the results
are partial. Some elements resolve; many do not. The honest summary is that **the
corpus mixes recognisable borrowings with material that was never meaningful in any
language.**

And that mixture is the interesting part.

---

### Why unintelligibility might be the point

Four explanations have been offered and they are not exclusive.

**They are corruptions.** Foreign names transmitted through generations of copyists
who did not know the languages, degrading toward gibberish. True of some, certainly.

**They are deliberately foreign.** Power was widely held to reside in the ancient
and the distant, and a formula in an unknown tongue carried the authority of
elsewhere. This is a marketing argument and it is not cynical — the practitioners
seem to have believed it too.

**They are technically required.** Some texts insist that the sounds themselves
matter, that pronunciation must be exact, that meaning is irrelevant to the
operation. On this view the *voces* are not degraded words but a different kind of
thing: acoustic instruments rather than statements.

**And they cannot be checked.**

That last one deserves emphasis, because it is the observation an outsider can make
and the practitioners could not.

**A formula in a known language can be got wrong in a way anyone can identify.**
If a spell is in Greek and you misremember it, a Greek speaker will tell you. If it
is in *ablanathanalba*, there is no dictionary, no grammar, no native speaker, and
no possible correction. The practitioner's authority over the formula is total.

I want to be careful not to present this as fraud. It very probably was not, in
most cases. But whatever the intention, the structural effect is that
**unintelligible formulae are unfalsifiable formulae**, and traditions that use them
have acquired a property that traditions using ordinary language do not have.

---

### What magical language claims

Chapter 3 established the class of utterances that change the world by being
spoken, and found the power lying in the convention rather than in the sound. A
marriage is real because a society recognises the procedure.

**Magical utterance claims something different.** It claims that the words act on
the world directly — that the substance changes, the spirit comes, the fever
breaks — with no social convention mediating and no institution required to
recognise anything.

This is a genuinely different claim and the difference is not a matter of degree.

A performative fails if the speaker lacks standing. A magical formula, on its own
account, fails if the speaker lacks *knowledge or purity or technique* — but not
because a community declined to recognise him. The Greek papyri are full of
instructions for solitary operations, and the practitioner does not need a
congregation.

**This book does not adjudicate whether magical utterance works.** That question is
outside what historical method reaches, and the series has declined it in all three
volumes, in both directions. What can be reported is what the practitioners claimed,
what they did, and how the claims are structured — and the structure is what this
chapter is for.

---

### The distinction that holds

Here is the cleanest single division in this book, and it survives everything Part
VI throws at it.

**A magical word is directed at the cosmos. A Masonic word is directed at a
doorkeeper.**

The magical word is uttered to accomplish something in the world: to compel a
spirit, alter a substance, cause an event. It is aimed outward, at a reality
assumed to be responsive to correctly formed speech.

The Masonic word is exchanged to establish identity. It is aimed at a person, who
decides whether to admit another person. The effect it produces is admission, and
the mechanism is that a man in a chair is satisfied.

**One is an operation. The other is an authentication.**

Run the distinction against the Craft degrees and it holds without exception. There
is no invocation in Masonic ritual. No spirits are named. No results are claimed.
There is no operator — the officers are performers of a fixed script, not
practitioners composing an operation with an aim in view. Nothing is expected to
happen in the world because a word was said.

The preceding volume reached this from the direction of ritual structure. This
chapter reaches it from the direction of magical practice, and the two accounts
agree.

---

### What operative practice leaves behind

One further argument, and it is the strongest available on this point.

Operative magical practice leaves **operative texts.** Instructions. Materials.
Timings. Names. Claimed results. The grimoire tradition of Chapter 28 is enormous
and explicit: it tells you what to say, when, wearing what, having fasted how long,
and what will happen.

Where bodies adjacent to Freemasonry actually practised theurgy, they did this
too. Martinez de Pasqually's Élus Coëns, from the 1760s, practised invocation, and
we know because the instructions survive — named founder, dated documents, explicit
procedures.[^2]

**When operative practice enters an organisation, it does so visibly.**

Craft ritual contains none of it. Not in the eighteenth-century exposures, written
by hostile parties who would have been delighted to report it. Not in the ritual
editions. Not in the lodge records.

**That absence is not an absence of evidence. It is evidence of the absence of that
kind of material**, and it is about as strong as a negative finding in this field
gets.

---

### Why the mistake persists

If the distinction is this clean, why does the occult reading of Masonic words
survive?

Three reasons, and Chapter 35 develops them.

**Because Masonic and magical ritual share a form.** Both use graded initiation,
prescribed wording, ceremonial space, restricted knowledge. The form was borrowed —
by the magical orders, from Masonry, in the nineteenth century, and in that
direction only. Book I established the chain.

**Because "magic" is a word that moved.** Before the nineteenth century it meant
the manipulation of hidden properties in things. After Lévi it meant the operation
of will through symbol upon the operator, and under *that* definition a great deal
of Masonic practice falls inside. The preceding volume worked this out at length;
the short version is that the practice did not change, the word moved across it.

**And because a word that opens a door is genuinely impressive.** Chapter 1 began
there and it remains true. A man says four syllables and a shut door opens. The
mechanism is entirely conventional and the experience is not.
