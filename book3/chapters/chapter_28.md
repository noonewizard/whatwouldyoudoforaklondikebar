# CHAPTER TWENTY-EIGHT
## Divine Names in Ritual Magic

Open almost any European grimoire and you will find the God of Israel being
addressed in Hebrew by a Christian operator who does not read Hebrew, in order to
compel a spirit with an Arabic-sounding name to perform a service of no religious
character whatever.

This is not a corruption of the tradition. It is the tradition, and its structure
repays attention.

---

### What the grimoires are

A grimoire is an instruction manual. That is the useful thing to hold onto,
because the genre is often discussed as though it were devotional literature and it
is not. It is procedural: do this, then this, wearing this, at this hour, saying
these words, and the following will occur.

The corpus runs from the medieval period into the nineteenth century and beyond.
The *Sworn Book of Honorius*, the *Ars Notoria*, the *Key of Solomon*, the *Lesser
Key*, the *Heptameron*, the *Grimorium Verum*, the *Grand Grimoire*. Manuscripts
circulated, were copied, altered, attributed to Solomon or Moses or Honorius, and
eventually printed.[^1]

Their contents are remarkably consistent in shape. Preparation of the operator —
fasting, washing, abstinence, often for days. Preparation of instruments — knives,
wands, parchment, ink, each with its own specification. Preparation of space — a
circle, drawn, inscribed. Timing — planetary hours, days of the week, phases of the
moon. And then the conjuration: a long address, naming the spirit and naming God,
in which the spirit is commanded to appear.

**The commanding is done by divine names.** That is the engine of the whole
apparatus.

---

### The logic

The reasoning is explicit in the texts and it is coherent on its own terms.

A spirit is a creature. God is its creator, and stands to it in a relation of
absolute authority. An operator who cannot compel the spirit in his own name can
compel it in God's — by invoking the names, and thereby invoking the authority they
carry.

The conjurations read accordingly. *I conjure thee by* — and then a list, sometimes
running to a page, of divine names: Hebrew names in transliteration, Greek epithets,
Latin phrases, names from the tradition that are not in any scripture at all.

**This is an argument from delegated authority, and its structure is legal.** The
operator is not claiming power. He is claiming standing to invoke someone else's,
and the spirit is being served with a writ.

Notice that this makes the practice, on its own account, thoroughly orthodox — which
is precisely what the grimoire authors maintained and the Church did not accept. A
man compelling a demon in the name of God could describe himself as exercising
dominion granted at creation. The authorities took the view that the traffic itself
was the offence, regardless of which names were used.

---

### Where the names came from, and what happened to them

Here is the part relevant to this book's argument.

The Hebrew divine names in the grimoires arrived through a long chain, and the
chain degraded them.

**Some are genuine**: *Adonai*, *Elohim*, *Tetragrammaton* (as a Greek description
rather than a name), *Shaddai*, *Eheieh*. These are recognisable and their
provenance is clear.

**Some are artefacts.** *Agla* is a notarikon — the initials of *Atah Gibor
Le'olam Adonai*, "Thou art mighty forever, O Lord" — which is a real Hebrew phrase
compressed by a real Hebrew technique, and which then circulated in Latin magical
texts as though it were itself a name.[^2] **A technique's output became an
object.**

**And some are corruptions**, transliterated by scribes without Hebrew, copied by
further scribes with less, until the form in a seventeenth-century manuscript is
several removes from anything pronounceable in the original.

This is the ordinary fate of borrowed sacred vocabulary and it is not a scandal.
What matters is the direction of travel: **from a tradition that used these names
reverentially and did not use them operatively, into a tradition that used them
operatively and had no access to the original context.**

Jewish practice, as Chapter 10 established, restricted the Name's use. The
grimoire tradition multiplied it. The two could hardly be further apart in
intention, and the second is drawing on the first without inheriting anything of
its discipline.

### Abracadabra

The most famous magical word in English has a documented history and it is worth
giving, because it is unusually clean.

It appears in the *Liber Medicinalis*, a medical poem attributed to Quintus Serenus
Sammonicus, from around the third century CE. The instruction is for tertian fever:
write the word on a piece of parchment, and beneath it write it again with the last
letter removed, and again, and again, until a single letter remains — producing an
inverted triangle. Wear it round the neck.[^3]

Three things about this.

**The form is the operation.** It is not the sound that matters but the diminishing
shape, which enacts the reduction it is meant to produce. The fever is to dwindle
as the word dwindles. This is sympathetic logic of the most legible kind.

**The etymology is unknown.** Proposals connect it to Aramaic, to Hebrew, to a
Gnostic deity name, and none is secure. Sammonicus does not explain it and
apparently did not know.

**And it is a *written* charm.** The patient wears the parchment. This is textual
amulet practice, which is extensively documented across medieval Europe, and which
Don Skemer's study established as a normal rather than a marginal activity — used
by ordinary people, frequently with clerical involvement.[^4]

**The word on the page does the work.** Not spoken; worn. That is a different
modality from the conjuration and it is at least as widespread.

### The Masonic comparison, one more time

Run the whole apparatus against Craft ritual and the result is the same as Chapter
27's, with sharper edges.

**No divine names are used operatively.** The Volume of the Sacred Law is present
and is treated with respect, but nothing is invoked *by* any name and nothing is
commanded.

**There are no spirits.** Nothing is summoned, addressed, bound or dismissed.

**There is no operator.** The officers perform a script; nobody composes anything;
nobody has an aim beyond conducting the ceremony correctly.

**There are no claimed results.** No grimoire-style promise that the operation will
produce an effect in the world.

**And there is no apparatus.** No circle, no planetary hours, no consecrated
instruments, no purificatory regime of the grimoire kind.

The preceding volume put this structurally: **a correspondence system is a
technology for operators, and Masonic ritual is a technology for candidates.**
Agrippa's tables are for somebody who intends to compose an operation and perform
it. They are useless to a man having something done to him.

Chapter 30 tests that distinction against the cases where it might fail.

---

### What the grimoires do share with Masonry

To be fair to the resemblance, since this book has spent the chapter denying it.

Both traditions take **preparation** seriously — the operator fasts and washes, the
candidate is prepared and divested. Both use **prescribed wording** and treat
deviation as a fault. Both use **ceremonial space** marked off from ordinary space.
Both attach significance to **timing and direction**.

These are real similarities and they are the entailed features of ceremony as such.
Any system that wants to mark an occasion as not-ordinary will reach for
preparation, prescription, boundary and orientation, because the repertoire is
small.

**What is absent from the Masonic side is everything arbitrary** — the specific
names, the specific hours, the specific instruments, the specific claimed effects.
And shared arbitrary detail is how descent is established.

Convergence on form. No shared content. The finding of all three volumes, in the
place where the reader would most expect it to break down.
