# CHAPTER THIRTY-TWO
## The Sephirot

Ten of them, and almost everything a modern reader thinks they know about them is
younger than the printing press.

---

### The structure

The *sefirot* are the ten emanations through which, in Kabbalistic thought, the
hidden and unknowable God becomes manifest and creative.

In their developed form, with the names that became standard:

**Keter** (crown), **Chokhmah** (wisdom), **Binah** (understanding), **Chesed**
(loving-kindness), **Gevurah** (severity), **Tiferet** (beauty), **Netzach**
(victory or endurance), **Hod** (splendour), **Yesod** (foundation), **Malkhut**
(kingdom).

They are arranged in three columns — usually described as a right pillar of mercy,
a left pillar of severity, and a middle pillar of balance — and connected by paths.
Together with the twenty-two letters of the alphabet assigned to the paths, they
make the thirty-two of *Sefer Yetzirah*.

The structure is used to think about an enormous range of subjects: the process of
creation, the structure of the soul, the relation between divine attributes, the
condition of exile and repair. It is a diagram that can be applied to almost
anything, which is both its power and, for this book's purposes, its problem.

---

### The history, compressed

The word *sefirot* is in *Sefer Yetzirah*, where it means something closer to
primordial numbers than to emanations.

The developed doctrine emerges in **Provence and Spain from the later twelfth
century**: the *Bahir*, then the circle around Isaac the Blind, then Gerona, then
the *Zohar* — which appears in Castile in the 1280s, written in an idiosyncratic
Aramaic, presented as an ancient midrash of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai, and attributed
by modern scholarship substantially to Moses de León.

That attribution is Scholem's and it is broadly accepted, though not without
qualification: Yehuda Liebes and others have argued for a circle of authors rather
than a single one, and the question of how much older material the text
incorporates remains live.[^1]

Then **Safed in the sixteenth century**: Cordovero's systematisation, and Isaac
Luria's reworking, which introduced the ideas of divine contraction, the shattering
of the vessels, and cosmic repair — and which became, through Luria's students, the
dominant form of Kabbalah in the Jewish world.

**None of this is ancient in the sense the popular literature means.** The Zohar is
thirteenth-century. Lurianic Kabbalah is sixteenth. Both are considerably later
than the Talmud, and both were controversial within Judaism.

---

### The diagram is late

Here is the detail that this chapter exists to establish, because it disposes of a
surprising amount.

**The familiar Tree of Life diagram — ten circles, three columns, twenty-two
connecting paths — is a printed-era object.**

The earliest widely known printed version appears on the title page of Paulus
Ricius's Latin *Portae Lucis* of 1516, a translation of Gikatilla's *Sha'arei
Orah*, showing a bearded figure holding a tree of ten spheres.[^2]

Manuscript diagrams exist earlier and the concept is certainly older than any
drawing of it. But **the standardised image, with the paths fixed and the
arrangement settled, belongs to print** — and print is what made it standard, because
a printed diagram is identical in every copy and a manuscript one is not.

This matters for two reasons.

**A diagram invites operations that a doctrine does not.** Once the structure is
drawn, one can trace paths, count them, assign things to them, and move around
them. The visual form generates practices that the textual form does not obviously
support.

**And the assignments are not fixed by the tradition.** Which letter goes on which
path is not settled in the Jewish sources, and different authorities differ. That
latitude becomes very important in Chapter 35, because the nineteenth century
filled it with material from outside.

---

### What the Sephirot are not

Three corrections, since all three are standard in the popular literature.

**They are not a map of the psyche.** That reading is twentieth-century, owing a
great deal to the encounter between occultism and depth psychology, and it is not
what the Jewish sources are doing. They are describing the divine, and the
correspondence to the human is by analogy — the human being made in the image —
rather than by identity.

**They are not tarot.** There is no tarot in Jewish Kabbalah. None. The
correspondence between the twenty-two letters and the twenty-two trumps is
**Éliphas Lévi's, from the 1850s**, and Chapter 35 deals with it. Any book that
presents the tarot-Hebrew correspondence as traditional is repeating a
nineteenth-century French innovation as though it were medieval Spanish.

**And they are not a system of universal correspondences.** The Jewish sources
assign a great deal to the *sefirot* — attributes, patriarchs, parts of the body,
stages of a process — but they do not constitute the comprehensive
letter-planet-metal-colour-perfume-deity grammar that a modern reader expects. That
comprehensiveness comes from the Hermetic reconstruction, and it comes from
Agrippa's tables rather than from Kabbalah.

---

### The pillars, and Masonry

Now the claim this chapter has to test, since it is the most frequently asserted
Masonic-Kabbalistic link.

**The claim:** the two pillars of Masonic symbolism — named in the ritual after the
pillars of Solomon's Temple — represent the Kabbalistic pillars of Severity and
Mercy.

Apply the eight questions.

*When does the connection first appear?* Nineteenth century.

*Who made it?* Lévi, then Pike in 1871, then the Golden Dawn.

*What documents it?* *Morals and Dogma* and Golden Dawn papers. No
eighteenth-century Masonic source.

*Was the person a Mason?* Pike yes. Lévi no. The Golden Dawn figures were Masons
making the claim inside a non-Masonic body.

*Was the system available?* Yes — Knorr's *Kabbala Denudata* from 1677.

*Is there evidence of transmission?* **None for the eighteenth century.**

*Is the interpretation modern?* Yes.

*Accepted, disputed or speculative?* **Speculative.**

**And there is a simpler explanation that fits everything.** The two pillars are in
1 Kings 7 and 2 Chronicles 3, by name, with dimensions. A tradition organised around
Solomon's Temple, using biblical furniture throughout, would put the Temple's
pillars in its symbolism without needing any Kabbalah at all.

**Verdict: E5.** A nineteenth-century overlay on a biblical borrowing.

---

### The tempting error

One methodological note, because this chapter's material is where the temptation is
strongest.

The Tree of Life is an extraordinarily flexible diagram. Ten positions, three
columns, twenty-two paths, and a tradition of applying it to whatever needs
analysing. Almost any structure with roughly that many elements can be mapped onto
it, and once mapped, the correspondence looks compelling.

**That flexibility is exactly why a mapping proves nothing.**

A diagram that can accommodate any structure has no discriminating power. If
Masonic symbolism maps onto the Tree, so does the layout of a cathedral, the plot
of a novel, and the org chart of a medium-sized firm — and the fact that mappings
can be constructed tells us about the diagram's generality rather than about any
historical relation.

Chapter 39 formalises this as **over-determination**: a scheme that explains
everything excludes nothing, and a reading that cannot fail is not a finding. It is
the single most common failure in the material this book examines and the Tree of
Life is its most productive instrument.
