# CHAPTER THIRTY-THREE
## The Alphabet as Cosmos

In thirteenth-century Spain a man sat alone at night with a pen, writing
combinations of Hebrew letters, moving his head in prescribed directions as he
pronounced them, until something happened to him.

Abraham Abulafia called this *hokhmat ha-tseruf*, the science of combination, and
he described what it produced in language that left his contemporaries deeply
uneasy.

---

### What Abulafia did

The technique, as far as the texts allow reconstruction, involved systematic
permutation of the letters of divine names — writing them, combining them in
sequence, pronouncing the combinations with prescribed breathing and prescribed
movements of the head corresponding to the vowels.

It was not interpretation. It was a **practice**, undertaken for its effects, and
the effects Abulafia reports are substantial: an untying of the knots that bind the
soul, an overflowing, a condition he describes in terms of prophecy.[^1]

He was not a marginal figure in his own estimation. He travelled to attempt an
audience with Pope Nicholas III — an episode that could have ended very badly and
did not, the Pope having died before the meeting — and he attracted enough
opposition from Solomon ibn Adret, the leading halakhic authority of the day, to
have his works condemned.

---

### The Scholem–Idel dispute, and why this book takes a side

Here is a place where scholarship divides and the division matters for Book III's
argument, so it should be stated rather than glossed.

**Gershom Scholem** created the modern academic study of Jewish mysticism, and his
synthesis placed the *theosophical* strand at the centre: the *sefirot*, the
structure of the divine, the *Zohar*. Abulafia's ecstatic and linguistic practice
appears in Scholem's account as a significant but somewhat marginal current.

**Moshe Idel** has argued, across a series of books from the late 1980s, that this
underrates the linguistic strand — that ecstatic Kabbalah is older, more central,
and more continuous than Scholem allowed, and that the techniques of combination
are not a byway but a main road.[^2]

**This book's Part VII depends on Idel being substantially right**, since its
subject is language, and I am saying so rather than assuming it.

If Idel is right, then Jewish tradition contains a developed practice of operating
on letters for transformative effect, and that practice is a genuine historical
phenomenon of the kind the Renaissance Christian Hebraists were reaching for when
they reached for Kabbalah.

If Scholem is right, the letter-practice is a smaller thing and the main tradition
is about divine structure rather than linguistic technique.

**Neither position helps the Masonic claims**, which is worth noting before anyone
builds on the concession. The dispute is about the internal shape of Jewish
mysticism. It does not supply a route to eighteenth-century London under either
reading.

---

### What the practice assumes

Abulafia's technique makes sense only on a particular view of language, and the
view is *Sefer Yetzirah*'s.

**If letters are the elements of creation, permuting them is an operation on
reality.** Not a meditation *about* reality, not a symbolic representation of it,
but a manipulation of the material from which it is constituted.

This is the strongest version of the Cratylan position — that names are natural
rather than conventional — and it is held here with full consistency. The letters
are not arbitrary. They are prior to the things they compose.

**And it explains why the practice is Hebrew-specific.** If the letters of the
Hebrew alphabet are the elements of creation, then combinations in Hebrew have a
purchase that combinations in Latin or Castilian do not. Abulafia's technique does
not translate, and on its own premises it could not.

That is a point worth holding for Chapter 35, where a tradition will do exactly
this technique in English and French, on the assumption that the effects transfer.

---

### The inward turn

One feature of Abulafia's practice deserves separate notice because of what it
anticipates.

**The operation is on the practitioner.**

The permutations are not aimed at compelling a spirit, altering a substance, or
producing an event in the world. They are performed to produce a change in the
person performing them: the untying of the soul's knots, the arrival of prophecy.
The target is internal.

This distinguishes it sharply from the grimoire tradition of Chapter 28, where the
whole apparatus is aimed outward at an entity to be commanded.

**And it anticipates, by six hundred years, the relocation of magic that Lévi would
perform in the 1850s** — the shift from magic as operation on the world to magic as
operation on the operator, which the preceding volume identified as the hinge on
which the modern occult understanding of ritual turns.

I want to be careful about what that anticipation establishes. **It is not a
transmission claim.** Lévi read a great deal, including Kabbalistic material in
Latin translation, and it is possible that something reached him. But the
possibility is not evidence, the specific route has not been traced, and the
simpler account is that two traditions concerned with what ritual does to a
practitioner arrived at similar emphases because the subject constrains the
answers.

Convergence, again, in a place where descent would be convenient.

---

### The Masonic non-connection

Brief, because the material does not support anything longer.

**Craft ritual contains no permutational practice.** No combination of letters, no
prescribed pronunciation of divine names, no technique performed for its effect on
the practitioner's state.

What Masonic ritual does with words is exchange them, and Chapter 20 established
that the exchange is a credential operation.

**The one superficial resemblance is the divided delivery of a word** — the practice
Chapter 22 examined — and a reader who knows Abulafia might see a family likeness in
the idea that a word is handled letter by letter rather than spoken whole.

The likeness does not survive examination. Abulafia divides a word to *recombine*
it, generating new sequences for their effect. Masonic practice divides a word to
*withhold* utterance, and nothing is recombined; the plaintext passes intact and
the letters are the letters.

**One operation generates. The other conceals delivery.** They look alike and do
opposite things, which is a pattern this book has met before and will meet again in
Part IX.

---

### The dangerous attraction

A closing observation about why this material exerts such a pull.

Abulafia's practice is the best historical instance of something that a great many
people want to be true: that language, properly operated, can transform the person
operating it. It is documented, it is serious, it was taken seriously by people who
disagreed with it, and it produced effects its practitioner recorded in detail.

**That is a genuine finding and it does not license the extensions built on it.**

It does not establish that the effects were what Abulafia thought they were. It
does not establish that the technique works in other languages. It does not
establish that any other tradition inherited it. And it certainly does not
establish that an eighteenth-century English fraternity, whose ritual contains no
permutation and no divine names used operatively, is doing a version of the same
thing.

What it establishes is that somebody did this, in Spain, in the thirteenth century,
and wrote it down.

That is a considerable thing to have established, and it is all that the evidence
supports.
