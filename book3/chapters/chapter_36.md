# PART VIII — NUMBERS

# CHAPTER THIRTY-SIX
## Three, Five, Seven

Masonic ritual uses numbers constantly and does not explain them, which has been an
invitation.

Three degrees, three principal officers, three lights, three knocks, three steps.
Five orders of architecture, five senses, five points. Seven liberal arts and
sciences; seven required to make a lodge perfect, in the traditional formula.

The esoteric literature reads these as survivals of a numerical doctrine. This
chapter argues that they need no such explanation, and that the reason is more
interesting than the doctrine would have been.

---

### Three, and the structure of a group

Three is the commonest number in Masonic symbolism and there are good reasons for
it that have nothing to do with mysticism.

**Three is the smallest number that makes a structure.** Two parties can only
agree or disagree; a third makes a majority possible, a witness available, a
mediator conceivable. Every deliberative body in human history has discovered
this, and the number of tripartite institutional arrangements in the world is not
evidence of a shared esoteric inheritance.

**Three is the smallest number that makes a sequence.** Beginning, middle, end.
Past, present, future. A narrative with two positions is a contrast; with three it
is a development. Van Gennep's account of rites of passage — separation,
liminality, incorporation — is a three-part structure because a transition requires
a before, a during and an after, and there is no way to have fewer.

**Three is what a small room needs.** A Master and two Wardens is the minimum
arrangement for conducting business with the officers distributed around a space.
The three principal officers of a lodge sit at three stations because a room has
corners and a ceremony needs a circuit.

**And three is the limit of immediate apprehension.** There is a well-established
finding in cognitive psychology that small quantities up to about three or four are
apprehended immediately, without counting — a phenomenon usually called
subitising. Beyond that we count. A ritual that wants a number to be *felt* rather
than *tallied* is constrained to work below that threshold.[^1]

None of this requires Pythagoras.

---

### Five

Five appears less often and its occurrences are traceable.

**Five orders of architecture** — Tuscan, Doric, Ionic, Corinthian, Composite. This
is not a mystical number. It is the standard enumeration of classical orders in the
architectural literature of the sixteenth century onward, from Serlio and Vignola,
and it was the common property of any educated eighteenth-century person with an
interest in building.

**Five senses.** Aristotelian, standard, and in every schoolbook.

**Five points**, in the third-degree material: a fivefold arrangement, and the
number is a consequence of the anatomy involved rather than of any prior
commitment.

**And the human body.** Five fingers, five toes, and the five-pointed figure that
results from a body with limbs extended — which is the Vitruvian material Chapter 45
treats.

**Every Masonic five has a source outside numerology**, and the sources are
architectural, Aristotelian or anatomical.

---

### Seven

Seven is the interesting one, because its Masonic uses are genuinely inherited —
just not from where the literature says.

**Seven liberal arts and sciences**: grammar, rhetoric, logic, arithmetic,
geometry, music, astronomy. The *trivium* and *quadrivium*, the curriculum of
medieval European education, codified by Martianus Capella and transmitted through
Boethius and Cassiodorus, and the standard structure of university study for a
thousand years.

**This is in the Old Charges.** The *Regius* manuscript of the 1390s discusses the
seven sciences and places geometry among them. It is the oldest substantive content
in the Masonic textual tradition, and it is straightforwardly a medieval educational
curriculum in a document written for masons.

**The seven required to make a lodge perfect**: a quorum rule, of the kind every
organisation has.

Where seven comes from more broadly is not mysterious either: the seven classical
planets, which are simply the celestial bodies visible to the naked eye that move
against the fixed stars, and the seven-day week that follows from them. Any
tradition in the Western world inherits seven without having to be taught it.

---

### The method: what a number has to do to be significant

Here is the test, and it is Part IX's method applied early.

**A number in a system is significant if its value is arbitrary.**

If the number could have been otherwise and was not, something chose it, and the
choice may carry information.

If the number is **entailed** — if it follows from the structure, the materials, or
the practical situation — then nothing chose it, and it carries no information
about anybody's intentions.

Apply this.

**Three officers**: entailed by the requirements of conducting a meeting in a room.
Not significant.

**Three degrees**: partially entailed. Two existed; a third was added under
institutional pressure. The count is a historical accident, not a design.

**Five orders**: entailed by the architectural literature. There are five because
Vignola said so.

**Seven liberal arts**: entailed by a medieval curriculum. There are seven because
Martianus Capella enumerated seven.

**Not one Masonic number survives the test as arbitrary**, and therefore not one
carries a numerical doctrine.

---

### Why the test matters

Because it works both ways, and a test that only disconfirms is not a test.

**An arbitrary number is genuinely informative.** If a ritual specified that a
candidate must circumambulate seventeen times, that would be worth investigating:
seventeen is not entailed by anything, and its presence would demand an
explanation.

**Thirty-three is the best Masonic example of a number that is nearly arbitrary and
is still not mystical.** The Scottish Rite has thirty-three degrees because the
Supreme Council at Charleston in 1801 arrived at that count by consolidating the
twenty-five degrees of the Rite of Perfection with additional material. The number
is not entailed; it could have been thirty-one or thirty-five. And the explanation
is administrative rather than doctrinal, and it is documented.

**An arbitrary number with a documented mundane origin is the strongest possible
case against a mystical reading**, because the alternative explanation is not merely
available but recorded.

---

### The seven that is not in the ritual

A useful negative case, because it shows the test working in the other direction.

If Masonry were organised around a numerical doctrine, we would expect the
canonical numbers to appear *systematically* — not merely to be present, but to
structure things.

Take seven. It is a number with enormous traditional weight: seven planets, seven
days, seven liberal arts, seven branches of the menorah, seven seals, seven
sacraments in one tradition, seven deadly sins.

**Masonic ritual uses it twice, in passing, and neither use is structural.**

The seven liberal arts appear in a lecture as an inherited curriculum. The quorum
formula is an administrative rule. **Nothing in the lodge is arranged in sevens.**
There are not seven officers, or seven stations, or seven steps, or seven
ornaments, or seven degrees.

**Compare a system that genuinely is organised numerically.** The Golden Dawn's
grade structure maps onto the ten sephirot, deliberately and completely — each grade
assigned to a position, with the correspondences stated in the order's own papers.
That is what a numerical organising principle looks like from inside, and it is
visible without any decipherment.

**Masonry has three of some things, two of others, five of a few, and no discernible
scheme**, which is exactly what one would expect from a ceremony assembled over
decades from whatever was to hand.

**An organising number organises.** Where it is genuinely present, you do not have
to hunt for it.

### The base-rate problem, previewed

One further consideration, which Chapter 39 formalises.

Masonic ritual contains a great many numbers. Officers, lights, steps, knocks,
degrees, tools, ornaments, jewels, orders, sciences, points, columns, years in the
traditional history.

The esoteric literature searches this set for numbers matching a list of
significant values — 3, 5, 7, 12, 33, 47, 72 — and reports the matches.

**The list of Masonic numbers is large. The list of significant numbers is large.
Matches are guaranteed.**

That is the multiple-comparisons problem, and reporting the hits without counting
the attempts is the commonest error in this entire literature. The preceding volume
demonstrated it computationally: the same procedure applied to root vegetables
produced the same density of striking results as applied to Masonic vocabulary.

Chapter 39 runs the numbers for this volume.
