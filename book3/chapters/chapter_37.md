# CHAPTER THIRTY-SEVEN
## Twelve and Forty-Seven

Two numbers with very different histories, and the second is the most consequential
number in Masonic literature.

---

### Twelve, and why it is everywhere

Twelve tribes. Twelve months. Twelve signs of the zodiac. Twelve apostles. Twelve
hours on a clock face, twice. Twelve inches, twelve pence, twelve in a dozen.

The ubiquity has been read as evidence of a shared ancient system, and the
explanation is considerably duller.

**Twelve is the most divisible small number.** It divides by 2, 3, 4 and 6. Ten
divides by 2 and 5 only. For a culture doing arithmetic without decimal notation,
dividing quantities into halves, thirds and quarters constantly, twelve is
enormously more convenient than ten, and duodecimal counting survives in English
measurement for exactly that reason.

**And the year contains about twelve lunations.** Twelve point three-seven, to be
more precise, which is why every lunar calendar in history has had to deal with the
remainder — by intercalating months, by adjusting, by accepting drift. The twelve is
astronomical and the awkwardness of the fraction is the reason calendars are
complicated.

**Twelve is entailed by arithmetic convenience and the sky.** A tradition that uses
twelve has not inherited a doctrine; it has noticed what its neighbours noticed.

Masonic use of twelve is slight, which is itself worth registering. It is not a
Masonic number in any significant sense.

---

### Forty-seven

And now the number that matters.

In the 1723 *Constitutions*, in the traditional history that opens the volume,
James Anderson refers to the forty-seventh proposition of Euclid's first book —
what everyone calls the theorem of Pythagoras — and associates it with the
foundation of Masonry.[^1]

The proposition itself is the familiar one: in a right-angled triangle, the square
on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides.

From this, an entire numerological literature. Forty-seven as a sacred number.
Forty-seven appearing in Masonic contexts. Forty-seven, occasionally, as a key to
the whole system.

**Run the test from the previous chapter. Is forty-seven arbitrary or entailed?**

It is entailed, and by something completely mundane. **Forty-seven is the
proposition's position in Euclid's ordering.** Euclid arranged Book I in a logical
sequence in which each result depends on those before it, and the Pythagorean
theorem falls where it falls because of what it needs. It is the forty-seventh
because forty-six things had to be established first.

Nobody chose the number. It is an index.

**A number that is a position in a list carries no information about anybody's
intentions**, and anyone treating forty-seven as significant is treating a page
number as a message.

---

### What Anderson was actually claiming

Set the numerology aside and the passage becomes considerably more interesting,
because it tells us something real about what Masonry thought it was in 1723.

Anderson is claiming that **geometry is foundational**. Not that a particular number
is magical, but that the science of measure and proportion underlies the craft, the
arts, and by extension the order of things.

**And that claim is a claim of its moment.**

The circle that produced the 1723 *Constitutions* was thick with Fellows of the
Royal Society. John Theophilus Desaguliers, Grand Master in 1719, was a Huguenot
refugee, an experimental philosopher, and the man who demonstrated Newton's
mechanics to paying audiences in London. Ric Berman's work has traced the network in
detail, and it is a Newtonian and Whig network before it is anything else.[^2]

Newton had shown, within living memory, that the heavens obey mathematics — that the
universe is a geometrical object with calculable behaviour.

**In that world, a fraternity organised around geometry was not archaic. It was the
most modern thing available.**

This matters because Masonry is habitually read backwards through its
nineteenth-century occult interpreters, who found in the square and compasses a
cosmic symbolism of enormous antiquity. The men of the 1720s were not looking back
to Egypt. They were looking at the *Principia*. When they called God the Great
Architect of the Universe, the most natural reading is not that they had recovered a
Hermetic doctrine but that they had read Newton and thought the universe looked
designed.

---

### The objection, and the answer

There is an obvious counter and it deserves meeting.

**Geometry is not new in 1723.** The Old Charges call Masonry a geometrical science;
the *Regius* manuscript of the 1390s makes the identification. Geometry is the
oldest continuous element in the whole tradition, predating Newton by three
centuries.

**The objection is correct about the fact and wrong about what follows.**

The medieval identification requires no philosophical explanation at all. A guild
of stonemasons calls itself a geometrical science because geometry is what
stonemasons use — setting out a foundation, cutting a voussoir, raising a vault are
geometrical operations performed by hand.

**What changes in 1723 is not the presence of geometry but what geometry is taken to
be evidence of.** For the operative craft it was a technique. For Anderson's circle
it had become the language in which the universe was written — and the difference is
visible in what they do with it. The claim that geometry underlies not merely
building but all the arts and sciences, and the elevation of a proposition of
Euclid to foundational status, are not craft statements. They are philosophical
ones, made by men who had watched Desaguliers demonstrate the solar system.

**Continuity of vocabulary is not continuity of meaning.** That a word survives from
the *Regius* manuscript tells us nothing about what the men using it in 1723 took it
to mean, and this is a model for reading a great deal of Masonic material.

### Seventy-two, and why it is not here

A useful absence, since this chapter has been about a number that is present.

**Seventy-two has enormous traditional weight.** The Shemhamphorash, the
seventy-two-fold divine name of Jewish and later occult tradition, derived from
three verses of Exodus 14. Seventy-two angels. Seventy-two conspirators against
Osiris. Seventy-two translators of the Septuagint in the traditional account.
Seventy-two goetic spirits in the *Lesser Key*.

**It is one of the numbers most heavily loaded in the Western esoteric repertoire**,
and it appears in the sources this book has surveyed constantly.

**It is not in Masonic ritual.**

Not in the catechisms, not in the exposures, not in the degrees. No seventy-two of
anything. It appears in the later high-degree systems and in the occult literature
about Masonry, both of which are nineteenth-century or later and neither of which
is Craft practice.

**Consider what that implies.** If the Craft degrees had been assembled by people
working from Kabbalistic or Hermetic materials, seventy-two is among the first
numbers one would expect to find, because it is among the most prominent in exactly
those materials.

**Its absence is a negative finding of some strength**, and it has the virtue of
being the kind of negative finding that could have come out otherwise. This is not
an argument from silence about an obscure detail; it is the absence of one of the
most conspicuous numbers in the tradition that Masonry is alleged to have inherited.

**The numbers that are present are the ones a room, a curriculum and an
architectural treatise supply.** The numbers that are absent are the ones a
Kabbalist would have brought.

That is about as clean a test of the transmission hypothesis as the material
permits, and it comes out negative.

### What a number would have to do

To close Part VIII's methodological thread before Chapter 38 states it formally.

For a number in Masonic ritual to carry a doctrine, it would have to be **arbitrary**
— it could have been otherwise — and **stable**, appearing consistently across
jurisdictions rather than varying, and **unexplained by any mundane source**.

Three fails: entailed by the requirements of a room and a sequence.
Five fails: entailed by the architectural literature.
Seven fails: entailed by a medieval curriculum.
Twelve fails: entailed by arithmetic and the moon.
Forty-seven fails: it is an index in a book.
Thirty-three fails: it is an administrative total from 1801.

**Six for six**, and the pattern is not accidental. Ritual numbers are almost always
entailed, because rituals are built out of practical arrangements — how many people
a room needs, how many stages a sequence has, what the available literature
enumerates — and practical arrangements determine their own counts.

The next chapter asks what it would take to find an exception.
