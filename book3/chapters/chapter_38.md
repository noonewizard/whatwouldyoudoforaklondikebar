# CHAPTER THIRTY-EIGHT
## Numbers That Mean

There is a point at which mathematics becomes symbolism, and the whole difficulty
of Part VIII is that nothing marks the crossing.

A number is a number. The same 7 that counts the liberal arts counts the days of
the week and the planets and the branches of a candlestick, and nothing in the
figure itself announces which occurrences are meaningful and which are coincidence.
The distinction has to be made from outside.

---

### Three ways a number can be in a system

**Counted.** The number is a tally of things that happened to be there. Three
officers because three stations. Five orders because the architectural literature
enumerates five. The number reports a quantity and carries nothing else.

**Chosen.** Somebody selected this number when others were available, and the
selection may have been for a reason. This is the only category that can carry a
doctrine.

**Found.** A reader has discovered the number in material that was not organised
around it — by counting something nobody thought to count, or by arithmetic
performed on values assigned later.

**Almost every numerological claim about Freemasonry is in the third category and is
presented as though it were in the second.**

---

### How to tell counted from chosen

Four questions, and they are Part IX's tests in miniature.

**Could it have been otherwise?** If the number follows necessarily from the
arrangement, nobody chose it. Three officers is not a choice if three stations are
required.

**Is it stable?** A chosen number should be the same everywhere. Masonic practice
varies enormously between jurisdictions, and a number that varies is not carrying a
doctrine — it is carrying local practice.

**Is there a mundane explanation on record?** Thirty-three has one: Charleston,
1801, an administrative consolidation. A documented ordinary origin is not merely
an alternative to a mystical reading; it is a decisive one, because the mystical
reading has to explain why the documented process produced the number by accident.

**And was the count specified before it was made?** This is the sharpest of the
four and it is the one that catches "found" numbers.

---

### The counting problem

Consider what is involved in discovering that a Masonic ritual contains, say,
seventy-two of something.

You have to decide what to count. Words? Steps? Officers' actions? Symbols on a
tracing board? Letters in a phrase? Each is a different count and there is no rule
selecting among them.

You have to decide what counts as one. Is a compound symbol one item or two? Is a
repeated action counted each time or once?

You have to decide where to start and stop. One degree? All three? Including the
opening and closing? Including the lecture?

**Each decision is a fork, and a determined searcher faces dozens of forks and can
take any path.** With enough forks, every target number becomes reachable.

This is the multiple-comparisons problem and it is not a subtle statistical point.
It is the plain observation that **a person who tries many things and reports the
one that worked has not found a pattern — they have selected one**, and the
selection is invisible in the write-up because failed attempts are not published.

---

### The demonstration

The preceding volume of this series ran this as an experiment rather than asserting
it, and the result is worth carrying into this book because it is the cleanest
available illustration.

Twenty words central to Masonic symbolism were fixed in advance. Twenty control
words were fixed in advance too, chosen to be as unpromising as possible —
household objects and root vegetables. The same procedure was applied to both:
simple English gematria, A=1 through Z=26.

Then two questions: how many pairs within each set share a value, and how many
words land on a conventionally significant number.

**Masonic set: three pairs and one significant hit.**
**Control set: three pairs and one significant hit.**

Identical yield. The parsnips performed exactly as well as the pillars.[^1]

Among the control results was the observation that one root vegetable summed to 93
— a number of some importance in twentieth-century Thelemic practice — which nobody
believes signifies anything about root vegetables.

**The procedure was not detecting. It was generating**, and it generates at the same
rate from any sufficiently long list of words, because twenty words mapped onto a
range of about eighty integers will produce collisions at a predictable rate and the
words' meanings make no difference to the arithmetic.

---

### Where real numerical design exists

The chapter would be dishonest if it ended there, because designed numerical
patterns are real and some of them are in plain sight.

**Architectural proportion is genuine.** Buildings are designed to ratios, the
ratios are specified in treatises, and where drawings or specifications survive we
can say so with confidence. Chapter 45 treats this.

**Liturgical and poetic numerical structure is genuine.** Acrostic psalms are built
on the alphabet; the structure is visible in the Hebrew and is not disputed.
Medieval and Renaissance poets constructed works to numerical schemes and sometimes
said so.

**And musical proportion is genuine**, since intervals are ratios and the ratios are
the physics.

**What distinguishes these from Masonic numerology is that the design is stated.**
There is a treatise, a specification, a visible acrostic, a composer's note. The
pattern was announced by the person who made it, or is structurally undeniable once
pointed out.

**The rule: a designed pattern leaves evidence of design that is independent of the
pattern itself.** Where the only evidence for design is the pattern, there is no
evidence for design.

---

### The honest position on numbers

Part VIII's conclusion, stated plainly.

**Masonic ritual uses numbers. It does not encode doctrine in them.**

Every number examined in these three chapters has a mundane source that is either
documented or obvious: practical arrangement, medieval curriculum, architectural
literature, astronomical fact, administrative consolidation, position in Euclid.

**No Masonic number survives as arbitrary**, and arbitrariness is the precondition
for a number to be carrying anything.

This is a negative finding and it is unglamorous. It is also, I think, more
respectful of the tradition than the alternative — because the alternative requires
believing that eighteenth-century English tradesmen and gentlemen were encoding a
numerical theology they never mentioned, in a system they published, using numbers
that turn out to have been determined by how many corners a room has.

**What the numbers actually show is a ritual built by practical people out of the
materials of their century**, and that is a better story than the one it replaced.

Part IX now takes the method that produced these findings and states it formally,
because it is the thing in this book most worth taking away.
