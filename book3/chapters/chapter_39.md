# PART IX — THE CIPHER PROBLEM

# CHAPTER THIRTY-NINE
## Coincidence

This is the floor of the descent. Everything above has been material; what follows
is method, and it is the part of the book most likely to be useful to someone who
never opens another word about Freemasonry.

The question is simple to state and hard to answer well. **When you find something
striking, how do you tell whether you found it or made it?**

---

### The shape of the problem

Start with the observation that makes everything else necessary.

**Meaning is not scarce.** Human language is dense with words, human culture is
dense with symbols, and human perception is extremely good at connecting things.
Given any two bodies of material and a procedure for relating them, relations will
be found.

This is not a defect in the material or in the searcher. It is arithmetic.

Consider the simplest case. Twenty words, each assigned a number by some rule, with
the numbers landing in a range of about eighty. How many pairs share a value?

Twenty items make 190 possible pairs. Spread across eighty values, the expected
number of collisions is roughly two or three — and the preceding volume's experiment
found three in the Masonic set and three in the vegetables, which is exactly what
the arithmetic predicts.[^1]

**Nothing was hidden in either list. The collisions are a property of the numbers,
not of the words.**

---

### Search space

The single most important concept in this part, and the one almost never stated in
the literature it applies to.

**The search space is the number of things you could have tried.**

A claim reports one result. What it does not report — what it usually cannot report,
because nobody kept track — is how many alternatives were available and were
rejected along the way.

Take the Atbash case from Chapter 16. The claim is that one Hebrew spelling of one
name transforms into one meaningful word. The search space is:

- **The spellings.** A foreign name transliterated into Hebrew admits many
  consonantal renderings. For *Baphomet* I enumerated **216** under reasonable
  assumptions about which letters can render which sounds and which vowel-letters
  are optional.
- **The transformations.** Atbash is one temurah scheme among several. Albam,
  Atbach and others are equally traditional.
- **The target languages.** The output was accepted as *Greek*. Hebrew, Aramaic,
  Greek and Latin were all in play.
- **The acceptance criterion.** How close does an output have to be to a real word?
  Defective spellings allowed? Vowels supplied freely?

**Multiply these and the space is not 1. It is in the thousands.**

A result found in a space of thousands, reported as though it were found in a space
of one, has been misrepresented — usually without anyone intending to misrepresent
anything, because the alternatives were never counted.

---

### Base rate

The companion concept. **How often does this happen anyway?**

For the Atbash material I computed it directly. Take random strings over the Hebrew
alphabet; apply Atbash; ask how often the output lands on a word in a target
lexicon.

For a five-letter string against the lexicon I was using, the analytic figure is
about **0.000116 per cent** — roughly one in a million — and two hundred thousand
Monte Carlo trials agreed with the calculation.[^2]

One in a million sounds decisive, and this is exactly where Chapter 16's lesson
bites. **A base rate computed against a target list that was written by someone who
knew the answer is not a base rate.** The number is correct and the framing is
contaminated.

**The base rate must be computed against a criterion fixed in advance**, and if it
is not, the calculation measures nothing except how generous the analyst was
feeling.

---

### Multiple comparisons

The third concept, and the one that turns small effects into apparent discoveries.

If you test one hypothesis at a threshold that would be met by chance one time in
twenty, you will be wrong one time in twenty. If you test a hundred hypotheses at
the same threshold, you will find about five spurious results — and if you report
only those five, your paper looks like a set of discoveries.

**This is not fraud and it usually is not even carelessness.** It is what happens
when a searcher explores material, notices what stands out, and writes about what
stood out. The failures are not concealed; they are simply not memorable.

The literature examined in this book is built almost entirely this way. A writer
examines Masonic ritual for numerical, cryptographic or symbolic patterns; most
attempts yield nothing and are forgotten; the few that yield something become a
chapter.

**The remedy is not sophistication. It is a notebook.** Record the attempts before
knowing which will succeed, and report the denominator.

---

### Over-determination

A fourth failure, subtler than the others and more seductive.

**A reading that explains everything excludes nothing.**

If a symbolic scheme can accommodate any datum — if every feature of a ritual, and
every feature it might have had instead, can be fitted into the same framework —
then the framework is not making a claim about the ritual. It is making a claim
about its own flexibility.

The Tree of Life is the most productive instrument of this kind in the Western
esoteric repertoire, as Chapter 32 observed: ten positions, twenty-two paths, a
tradition of applying it to anything with roughly that many parts. Mappings can be
constructed for Masonic ritual, for a cathedral floor plan, for a novel's structure.
That mappings *can* be constructed is a fact about the diagram.

**The diagnostic question: what would this framework have been unable to
accommodate?** If the answer is nothing, the framework has no discriminating power,
however satisfying its applications feel.

---

### Apophenia, without contempt

The cognitive side, and the chapter's most important paragraph.

The tendency to perceive structure in noise has a name — **apophenia** — and it is
not a pathology. It is the ordinary operation of a perceptual system shaped by an
environment in which missing a real pattern was far more expensive than seeing a
false one. A rustle that is nothing costs a startle. A rustle that is something and
is missed costs everything.

**We are machines for detecting structure, and we detect it whether or not it is
there.**

So the person who finds a hidden geometry in a floor plan, or a name in a set of
initials, or a pentagram in a street grid, is not stupid and is usually not
dishonest. They are doing what human perception does, enthusiastically, on material
that rewards it.

I felt the pull myself when the Atbash transformation came out. I had written the
controls into the exercise in advance specifically because I knew I would, and it
still took three attempts to see what was wrong.

**The tests in this part are not a way of calling such people fools. They are the
only method anyone has — including the person doing the discovering — for telling the
two cases apart.**

---

### And the opposite danger

Because a chapter that only warns against finding patterns has taught half a lesson.

**Some patterns are designed.** Anderson really did put Euclid's forty-seventh
proposition at the foundation of Masonry in 1723. Masons really did carve pigpen
inscriptions on gravestones. Dee and Kelley really did construct Enochian. Alberti
really did invent polyalphabetic cipher.

In every one of those cases the design is demonstrable, and the next chapter is
about what demonstrability consists of.

**The task is not to reject patterns. It is to determine whether intentionality can
be shown** — and that is a different question from whether the pattern is there,
which is why the two chapters that follow separate them.
