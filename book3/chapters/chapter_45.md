# CHAPTER FORTY-FIVE
## Geometry Without Words

There is a real subject here and a large literature of imposture on top of it, and
separating them requires the apparatus of Part IX applied to material considerably
more respectable than the online decoders.

---

### What is genuinely true

**Buildings are designed to proportions, and the proportions are specified in
writing.**

This is not in dispute and it is not esoteric. It is the ordinary content of
architectural theory from antiquity onward.

**Vitruvius**, writing in the first century BCE, sets out the principles: symmetry,
proportion, the derivation of a building's measures from a module, and the analogy
between the proportions of a well-formed building and those of a well-formed human
body. That last passage produced the Vitruvian figure, drawn many times in the
Renaissance and most famously by Leonardo.[^1]

**Alberti**, in *De re aedificatoria* (1452), develops a theory of proportion
explicitly grounded in musical ratios — the intervals that please the ear expressed
as small whole-number relations, applied to the dimensions of rooms.

**Palladio** works to systematic proportional schemes and publishes them, with
dimensions, in the *Quattro Libri* of 1570.

**In every case the design is documented by the designer.** We know these buildings
are proportioned because the architects wrote treatises saying how and why. There
is no decipherment involved. There is reading.

**This is the gold standard from Chapter 41 applied to buildings.** The rule exists
independently of the claim, was fixed before construction, and there is direct
evidence of intention — a book, with the author's name on it.

### Pythagoras and the ratios

The underlying idea is older and it has one genuinely remarkable component.

The Pythagorean discovery that musical consonance corresponds to simple numerical
ratios — that halving a string produces the octave, that 3:2 gives the fifth and 4:3
the fourth — is **real, checkable, and was properly astonishing.**

It is arguably the first case in European thought of a qualitative experience
turning out to have an exact mathematical structure. Anyone can verify it on a
stretched string in an afternoon, and the verification is why the discovery carried
the weight it did.

**From that, an enormous inference**: that number underlies the sensible world
generally, that harmony is a cosmic principle, and that a building proportioned to
the musical ratios participates in the same order.

**The discovery is solid. The inference is a philosophical commitment**, and the
two should not be run together. That intervals are ratios is physics. That the
cosmos is therefore harmonically ordered is a further claim requiring further
argument, and the Pythagoreans supplied the argument rather than finding it in the
string.

### Where it goes wrong

Now the modern literature, and the failure has the exact shape of Chapter 43's.

The procedure for discovering sacred geometry in a building runs like this. Obtain a
plan. Identify significant points. Draw lines between some of them. Observe that
certain lines meet at angles close to some canonical value, or that certain
distances approximate the golden ratio, or that a figure can be fitted so its
vertices fall near some of the points.

**Every step is a decision and none is constrained.**

**Which points count?** A medieval church offers hundreds — every pier, respond,
boss, bay corner, window center. The number of candidate points is large and the
selection is made by the analyst.

**How close is close enough?** The golden ratio is 1.618. Is 1.61 a hit? 1.65? 1.58?
The literature almost never states a tolerance, and an unstated tolerance is set by
whether the answer came out.

**How many attempts?** A figure can be scaled freely, rotated through 360 degrees,
and placed anywhere. The positions available are effectively unlimited.

**And the golden ratio specifically is a menace**, because 1.618 is close to a great
many ratios that arise for other reasons: 8:5 is 1.6, 13:8 is 1.625, and any
building using small whole-number proportions will produce approximations
constantly. A tolerance of a few per cent catches most of them.[^2]

**The control**, from the previous chapter: four hundred thousand random placements
of a pentagram on a plain jittered lattice produced a fit with maximum vertex
deviation of 5.7 per cent of the circumradius. **On a drawing, that is a
pentagram.** The lattice contains nothing.

### The test, stated for buildings

**A building's proportions are demonstrated when the design is documented
independently of the measurement.**

Palladio passes: there is a book. Vitruvius passes. A Gothic cathedral for which
the master mason's setting-out method is known from a lodge book or a drawing
passes — and some are known, which is why the historical study of medieval
geometric practice is a real discipline with real results.

**Retrospective fitting fails**, always, because it cannot distinguish a building
with designed proportions from one without. It returns a positive either way.

This is not a claim that no building has sacred geometry. Many do. **It is a claim
about a method** — that the method of measuring afterward and finding ratios cannot
tell us which, and that only documentary evidence of the design process can.

### Masonic geometry, specifically

And the Craft's own use, which is more modest than either its admirers or its
critics assume.

**Masonic ritual contains no geometric construction.** No compass-and-straightedge
work is performed. No proportions are specified. No measurements are given, except
in the borrowed biblical dimensions of the Temple.

What it contains is **geometry as a subject of praise** — the claim, from the Old
Charges onward, that geometry is the foundation of the craft and, by Anderson's
1723 extension, of the arts and sciences generally.

**That is a philosophical position, not a practice.** Chapter 37 argued that its
eighteenth-century form is Newtonian rather than Hermetic: men who had watched
Desaguliers demonstrate the solar system, asserting that the universe is a
geometrical object.

The working tools are instruments of *testing* — square, level, plumb — and their
moral applications in the ritual are applications of testing: is this true, is this
level, is this upright. **They are used to check, not to construct.**

**A tradition that praised geometry and performed none is exactly what the evidence
shows**, and it is consistent with everything else in this book about the Craft's
relation to technique.

### What the medieval masons actually did

A section in fairness to the operative craft, because the deflationary argument
above can be heard as a claim that medieval builders had no geometry, and that is
badly wrong.

**They had a great deal, and it was practical.**

A mason setting out a foundation, cutting a voussoir for an arch, or raising a
vault is performing geometrical operations. The methods were transmitted by
apprenticeship and by drawing, and where lodge books and setting-out drawings
survive we can see them: constructions with compasses and straightedge, techniques
for deriving one dimension from another, methods for generating a complex shape from
a simple one.

**The technique of *ad quadratum* and *ad triangulum* construction is real** —
deriving a building's proportions by rotating a square within a square, or by
equilateral triangles — and it is documented in the surviving material. The
argument at Milan cathedral in the 1390s, in which the masons disputed which system
to use and the proceedings were recorded, is the best-known instance.

**This is genuine historical sacred geometry and it has the feature Chapter 41
requires: the design is documented independently of the measurement.** We know
because the masons said so, in writing, while arguing.

**And note what it is not.** It is a set of *construction techniques* — ways of
getting a building to come out right with a rope, a square and a pair of compasses.
It is not a system of numerical symbolism, and the surviving material does not
suggest the masons thought their ratios encoded doctrine. They thought they were
building something that would stand up and look right.

**The distance between that and modern sacred-geometry literature is the distance
between a craft and a cosmology**, and the literature's habit of citing medieval
practice in support of numerical mysticism reverses what the practice actually
was.

Which is, incidentally, the same reversal Chapter 37 identified in the Masonic case:
**geometry as technique, later read as geometry as revelation.**

---

### The three geometries

To close the chapter with the distinction the brief requires.

**Historical sacred geometry** is a real subject: the documented use of proportional
systems in religious architecture, studied through treatises, drawings and lodge
books. It has a literature and it has findings.

**Masonic geometry** is a rhetorical and moral usage: geometry praised as
foundational, its instruments moralized, no construction performed.

**Modern New Age sacred geometry** is a retrospective-fitting practice applied to
whatever is to hand, without stated tolerances or controls, and it has the epistemic
status Chapter 43 assigned to its city-plan equivalent.

**These are three different things.** The first is scholarship, the second is
history, and the third is a hobby — and merging them, which happens constantly, gives
the third the credibility of the first.
