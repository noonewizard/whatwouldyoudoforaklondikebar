# CHAPTER FORTY-SIX
## Orientation

A lodge is oriented. There is an east, and things come from it, and the principal
officer sits there.

This is the most heavily interpreted spatial feature in Masonic practice and the
one where the half-of-everything rule does most of its work.

---

### The claim

The standard esoteric reading: Masonic orientation preserves an ancient
solar-religious practice. Temples across the ancient world were oriented; the sun
rises in the east; light comes from the east; the Master sits in the east as the
sun does. Therefore Masonry inherits a solar cult.

**The observations are accurate.** Ancient temples frequently were oriented.
Christian churches conventionally face east. The sun does rise there. The Master
does sit there, and Masonic sources do explain the arrangement by reference to the
sun.

**The inference is what fails**, and it fails on cardinality.

---

### Counting the alternatives

Chapter 42's Test 6 asks for the base rate, and here the base rate is trivially
computable.

**How many directions could a tradition orient on?** Four cardinal directions, or
eight if intercardinals are allowed.

**How many are attractive?** East and west, because the sun does something visible
there. North is attractive in high latitudes because of the pole star. South is
attractive in some traditions for other reasons.

**So the realistic field is two, or at most four.**

Two traditions agreeing on a two-way feature agree by chance half the time. Finding
that Masonry and Egyptian temple practice both orient on east is not finding
anything: half of everything goes one way.

And there is a further point. **East is not an arbitrary choice.** It is where the
sun rises, and a tradition that wants a direction associated with beginnings,
light, and arrival will pick it *for reasons available to anybody*. Nobody needs to
be taught this. Any group of people in any century, asked to nominate a direction
for light to come from, will nominate east, because that is where light comes
from.

**A choice that everyone would make independently carries no information about who
influenced whom.** This is the entailed-versus-arbitrary distinction from Chapter
38 applied to space, and it disposes of the solar-cult inference completely.

---

### The east that is not east

And now the detail that ends the argument, which the preceding volume identified and
which deserves repeating because it is decisive.

**The Master's station in a lodge is not necessarily in the geographical east.**

Lodges meet in rooms. Rooms have whatever orientation the building has. A lodge
room over a public house in a street running north to south will have its "east"
wherever the furniture goes — and the Craft has always known this and has never
regarded it as a problem.

The east of a lodge is **a ritual designation, not a compass bearing.** The station
is called the east; the Master occupies it; light is said to come from it. Where
the sun actually rises has no bearing on any of this.

**A tradition preserving a solar cult would care about the sun.** This one does not.
It uses solar language to explain a seating arrangement whose actual position is
determined by the shape of the room.

That is the difference between a symbol and a survival, and it is visible in the
furniture.

---

### What orientation actually does

Set the inheritance question aside and the practical function is clear enough, and
it is Chapter 44's argument applied.

**It creates a reference frame.** Once a direction is designated, every position in
the room is defined relative to it. The Senior Warden is in the west, the Junior in
the south, the door in a fixed relation. The room becomes a coordinate system, and
coordinate systems make instructions possible: a candidate can be conducted, and
the conducting can be prescribed identically in every lodge in the world regardless
of the building.

**This is what makes Masonic ritual portable**, and portability is the Craft's most
underrated technical achievement. A ritual keyed to the actual east would require
a building or a survey. A ritual keyed to a designated east requires a room and an
agreement, and can therefore be performed in a tavern, a tent, a ship, or a prison
camp — all of which have happened.

**The designation is the technology.** Smith's emplacement thesis again: the
sacrality, and here the geometry, is conferred by an act rather than found in the
site.

---

### The circuit

One more spatial feature, and it is the most-claimed.

The candidate is conducted around the lodge, and in most workings the movement is
clockwise — following the apparent course of the sun.

A large literature treats this as solar-cult survival. Apply the tests.

**Chronology** permits it trivially: sun-following ritual movement is older than
everything. **Transmission**: no route has been produced. **Intertextuality**:
Masonic sources explain the direction by the sun as a natural emblem of
regularity — a stated and sufficient explanation requiring no ancient cult.
**Institutional continuity**: none.

**And the parallel is worthless as evidence regardless**, because clockwise
circumambulation is close to universal wherever there is circular ritual movement at
all. **There are two directions. Half of everything goes one way.**

What the sources support is duller and more useful: the lodge is modeled on a
building oriented east to west, the sun's course supplies the natural direction of
travel in such a building, and a Craft busy identifying itself with order and
regularity found in that a congenial emblem.

---

### The one spatial feature that is arbitrary

The argument of this chapter has been that Masonic spatial features are entailed
rather than chosen, and a chapter making that argument should look for the
exception rather than waiting to be shown one.

There is one, and it is worth examining because of how it behaves.

**The three principal officers are placed at east, west and south — and not north.**

The north is explicitly treated as a place of darkness in most workings, and it is
left without a principal station. Now, is that entailed?

Partly. A room needs stations distributed around it, and three stations in a
rectangular space will naturally take positions that are not adjacent. But the
specific exclusion of the north, and its characterization as dark, is not forced by
anything about rooms.

**The explanation offered in the sources is solar**: the sun never reaches the
north, in northern latitudes, and a building oriented east-west has a north side
that receives no direct light.

**And that explanation is correct and is a fact about latitude.** In the northern
hemisphere the north face of a building is the one that stays in shadow. Anyone who
has lived in a house knows which rooms are cold.

Which makes it entailed after all — by the sun's actual behavior at the latitudes
where the ritual was composed.

**And it generates a testable prediction**, which is the useful part. If the
north-as-darkness feature is a fact about latitude rather than a transmitted
doctrine, then it should sit awkwardly in the southern hemisphere, where the
relation reverses.

**It does, and Masonic practice has simply carried it over unchanged.** Australian
and South African lodges retain the northern symbolism in a hemisphere where the
sun does not behave that way.

This is exactly what a transmitted convention looks like when it outruns the
conditions that produced it — and exactly what a live cosmological doctrine would
not look like, since a tradition that genuinely held the north to be dark because of
the sun would have noticed. **The symbolism was inherited as furniture, not as
astronomy.**

---

### The rule, stated for use

Part X's methodological contribution, and it generalizes well beyond this subject.

**Before being impressed by a parallel, count the alternatives.**

Where a feature has few possible forms, a match between unconnected traditions
carries almost no information. Orientation: four options, realistically two.
Circumambulation: two. Night or day: two. Blindfold or not: two.

Four such features matching looks overwhelming and has a chance probability of
about one in sixteen — which, set against the hundreds of initiatory traditions
available for comparison and the analyst's freedom to choose which features to
count, is not a finding at all.

**What would carry information is a match on something arbitrary**: not the
direction of movement but a specific number of circuits with a specific challenge
at a specific point, in idiosyncratic wording, where dozens of alternatives existed
and no functional reason selects this one.

That is the evidence of descent textual critics use. It is what this series has
looked for across three volumes, in ritual, in symbolism and in language.

It has not been found.
