# CHAPTER FORTY-EIGHT
## Remembering

A man who has not attended a lodge for fifteen years can still be examined, and
will pass.

This is not a claim about the power of ritual. It is an ordinary institutional
fact, documented by the practice of examining visitors, and it tells us something
precise about what kind of storage Masonic ritual uses.

---

### Why the ritual is memorable

Four properties, and none of them is mysterious. All four are things the
memory literature would predict.

**It is procedural rather than declarative.** The candidate does not learn *about* a
sequence; he performs one. Procedural memory — the knowing-how that underlies
riding a bicycle or playing an instrument — is stored differently from factual
recall and is markedly more durable. It degrades slowly and survives disuse.

**It is spatial.** Everything happens somewhere: the door, the three stations, the
circuit, the altar. Human memory for space and for routes through space is
unusually good, and the classical art of memory exploited exactly this by
attaching things to be remembered to imagined rooms.[^1]

**It is rhythmic and dialogic.** A catechism has a shape — question and answer,
with expected stress and cadence. Verse is easier to memorize than prose for the
same reason, and the effect is substantial. An examiner who knows the rhythm hears
a wrong answer as a stumble before he hears it as an error.

**And it is emotionally marked.** The third degree in particular is arranged to be
affecting, and material encoded under arousal is retained better than material
encoded flat.

**Four channels, all reinforcing.** A tradition that wanted its material to survive
without books could hardly do better, and a tradition that was not trying to would
still get this result from doing ceremony well.

---

### Word and body

The brief for this book asks for an analysis of the relationship among word,
gesture, token, sign, grip, movement and space, and this is where it belongs.

Consider what accompanies a Masonic word in actual use.

It is **spoken** in a particular manner. It is accompanied by a **grip** — a physical
act performed with another person. It is associated with a **sign**, a gesture. It
belongs to a **position** in the room and to a **moment** in a sequence. It was first
received in a state of **restricted vision**, after a **movement** through space.

**The word is the smallest component of a multi-channel event.**

And this has a consequence that is directly relevant to everything Part V argued.

**A word extracted from that event is not the same object.** Printed in an exposure,
it is a string. In use, it is the verbal component of something that also involves
a hand, a posture, a place, a person opposite, and a history of having been received
under particular conditions.

This is not mysticism. It is the plain observation that context is part of what is
stored. Ask someone to recall a phone number and they may reach for a keypad with
their fingers. Ask a musician for a passage and they will often have to play it.
**The memory is in the doing, and the doing includes the body.**

---

### Which is why publication failed

The whole of Part V's finding, restated in the vocabulary of memory.

**The exposures printed the verbal channel and could not print the others.**

They gave the words. They *described* the grips and signs in prose, which is not the
same as conveying them — Chapter 20 made this point and it is worth the repetition,
because it is the mechanism by which the institution survived. A grip must be shown,
by a person, to a person, in the same room. A description enables a reader to know
that a grip exists and roughly what it involves. It does not enable him to perform
one convincingly in front of a man who has done it a thousand times.

**The physical channels are tacit knowledge in Polanyi's exact sense** — learnable in
ten seconds by demonstration, and not statable.

Three centuries of printed exposures have never transmitted one.

---

### Ritual as a memory technology

The wider claim, stated at the strength the evidence supports.

Initiatory systems that transmit without books face a problem: how do you preserve a
body of material across generations, accurately, in a population that may be
partially literate, without a text that can be corrupted by copying?

**Encoding it in a multi-channel performance is a good solution**, and it has been
independently arrived at many times, which is what Chapter 46's rule would predict
of a good solution to a recurring problem.

The material is distributed across procedure, space, rhythm and body. It is
rehearsed continuously, because every meeting performs it. It is checked constantly,
because errors are audible to everyone present. And it is held by many people at
once, so that no single loss destroys it.

**Compare a manuscript**, which can burn, be miscopied, or be read by the wrong
person.

**But note the limit**, because this argument is regularly overextended. What such a
system preserves well is *performance*: sequences, words, gestures, order. What it
preserves poorly is *interpretation* — and Masonic practice demonstrates this
comprehensively. The ceremonies have transmitted with remarkable fidelity across
three centuries and six continents. The explanations have not converged at all, and
Chapter 21 argued that they cannot, because nothing exists that could adjudicate.

**Ritual is excellent at transmitting what to do and hopeless at transmitting what
it means.**

Which is a strong argument against the whole family of claims this book has been
examining. A tradition allegedly carrying an ancient doctrine in its ritual would
be using the one channel that does not carry doctrine.

---

### Why interpretation does not transmit

The asymmetry stated above — ritual preserves performance well and interpretation
badly — deserves more than an assertion, because it carries a good deal of weight in
this book's argument.

**The evidence is Masonic practice itself, and it is unusually clean.**

The ceremonies have transmitted with remarkable fidelity. A visitor from an English
lodge can follow an Australian one; the structure, the sequence, the officers, the
essential actions are recognizable across three centuries and six continents. Where
variation exists it is local and traceable, and the jurisdictions are aware of their
differences.

**The interpretations have not converged at all.** Asked what the Lost Word
signifies, Masonic commentary has proposed truth, the divine name, moral character,
lost innocence, the knowledge of God, consciousness, identity, the self before the
Fall, and the secret of Masonry itself — and every one of these readings remains in
circulation, because nothing exists that could retire one.

**Same tradition. Same transmission channel. Opposite outcomes.**

The explanation is structural rather than mysterious. **A performance can be checked
in real time.** If an officer says the wrong word or moves at the wrong moment,
everyone present notices and someone corrects him. Error is detected and repaired at
every meeting, which is a remarkably effective error-correcting mechanism.

**An interpretation cannot be checked at all**, because the Craft has no authority
competent to rule on one. Anderson's 1723 settlement removed doctrinal authority in
order to admit men of differing religions, and the consequence — very likely
unintended — is that no Masonic body can say that a reading is wrong.

So errors in performance are corrected and variations in interpretation accumulate.
Over three centuries the first converges and the second explodes.

**And this is a strong argument against a claim this book has been examining
throughout.** A tradition allegedly carrying an ancient doctrine in its ritual would
be using the one channel that demonstrably does not carry doctrine. What ritual
transmits is what to do.

---

### What the career does

A final observation, carried from the preceding volume because Part XI needs it.

The account above describes what a candidate receives. It understates by a large
margin what a long-serving member accumulates.

**A man who does not lapse undergoes the ceremony once and then performs or witnesses
it perhaps a hundred times**, from every position in the room — as the one holding
the arm, the one asking the question, eventually the one in the east. He learns the
whole thing by heart because he must deliver it. He is corrected when he errs.

**What he ends up holding is not a memory. It is a competence**, of the same kind as
a musician's, and it is maintained the way competences are maintained: by use.

That is why he can still be examined after fifteen years. Not because the ceremony
was powerful, but because he did it several hundred times and the body does not
easily forget what it has done several hundred times.
