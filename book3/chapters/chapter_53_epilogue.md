# EPILOGUE
## What Was Lost?

Three books, and the same answer keeps arriving from different directions.

Book I asked where Masonic symbolism came from and found that the Craft did not
inherit the Western magical tradition but built the vehicle that carried it. Book
II asked what the ritual does to the people who undergo it and found techniques
independently arrived at rather than transmitted. This book asked what a word is,
that losing one could matter, and found that words are not the sort of thing that
can be lost.

**The pattern in all three is the same: convergence rather than descent, and
structures that work without anyone having designed them to.**

I did not expect that when I began, and I have come to think it is the most
interesting thing the series has to report.

---

### The answer, assembled

What was lost?

**Not a word.** There is no document in which anyone had it. The loss belongs to a
legend composed in the 1720s from biblical names and ordinary narrative materials,
and the substitution follows from the plot before it follows from anything else.

**Not a pronunciation**, though that is what went in the one historical case this
book examined closely. The Tetragrammaton's letters have been legible for three
thousand years, and the vowels went because a practice stopped and a consonantal
script does not record sound. That is the ordinary fate of an unspoken word.

**Not a doctrine.** Nothing Kabbalistic, Hermetic, Egyptian or Templar entered the
Craft degrees. The occult readings are nineteenth-century, they are datable, and
they arrived through a chain — Lévi 1854, Pike 1871 — whose every link is
documented.

**Not a cipher.** The substituted word varies between jurisdictions and has changed
over time, and a token its own transmitters do not transmit stably is not carrying
encoded information.

**What was lost, in every case this book examined, was the conditions under which a
word means anything.** The authority to speak it. The setting where speaking counts.
The condition of the person who would speak. The High Priest had four things and
kept only the letters. The Masonic candidate is given everything except the letters
and told so to his face.

Neither can perform the act. That symmetry is the finding, and nobody arranged it.

---

### What the method is worth

The part of this book most likely to outlive it has nothing to do with
Freemasonry.

**Ten tests**, in Chapter 42 and Appendix I, for any claim that something is hidden
in a text, a name, a number or a building. Provenance, dating, rule, precommitment,
controls, base rate, replication, plausibility, alternatives, intention.

They are not sophisticated. State the procedure before you run it. Count the
attempts, including the ones you abandoned. Build the control before you know what
the real material will yield. Say what would change your mind.

**And notice who wrote the answer key.**

That last one cost me three rounds of computation. I ran a control on the most
famous Atbash claim in the literature, and it passed. I ran a better control, and it
passed again. It was only on the third attempt that I asked where my list of
acceptable answers had come from, and found that I had written it, knowing the
result, with a Greek word on it that no Hebrew lexicon contains.

Remove that one entry and the claim returns nothing, exactly like every control.

**The transformation was real the whole time. What was worthless was my criterion**,
and the flaw was not in the arithmetic or the controls but in a list that looked
like part of the apparatus and was actually part of the answer.

I have printed all three attempts in the order they happened, including the wrong
one, because the sequence teaches more than the conclusion.

### The mystery that is left

The brief for this book asked that the reader finish with the mystery larger and
the evidence clearer, and those are not in tension.

**What the research dissolves is the manufactured mystery.** The cipher that was
never a cipher. The ancient transmission with no carrier. The decipherment whose
success criterion was written afterward. The sacred geometry found by a method
that finds it in a car park. The numbers that turn out to be a page reference and an
administrative total.

That material is gone, and it should be. It was never load-bearing, and the people
who built this tradition would not have recognized it.

**What is left standing is harder and older.**

A group of men assembled a ceremony in the 1720s, under ordinary institutional
pressure, from materials to hand. They needed a third degree. They made one that
takes something away rather than giving something, and they told the candidate the
truth about what he was receiving — that it was a substitute, that the thing itself
was unavailable, and that nobody had it.

Then they kept it that way. Through schism and union, across six continents, in
jurisdictions that agree about almost nothing else, changing nearly everything —
and not this.

**Whether anyone understood what they had built is doubtful, and it does not much
matter, because it has been working on people for three hundred years.**

---

### The question

So the book ends where the brief said it should, on the question it cannot answer
and thinks worth asking.

If the Word stands for truth — and every commentator who ever wrote about it has
read it that way, and the reading is as good as any other — then the ceremony is
telling a man that truth is not the sort of thing that can be handed over.

He can be given the string. He can be given standing, and company, and a room where
the words count, and an obligation that will hold him. All of that is real and the
institution confers it.

**What he cannot be given is the thing the word points at**, because the pointing is
all a word ever does, and the rest is on him.

Which raises the question this book has been walking toward for fifty-three
chapters and will not presume to settle:

**If the Word represents truth, what would it mean to recover truth, rather than
merely recovering a word?**

I do not know. Nothing in the evidence answers it, and I have tried to be careful
throughout about the difference between what the evidence shows and what I would
like it to show.

What I can report is that a tradition built a ceremony around that question in a
London tavern three hundred years ago, almost certainly by accident, and then
declined every subsequent opportunity to answer it.

The door was open the whole time. The room was ordinary. The word was never lost.

And something in there has held, for three centuries, for reasons that have very
little to do with secrets and rather a lot to do with what it is like to be handed
something and told, honestly, that it is not the thing you came for.
