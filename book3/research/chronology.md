Three columns, because the argument of Book III depends on their non-alignment:
**sacred-language traditions**, **cryptography**, and **Masonic ritual**.

Reading them together shows that the techniques most often proposed as sources for
Masonic word-practice were developed in traditions the Craft can be shown to have
had no contact with, and that the Masonic material appears late in both other
columns.

## WHAT THE COLUMNS SHOW

**1. The Masonic word-practice is old; the Lost Word is not.** A word, a grip and a
catechism are in place by 1696. The *loss* of a word — the entire dramatic
structure on which every esoteric reading depends — is not attested before the
1720s. Any account deriving the Lost Word from Hebrew name-traditions must explain
a gap of roughly sixteen centuries with no documented carrier.

**2. Atbash is the oldest item in the cryptography column and has nothing to do
with the third.** It is attested c. 600 BCE, in Hebrew, in scripture, applied to
proper nouns, as a literary device. Its next appearance in this chronology is 1984,
applied to a word the Templars are alleged to have used, by a modern author. There
is no intervening Masonic use.

**3. The cryptographic sophistication available to the eighteenth century far
exceeded what Masonry used.** Alberti (1467) and Vigenère (1586) were in print, and
polyalphabetic ciphers were known to any educated person with an interest. Masonry
adopted an unkeyed monoalphabetic grid — a system already two centuries obsolete
for concealment. **A body with something to hide had better options and did not take
them.**

**4. Kabbalah was available in Latin from 1677** — which is the one genuine
opportunity for transmission into an English-speaking fraternity, and the point at
which the §VIII protocol must be applied most carefully. Availability is not
transmission.

**5. Every Hermetic-Qabalistic reading of the Masonic word postdates 1854.** The
vocabulary in which the Lost Word is usually explained did not exist when the Lost
Word was invented.
