# COMPARATIVE INITIATION AND SACRED-WORD ANALYSIS

Per §17 of the dossier. Carried forward from Book II under two standing rules.

**Parallel is not descent.** Structural resemblance is not evidence of derivation
absent a documented transmission — a person, a text, a date, a verbal dependence.

**The half-of-everything rule** (Book II, ch 8). Where a feature has few possible
forms, a match carries almost no information. Before being impressed by a parallel,
count the alternatives.

## WHAT THE TABLE SHOWS

**1. Masonry's structure is unusual, and the unusual feature is the refusal.**
Most traditions either keep a word (Judaism, the mysteries) or deploy it (magic,
Sufism). Very few *stage its loss and decline to restore it.* The Royal Arch is
the exception within Masonry itself, and Book II argued that its existence shows
the deferral was felt as an incompleteness by the people inside it.

**2. The Eleusinian comparison is the one most often made and the least usable.**
The secret was kept. There is no participant's account of what was said in the
Telesterion. **Every reconstruction of the *legomena* is extrapolation**, and a
parallel drawn to an unknown is not a parallel.

**3. The Jewish case runs the opposite way to the Masonic one.** The Name is not
lost — the consonants have been legible for three millennia. What lapsed is a
pronunciation and, more importantly, the institutional setting in which
pronouncing it did anything. **Masonry dramatises a loss; Judaism practises a
restraint.** These are different operations and the literature routinely equates
them.

**4. Where a genuine transmission of sacred names *can* be documented, it looks
nothing like the Masonic claims.** Hebrew divine names appear throughout the Greek
Magical Papyri, in the mouths of non-Jewish practitioners. We can see the
borrowing: texts, dates, geography, verbal dependence. **That is what transmission
leaves behind**, and its absence in the Masonic case is informative.

---

## THE ANALYTIC RESULT

Applying the rules: the Masonic word-practice shares with other traditions only
**entailed** features — those any solution to the problem would have. A recognition
system needs something shared, something restricted, and some means of testing.
Every such system in history has those three, because the problem demands them.

What it does **not** share is arbitrary detail: no distinctive vocabulary crossing
between traditions, no peculiar sequence, no idiosyncratic error, none of the
fingerprints by which textual descent is established.

**Verdict: convergence, not descent — consistent with Books I and II.**
