# HISTORIOGRAPHY AND MAJOR SCHOLARLY DISPUTES

Per §§2, 20 of the dossier. Twelve live disputes. **No scholar is treated as
infallible (§XXXIV).** Where the field has not settled, the book reports the
disagreement rather than choosing a side and presenting it as consensus.

---

## DISPUTES CARRIED FROM BOOKS I AND II

**1. The date of the first Grand Lodge.** 1717 (Anderson 1738) versus 1721
(Prescott and Sommers). Bears on Book III only as a reminder that the tradition's
own foundational date rests on a single retrospective source. **[SVR]**

**2. Stevenson's Scottish thesis.** *The Origins of Freemasonry* (1988) relocated
the question to the Scottish lodges of 1598–1710. Contested in details, decisive in
method. Book III depends on it for the Mason Word's pre-1700 existence.

**3. The emergence of the third degree.** Secure to the 1720s. **The implication
that every esoteric reading of the Hiramic legend postdates the earliest lodge
minutes by a century is not generally drawn**, and Book III draws it.

**4. The origin of the Royal Arch.** Genuinely contested. The one place a pre-Lévi
Hebraic route is arguable. See `research/kabbalah.md`, Claim B.

## DISPUTES SPECIFIC TO BOOK III

**5. The date of *Sefer Yetzirah*.** Proposals span the 2nd to the 9th century —
seven hundred years, and the stakes are large: an early date would place
letter-cosmology before the rabbinic mainstream, a late one places it after Islamic
philosophical contact. **Hayman's critical edition (2004) is the necessary starting
point**, and it complicates the question rather than resolving it. **The book states
the range and does not pick.**

**6. Scholem versus Idel.** Scholem's synthesis (1941 onward) made the field and
treated theosophical Kabbalah as primary, with Abulafia's ecstatic/linguistic
strand as a byway. Idel (1988 onward) argues the linguistic strand is central and
older. **Book III's Part VII depends on Idel being substantially right**, since its
subject is language, and the book says so in text rather than assuming it.

**7. Memra and the Johannine Logos.** Older scholarship treated the Targumic Memra
as a genuine hypostasis and a direct antecedent of John 1. Much recent work treats
it as a translational convention with no independent standing. **Attribute both;
decide neither.**

**8. When the pronunciation of the Tetragrammaton ceased.** Traditionally located
at the destruction of the Temple (70 CE). The evidence for a gradual Second Temple
restriction is substantial, and the question of what "ceased" means — public use,
priestly use, all use — is not settled. **Rösel (2000) and McDonough (1999) on
opposite emphases.**

**9. The authorship and date of the *Zohar*.** Scholem's attribution to Moses de
León (c. 1280s) is broadly accepted; Liebes and others argue for a circle rather
than an author. Bears on any claim about "ancient" Kabbalah reaching the
eighteenth century.

**10. Yates's Hermetic thesis.** *Giordano Bruno* (1964) transformed the field and
is no longer accepted as she stated it. **Read for the questions, not the answers** —
and note that a great deal of popular Masonic esotericism rests on the version of
Yates that scholarship has since abandoned.

**11. The origins of the grid ("pigpen") cipher.** Claimed antecedents reaching to
the Templars or earlier are weak. Firm documentation is 18th–19th century. **[SVR]
— and the book must cite objects, not assertions.**

**12. Whether "magic" is a usable analytic category at all.** Tambiah, Graf and
Hanegraaff have each argued that the term imports more than it describes. Book II
resolved this by classifying five senses and never exchanging them; Book III
inherits that apparatus.

## THE RULE OF DIRECTION, RESTATED

Carried from Book II, because it disposes of a large fraction of the literature at
a stroke and the reason it is legitimate bears repeating.

**A source is primary for its own period and secondary — often unreliably — for any
earlier one.**

Pike on nineteenth-century American Masonry is a witness. Pike on Egypt is a reader
of the same documents available to us, with fewer of them and no critical
apparatus. His conclusions deserve consideration; his authority does not transfer.

The same applies to Lévi on ancient magic, Mathers on Kabbalah (he translated
Knorr's Latin, not the Aramaic), Leadbeater on the eighteenth century, and Mackey
and Oliver on anything before their own lifetimes.
