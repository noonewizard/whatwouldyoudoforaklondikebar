# THE LOGOS — RESEARCH MAP

Per §§VII, XXI. **Four distinct concepts carried by one Greek word**, routinely
merged into a single "Logos tradition" that never existed as such.

| | Sense | Period | Key texts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Heraclitean** — the rational principle ordering the cosmos; the account that things obey | c. 500 BCE | Fragments (DK B1, B2, B50) |
| **2** | **Stoic** — *logos spermatikos*; divine reason immanent in matter, identified with fate and with God | c. 300 BCE– | Chrysippus (frr.); Cicero, *De natura deorum*; Marcus Aurelius |
| **3** | **Philonic** — a mediating principle between the transcendent God and creation; God's instrument, variously "firstborn son," "image," "high priest" | c. 20 BCE–50 CE | *De opificio mundi*; *Legum allegoriae*; *De confusione linguarum* |
| **4** | **Johannine** — the Logos is a person, is God, and becomes flesh | c. 90–110 CE | John 1:1–18 |

**The move that makes the fourth unlike the other three: incarnation.** Heraclitus's
logos is a principle, the Stoics' is immanent reason, Philo's is a mediator —
none of them becomes a particular human being at a particular date. John 1:14 is a
discontinuity, not a development, and the patristic tradition spent four centuries
working out what it had committed to.

## THE HEBREW SIDE

Parallel and distinct. **The Johannine prologue's structure owes at least as much
to Jewish Wisdom literature as to Greek philosophy**, and the honest position is
that the sources are intertwined rather than separable.

- **Divine speech as creative:** Gen 1 ("and God said"); Ps 33:6 ("by the word of
  the LORD were the heavens made").
- **Wisdom personified:** Prov 8:22–31 — present at creation, "brought forth"
  before the world. The nearest scriptural antecedent to a mediating figure.
- **Memra** in the Targums — "the Word of the LORD" as a reverential circumlocution.
  Its relation to the Johannine Logos is **disputed**, and the book will not
  resolve it. Older scholarship treated Memra as a hypostasis; much recent work
  treats it as a translational convention. Attribute both, decide neither.
- **Dabar** — the Hebrew word for "word" carries a range Greek *logos* does not:
  word, matter, thing, event. **A Hebrew "word" is closer to a *deed* than a Greek
  one.** This is directly relevant to what "losing a word" could mean.

## RELEVANCE TO THE MASONIC QUESTION

Stated bluntly, because the temptation to build a bridge here is strong and the
evidence does not support one.

**Masonic ritual does not use Logos language.** It does not call the lost word a
Logos, does not quote John 1, and does not employ the vocabulary of divine
utterance. Its scriptural furniture is 1 Kings and 2 Chronicles: pillars, a temple,
a builder. The material is architectural.

**What the Logos material is doing in this book**, then, is not supplying a source.
It is supplying **the conceptual background against which a Western audience hears
the phrase "the Word."** A reader in 1730 who encountered a ritual about a lost
Word brought John 1 with him whether or not the compilers put it there — and that
is a fact about reception, which Part II establishes and Part XII returns to.

This is the §XLV distinction in one of its clearest forms: **the meaning is real
and the intention is unevidenced.** A candidate who heard "the Word" theologically
was not misunderstanding the ceremony; he was doing what any English speaker of his
century would do. But his hearing is not evidence about the ceremony's makers, and
the literature has consistently treated it as though it were.

**Evidence level for any Logos-to-Masonry transmission claim: E6.** For the
reception claim — that Masonic audiences heard theological resonance in the word
"Word" — **E2**, and it matters.
