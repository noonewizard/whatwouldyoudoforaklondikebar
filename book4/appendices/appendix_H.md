# APPENDIX H
## The Six Solomons

Consolidated from Chapters 28 and 57. Conflating any two produces a false history.

| # | Figure | Period | Evidence | Connection to magic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The historical king** | 10th c. BCE | No extrabiblical attestation; scale disputed | None — the category does not arise |
| 2 | **The biblical wisdom figure** | The text, composed later | 1 Kings; Wisdom of Solomon | None directly; supplies the warrant for everything else |
| 3 | **The legendary spirit-commander** | Late antiquity onward | Josephus (1st c.); *Testament of Solomon*; Talmudic shamir narratives; Qur'anic Sulaymān and later Islamic tradition | Central |
| 4 | **The grimoire operator** | Medieval onward | Greek *Hygromanteia*; Latin *Clavicula*; *Lemegeton* | Central, and operative — the texts are for use |
| 5 | **The patron of natural philosophy** | Renaissance/early modern | Bacon's *New Atlantis* (1627), Salomon's House | None — leads to the Royal Society |
| 6 | **The model of sacral kingship** | Renaissance/early modern | The Escorial; "new Solomon" panegyric | None |

### The point of the table

Figures 5 and 6 are the Solomons available to an educated Londoner in 1723 — the
wise king, the great builder, the patron of knowledge. The magical Solomon existed
in the culture and was disreputable.

**There is no evidence that Anderson's Temple history had the magical Solomon in
mind**, and a great deal of later writing has assumed the reverse (ch 57).

### The divergence worth noticing

Figures 3–4 and figure 5 grow from the *same* scriptural warrant — 1 Kings 4:33
and Wisdom of Solomon 7 — read by different people for different purposes. One
warrant produced both a tradition of spirit-commanding manuals and a program for
experimental science.

The warrant did not determine the outcome. The readers did (ch 57).
