# MASONIC ARCHITECTURE STUDY — DELIVERABLE 11

Actual surviving Masonic buildings and plans, per the master prompt's explicit
requirement to investigate real architecture rather than only symbolism.

---

## WHY THIS STUDY MATTERS FOR THE BOOK'S ARGUMENT

Books I–III examined Masonic ritual, symbol, and language largely as texts and
performances. This book's Part VIII and ch.65 require something those volumes did
not: **physical buildings that can be measured, dated, and compared**, giving the
book's "does the Lodge resemble a Temple" question an actual evidentiary basis
rather than a purely textual one.

---

## THE GENERAL FINDING, STATED IN ADVANCE OF FULL SURVEY

Most 18th-century English and Scottish lodges met in **rented rooms in taverns and
private houses** — this is well-established (per Books I–III) and means that for
the majority of the Craft's first century, "Masonic architecture" in the sense of
purpose-built symbolic buildings essentially does not exist. **Purpose-built
Masonic halls and temples are substantially a 19th-century and later phenomenon**,
consistent with the general pattern (established across this series) that the
occult/symbolic elaboration of Masonic material intensifies after 1850, though
architectural investment has somewhat different drivers (institutional wealth,
respectability politics, American civic building culture) than the Lévi/Pike
vocabulary chain.

## CANDIDATE BUILDINGS FOR CASE-STUDY TREATMENT (ch.65)

**Freemasons' Hall, London** (current building 1933, on a site used since 1775) —
the documentary record of successive buildings on this site provides a clean
before/after case for the "purpose-built vs rented room" transition.

**Grand Lodge of Scotland, Edinburgh** (current building 1858) — comparable case
for the Scottish constitution.

**House of the Temple, Washington, D.C.** (Scottish Rite Southern Jurisdiction
headquarters, completed 1915, architect John Russell Pope) — explicitly modeled
on the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, not on any reconstruction of Solomon's Temple;
this is a documented and instructive case of Masonic monumental architecture
NOT drawing on Temple-reconstruction imagery despite the name "Temple," which
complicates any simple "Masonic buildings = Solomon's Temple reconstructions"
assumption and should be reported as a genuine complication, not smoothed over.

**Detroit Masonic Temple** (1926, cited in Book II as an instance of the "portable
period vs. monumental period" contraction thesis) — reusable here for the
architectural-investment side of that same argument.

**Scottish Rite Temples generally in the American context** (numerous, varying
widely in style from classical to eclectic/exotic revival) — a comparative sample
should be assembled rather than treating any single building as representative,
per the jurisdictional-variation standing rule carried through this series.

## THE ORIENTATION QUESTION, PHYSICALLY TESTED

Book III ch.46 established that Masonic "east" is a ritual designation, not
necessarily a compass bearing, because lodges meet in rooms of whatever actual
orientation the building has. **This book's ch.36 should test that claim against
actual purpose-built Masonic halls specifically** (as opposed to the rented-room
case Book III addressed), since a purpose-built hall COULD in principle be
constructed with true compass-east orientation if the builders wished it. Whether
purpose-built 19th/20th-century Masonic temples in fact show a statistically
notable preference for true-east orientation, or continue the "designated east
regardless of compass bearing" pattern, is an empirically checkable question this
dossier flags for ch.36 but has not yet resourced with a systematic survey.
**[SVR — Tier 2 gap, would strengthen but is not essential to the chapter's core
argument, which can proceed on the ritual-designation reading already established.]**
