# CHAPTER TWO
## Solomon

There is no inscription naming him.

That sentence needs care, because it is easily misread in both directions. It does
not mean Solomon is a fiction. It means that, as of this writing, no contemporary
tenth-century-BCE text has been recovered from anywhere in the ancient Near East
that mentions a king of Israel called Solomon — and that this is a different
evidentiary situation from the one his father is in.

---

### The contrast with David

In 1993, at Tel Dan in northern Israel, excavators recovered fragments of a basalt
stele inscribed in Aramaic, erected by a foreign king boasting of victories over
Israel. Among the phrases is one reading, on the standard interpretation, *bytdwd*
— the House of David.[^1]

It is ninth century, roughly a hundred years after David's traditional dates. It
is not Israelite; it is the enemy's account. And it is external, physical,
datable evidence that by the ninth century a dynasty in the southern Levant was
known by the name of a founder called David.

The inscription was contested when published and remains discussed, but the
reading has held up well and is broadly accepted. **It is the single most
important external datum in this entire area of study.**

**There is nothing comparable for Solomon.**

This asymmetry is rarely stated plainly in popular treatments, in either
direction. Defenders of biblical historicity cite Tel Dan and let the confidence
transfer silently from father to son. Skeptics cite Solomon's absence and let it
transfer backward onto David. Neither move is warranted. The honest position is
that the evidence for the two men is not the same, and that Solomon's is thinner.

---

### The dispute, and why it cannot be waved past

What follows is the most contested question in biblical archaeology, and a reader
is entitled to know that this book will not resolve it.

**The traditional position**, associated historically with the school of William
F. Albright and defended in modified forms by scholars including Kenneth Kitchen
and Amihai Mazar, holds that the biblical account reflects a real tenth-century
polity substantial enough to have undertaken monumental construction. Certain
archaeological features — notably the six-chambered gates at Megiddo, Hazor and
Gezer — were long read as evidence of a centralized building program, and the
biblical note that Solomon fortified exactly those three cities was taken as
confirmation.[^2]

**The revisionist position**, associated most influentially with Israel
Finkelstein's "Low Chronology," holds that the strata containing those gates
should be dated roughly a century later, to the Omride kings of the ninth
century — which would remove the material basis for a tenth-century Solomonic
building program entirely. On this reading, tenth-century Jerusalem was a modest
highland town, and the grand United Monarchy is a retrojection by later writers
building a usable past.[^3]

**And a more radical position still**, associated with what is often called the
Copenhagen school — Niels Peter Lemche, Thomas L. Thompson, Philip Davies —
treats the United Monarchy as substantially a literary construction of the Persian
or Hellenistic period.

The Low Chronology has itself been challenged, notably on radiocarbon and
archaeomagnetic grounds, and the debate has not converged. **Competent, serious
scholars currently occupy the full range of positions**, and anyone who tells you
otherwise is selling something.

---

### What this means for the Temple

Directly and uncomfortably: **if the Low Chronology or a more radical position is
correct, the Temple as described in 1 Kings 6 may never have existed in the tenth
century at all**, and the description may be a later idealisation — possibly
modeled on the Second Temple its authors could actually see, projected backward
onto a founder-king.

If the traditional position is correct, a tenth-century Temple of roughly the
described kind is entirely plausible, and the text preserves a genuine memory.

**This book's position is that the question is open**, that it is not this
author's to close, and that — crucially — *the rest of the book does not depend on
the answer.*

That last point is worth dwelling on. Everything from Chapter 6 onward concerns
what the Temple *became*: how it was read, rebuilt, symbolized, ritualized and
imagined. That history is entirely real and entirely documented regardless of what
stood in Jerusalem in 960 BCE. **A building that was never built can still
generate three thousand years of consequence, and if anything the consequence is
more interesting in that case, not less.**

---

### The biblical Solomon, briefly

One further distinction, because it is routinely lost.

The Solomon of Kings is not a flat hagiography. He is granted wisdom at Gibeon,
judges the two mothers, builds the Temple, receives the Queen of Sheba — and then
1 Kings 11 turns on him hard: foreign wives, foreign gods, a forced labor levy,
and a kingdom that splits immediately on his death precisely because of the burden
he imposed.[^4]

**The text contains its own critique.** Popular treatments, Masonic and hostile
alike, tend to take either the wise builder or the tyrant and drop the other. The
source has both, and any account that smooths this is not reporting the text.
