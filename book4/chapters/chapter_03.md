# CHAPTER THREE
## The First Temple

Here is what the text says. Everything in this chapter is Level 2 — what ancient
authors wrote — and nothing in it is Level 1, what physically stood. The
distinction will matter enormously by Chapter 66, so it is worth watching from
the start.

---

### The plan

**1 Kings 6:2.** The house is sixty cubits long, twenty broad, thirty high.

**The porch** before the house is twenty cubits across the front — matching the
house's width — and ten cubits deep.[^1]

**The main hall**, the *heikhal*, occupies forty cubits.

**The inner sanctuary**, the *debir*, is twenty cubits long, twenty broad, twenty
high. A cube.[^2]

**Side chambers** surround the house on three sides in three storeys, built
against the wall but — the text is specific about this — not into it: the wall was
stepped with rebatements so the beams rested on ledges rather than being socketed
into the sanctuary masonry. The chambers are five, six and seven cubits wide in
ascending order.[^3]

That is a **tripartite longitudinal plan**: you enter through a porch at the short
end, cross a long hall, and arrive at a square innermost room. Chapter 5 shows
that this is a recognisable regional type.

---

### The materials

Stone, dressed at the quarry so that "there was neither hammer nor axe nor any
tool of iron heard in the house, while it was in building."[^4] Cedar from
Lebanon, supplied by Hiram of Tyre. The interior lined with cedar so that no stone
was visible, carved with gourds and open flowers, and overlaid with gold.

The floor of fir. Doors of olive wood and fir, carved with cherubim, palm trees
and open flowers, overlaid with gold.

**Two bronze pillars** at the porch, eighteen cubits high, with capitals of five
cubits, decorated with networks, chains and pomegranates. They are given names:
Jachin and Boaz.[^5] Chapter 38 returns to them at length.

**A bronze sea**, ten cubits across, five high, thirty in circumference, standing
on twelve oxen. Chapter 11 takes up the arithmetic of that description, which is
one of the most discussed numerical cruxes in the Hebrew Bible.

---

### The inner sanctuary

Inside the cube: **two cherubim of olive wood, each ten cubits high**, with
wingspans of ten cubits, positioned so that one wing of each touched a wall and
their inner wings met in the middle of the room. Overlaid with gold.[^6]

Beneath their wings, the Ark of the Covenant.

**The room has no light.** No window is described. The text says the LORD said he
would dwell in thick darkness.[^7]

**And it is entered once a year, by one man.** Chapter 7 treats what that
restriction does.

---

### What the description does not give

This is the part that matters for everything after Chapter 66, and it is easy to
miss while reading a passage so full of numbers.

**There is no elevation.** No description of what the building looked like from
outside, beyond the pillars and the general dimensions. No roofline. No
description of the facade.

**There is no architectural order.** No columns in the classical sense supporting
anything, no capitals of any known Greek or Roman type, no proportional system
stated as a rule.

**There is no construction method for the walls** beyond the note about dressed
stone and the ledge arrangement for the side chambers.

**And the cubit is undefined.** Chapter 11 and Appendix D take this up; the short
version is that at least two cubit lengths are candidates, differing by roughly a
handbreadth, so that every metric conversion of every figure above carries an
uncertainty band before any other question is asked.

**A reconstruction requires supplying all of this**, and every reconstruction ever
made has supplied it from somewhere — from the reconstructor's own century's
architecture, most often. That is not a criticism of the reconstructors. It is the
unavoidable condition of the task.

---

### The other Temple in the Bible

One complication that the popular literature almost always drops.

**Ezekiel 40–48 describes a Temple too**, in far greater dimensional detail than
1 Kings, measured by a man with a line of flax and a measuring reed. It is not the
same building. It is a visionary structure, described in exile, with a different
plan, different measurements, and a different purpose in its text.

This matters because **the most influential reconstruction in European history —
Villalpando's — is primarily a reconstruction of Ezekiel's Temple, not Solomon's**,
and the two have been conflated in the popular imagination ever since. Chapter 67
untangles it.

---

### What the text is for

A closing observation about genre.

1 Kings 6–7 is not a builder's specification. It has the texture of one — the
numbers, the materials, the sequence — but a builder could not construct from it,
and it is not addressed to builders. It is addressed to readers, and what it is
doing is establishing that the thing was done **properly**: at the right scale,
with the best materials, by the right people, in the right order, with no
shortcuts.

**It is an account of legitimacy expressed as an inventory**, and read that way
its emphases make sense — the gold, the cedar, the absence of iron tool-noise, the
seven years. These are the details that establish the building's standing, which
is what the text cares about, rather than the details that would let you put up
a wall.
