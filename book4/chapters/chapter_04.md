# CHAPTER FOUR
## The Archaeological Problem

The single most important fact about the archaeology of Solomon's Temple is that
there isn't any.

Not "the evidence is ambiguous." Not "the finds are disputed." **There has never
been an archaeological excavation of the site where the Temple is believed to have
stood, and under any foreseeable circumstances there will not be one.**

Everything else in this chapter follows from that.

---

### Why not

The Temple Mount — *Har HaBayit* in Hebrew, *al-Haram al-Sharif* in Arabic — is an
active place of worship for one religious community, the most sacred site on earth
for another, and of profound significance to a third. The Dome of the Rock stands
on it. The al-Aqsa Mosque stands on it. It is administered under arrangements that
are themselves politically delicate, and the surrounding city has seen violence
triggered by far less than an excavation.

**No archaeologist of any nationality or religious background is going to dig
there**, and the reasons are not ones a reasonable person would want overridden.

This is not a temporary gap awaiting better funding or better technology. It is a
permanent condition of the subject.

---

### What has been done

Three categories, and it is worth being precise about each, because popular
accounts blur them.

**Surface and peripheral survey.** The platform's exterior, the retaining walls,
the surrounding excavations in the Ophel and the City of David to the south.
Charles Warren's shafts and tunnels for the Palestine Exploration Fund in
1867–1870 remain the most extensive exploration of the substructures ever
conducted, and were carried out by digging *outside* the walls and tunnelling
horizontally.[^1]

**Incidental exposure.** In 1999–2000, works authorised by the Islamic Waqf to
create a new entrance to an underground prayer space removed a large volume of
earth from the platform. The material was dumped, and has since been the subject
of a sifting project. **Archaeologists of multiple backgrounds criticised the
works at the time for destroying stratigraphy** — which is the real loss, because
an artefact without its layer is an artefact without a date.

**And inference from comparanda**, which is Chapter 5's subject.

**None of this is an excavation of the Temple**, and no responsible account should
present it as one.

---

### What the surrounding city does show

Real evidence, and worth stating positively rather than only as absence.

Jerusalem was continuously occupied through the relevant periods. The Gihon Spring
fortifications are massive and early. The **Stepped Stone Structure** on the
eastern slope of the City of David is a substantial Iron Age construction whose
dating and function are debated but whose existence is not.

**The 586 BCE destruction is externally corroborated.** The Babylonian Chronicles
record the campaign; the archaeological destruction horizon across Judah is
unambiguous. Something catastrophic happened, at that date, consistent with the
biblical account's central claim if not its every detail.[^2]

This is the right way to hold the evidence: **a real city, really destroyed, on
the traditional date — and no direct evidence about the building at its center.**

---

### The Megiddo problem

A cautionary case, because it shows how archaeological confidence can reverse.

For decades, the six-chambered gates at Megiddo, Hazor and Gezer were presented as
"Solomonic" — physical confirmation of 1 Kings 9:15, which names exactly those
three cities as Solomon's fortifications. The argument was clean and it appeared in
every textbook.

**The dating has since been challenged**, most influentially by Ussishkin and by
Finkelstein's Low Chronology, which would place the gates in the ninth-century
Omride period. The debate is live; radiocarbon work has been deployed on both
sides; no consensus has emerged.[^3]

**What is instructive is not which side is right but how the certainty behaved.**
A feature that was, for a generation, the textbook illustration of the Solomonic
building program is now a contested datum that may belong to a different century
and a different dynasty. A reader encountering the older confident version in an
older book would have no way of knowing.

---

### The discipline this imposes on the rest of the book

Three rules, stated here and applied throughout.

**Every reconstruction is labeled.** When this book shows an image of the Temple —
Villalpando's, a modern line drawing, a museum model — the caption says it is a
reconstruction and says what it is reconstructed from. Not once at the start:
every time.

**Absence of evidence is reported as absence of evidence**, not converted into
either confirmation or refutation. The Temple's archaeological invisibility is not
evidence that it did not exist; a wooden-and-stone building destroyed, rebuilt
over, and then buried under a Herodian platform and two later sanctuaries would be
invisible whether or not it had existed.

**And no gap is filled with imagination.** Where the evidence stops, this book
stops, and says so. The temptation to bridge with a plausible-sounding sentence is
strong precisely because the gaps are large.

---

### The uncomfortable consequence

If you want to know what Solomon's Temple looked like, the honest answer is that
you are asking a question the available evidence cannot answer, and that everyone
who has ever answered it confidently — for two and a half thousand years — was
supplying the missing information from somewhere other than the building.

**Where they got it from is the subject of the rest of this book**, and it turns
out to be a considerably better story than the one archaeology would have told if
it could.
