# CHAPTER FIVE
## Solomon's Temple and the Ancient Near East

In 1980 a farmer near the village of 'Ain Dara in northern Syria turned up a
carved basalt lion. Excavation followed, and what emerged was a temple: Iron Age,
monumental, well preserved, and built on a plan that anyone who has read 1 Kings 6
will recognise immediately.

---

### What 'Ain Dara has

**A tripartite longitudinal plan.** Porch, main hall, inner shrine, arranged on a
single axis, entered at the short end. The same sequence, in the same order, as
the biblical description.

**Comparable proportions**, though the building is somewhat larger.

**Guardian figures** — lions and sphinx-like composite creatures — flanking the
approach and carved along the base, which is the iconographic category the
biblical cherubim belong to (Chapter 9).

**Decorative motifs** including palmettes, which parallel the palm trees of the
1 Kings interior description.

John Monson's comparative study, published in an accessible form in 2000, made the
case that this is the closest architectural parallel to the biblical Temple yet
excavated, and the assessment has held.[^1]

---

### What that does and does not establish

Run the comparison through the transmission protocol this series has applied since
Book I, and the result is instructive precisely because it is *positive* — which is
not how most of this series' transmission tests have come out.

**Chronology: passes.** The temple's use overlaps the relevant period.

**Geographical contact: passes, and well.** This is not a speculative link across
continents. Northern Syria and the Levantine coast were in continuous contact with
Israel and Judah. The biblical text itself says the Temple's craftsmen and
materials came from Tyre, which is precisely this cultural zone.

**Verbal or documentary dependence: not applicable and not claimed.** Nobody is
suggesting the builders of one read a text describing the other.

**Alternative explanation: shared regional convention.** And this is the correct
answer, not a fallback. The tripartite temple is a **type** in the Syro-Levantine
Iron Age, attested at multiple sites. A Phoenician-assisted royal temple in
tenth-century Jerusalem built to that type is exactly what one would expect, in
the way that a nineteenth-century English parish church being cruciform is exactly
what one would expect.

**Verdict: convergence on a regional building tradition.** The resemblance is
real, the explanation is mundane, and no exotic transmission is required or
supported.

---

### Why a positive finding matters here

This series has spent three volumes finding that proposed ancient connections
dissolve — the Templar chain, the Egyptian-mystery chain, the Kabbalistic chain,
each dated far later than claimed or unsupported by any documented route.

**A reader could reasonably start to suspect the method of producing only one kind
of answer.**

'Ain Dara is the corrective. Here is a proposed connection that holds up, tested
by the same questions, and the book reports it as clearly as it reports the
failures. **A method that can only debunk is not a method.** It is a disposition
wearing a method's clothes.

What the 'Ain Dara comparison does *not* license is the further step the popular
literature usually takes — that because the Temple resembles other ANE temples, it
must therefore share their theology, their cultic practice, or their mythology.
Architecture is borrowed more easily than belief, and a shared ground plan is a
shared ground plan.

---

### Tel Motza

The closest excavated temple to Jerusalem itself sits about seven kilometres away,
discovered during highway construction and excavated from 2012.

It is a monumental structure of the ninth or eighth century BCE, with an altar and
cultic artefacts including figurines, and it is **Judahite**.[^2]

Its significance here is double-edged, and the second edge is the one usually
dropped.

**It supports the general picture** that formal temple architecture on the
regional pattern existed in Judah in this era.

**And it complicates the biblical account of cultic centralisation**, because it
is a substantial temple operating close to Jerusalem in a period when the
Deuteronomistic ideology insists worship should have been concentrated at the
Jerusalem Temple alone. Either centralisation was less complete in practice than
in theory, or it came later than the text implies.

This book notes that complication rather than smoothing it, because smoothing it
would be exactly the kind of move Part XIV is about.

---

### Mesopotamia and Egypt: the comparisons that do not work

Two negative results, both useful.

**The ziggurat is not the relevant comparandum.** Mesopotamian temple towers are
stepped, massive, and structurally unlike the Levantine tripartite longitudinal
plan. Where popular writing conflates "Solomon's Temple" with "ziggurat," it is
comparing across architectural families. The Mesopotamian material is genuinely
relevant to Chapter 10's cosmological themes — temple as cosmic mountain — but
that is a *conceptual* parallel and should never be mistaken for a formal one.

**Egyptian temple architecture** shares the general principle of graded restriction
toward an inner sanctum, and differs in nearly everything else — plan, materials,
scale, and the religious system it served.

Its real value is as a control. **The fact that Egyptian, Mesopotamian and
Levantine temples all restrict access toward a centre tells us that restriction is
a near-universal solution to a near-universal problem**, not that any of them
learned it from the others. This is the half-of-everything rule from the preceding
volumes, applied to architecture: when a design feature has very few possible
forms, its recurrence across unconnected traditions carries almost no information.
