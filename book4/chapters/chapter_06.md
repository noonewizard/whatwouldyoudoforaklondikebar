# PART II — THE TEMPLE AS COSMOS

# CHAPTER SIX
## Sacred Space

What makes a piece of ground different from the ground beside it?

Nothing you can measure. Soil chemistry does not change at the threshold of a
temple. The question is worth asking in that blunt form because the answers
divide sharply, and the division runs through this entire book.

---

### Two accounts of sacredness

**The substantive account**: certain places *are* sacred, because something
happened there, or something is present there, or the place has a quality that
other places lack. A theophany occurred; a relic rests; the ground itself is
charged. On this account sacredness is discovered.

**The relational account**: places *become* sacred by being set apart — by an act
of designation, maintained by a community, marked by boundaries and enforced by
practice. On this account sacredness is conferred.

Jonathan Z. Smith's *To Take Place* made the case for the second as forcefully as
it has been made, and this series has used it since Book II.[^1] Smith's argument
is that ritual does not respond to the sacredness of a place; ritual *produces* it,
by acts of emplacement, attention and differentiation.

**The two accounts are not equally testable.** The substantive account makes a
claim historical method cannot reach. The relational account makes a claim about
observable human behaviour, and the behaviour is observable.

This book therefore works with the relational account throughout — not because the
substantive one is false, which it is not this book's business to determine, but
because the relational one is the one with evidence attached.

---

### The four operations

Strip the theology and sacred space is produced by four operations, all of them
visible in the biblical Temple and in every other temple this book examines.

**Boundary.** A line, marked and enforced, dividing inside from outside. The
Temple's courts, walls and gates do this at multiple levels. A boundary that is
not enforced is decoration; a boundary that is enforced is a fact about who may
do what.

**Threshold.** The specific point of crossing, which is always more elaborately
marked than the boundary it interrupts. Gates, porches, doorways. Chapter 48
examines what crossing one does to a person's attention and memory.

**Orientation.** A direction designated as primary, relative to which everything
else is positioned. The Temple faces east; the lodge has an east. Chapter 36
examines how loosely "east" can be held while still doing its work.

**Centre.** A point toward which the whole arrangement is oriented and from which
access is most restricted. Chapter 7 is about what happens when that centre is
made empty and dark.

**All four are arrangements, not substances.** Any group of people can perform
them, in any room, and the preceding volume in this series showed that Freemasonry
does precisely this — opens a room, works, and closes it, after which the room is
a hall with chairs in it again.

---

### Graded access

The single most consistent feature across the temples in this book, and the one
that carries the most information.

The Jerusalem Temple as described grades access sharply: an outer area, an inner
court, the hall, the innermost room. Different categories of person may proceed to
different depths. The Second Temple's arrangements, described by Josephus and in
the Mishnah, are more elaborate still — courts for gentiles, for women, for
Israelite men, for priests.[^2]

**This is a social structure expressed as a floor plan**, and it is legible
without any theology at all. Tell me how far into a building each category of
person may go, and I can tell you a great deal about how that society is
organised.

Chapter 83 develops this as the book's "architecture as governance" argument. What
matters here is the observation that **the Temple's most consistent message is not
about God. It is about hierarchy** — and that this is true of nearly every sacred
building ever constructed, which is one reason kings build them.

---

### The cost of the relational account

A caution, because this chapter has been building a framework and frameworks
conceal their costs.

If sacredness is conferred rather than discovered, then it can be withdrawn, and
the building can become a hall with chairs in it. That is a real consequence and
communities have felt it as loss.

It also means **the Temple's destruction did not destroy what the Temple did** —
because the operations that produced its sacredness are portable, and a community
can perform them elsewhere, which is exactly what happened after 70 CE and is the
subject of Chapter 85.

**A substance can be destroyed. A practice can be relocated.** Everything in Part
XVIII depends on that distinction, and it originates here.
