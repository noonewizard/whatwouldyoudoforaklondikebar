# CHAPTER NINE
## Cherubim

Whatever a cherub is, it is not a baby with wings.

The plump infant of Renaissance and Baroque painting — the *putto* — is a
classical artistic form that acquired the name by a long confusion. The biblical
cherubim are something else entirely, and reconstructing what requires setting
aside the visual vocabulary a modern reader arrives with.

---

### The biblical evidence

They are **guardians**. Their first appearance places them east of Eden with a
flaming sword, barring return.[^1]

They are **associated with the divine throne and with motion**. God is described
as enthroned upon the cherubim, and as riding upon a cherub.[^2]

They are **images in the Temple**: two of olive wood, ten cubits high, in the inner
sanctuary; and carved on the walls and doors throughout, alternating with palm
trees and open flowers.[^3]

And in Ezekiel's vision they are described at length and in detail — four faces,
four wings, wheels, movement in any direction without turning — a description so
elaborate it became the foundation of an entire mystical tradition (Chapter 52).

**Note that the Ezekiel description and the 1 Kings Temple cherubim are not
obviously the same kind of being**, and the relationship between them is a genuine
exegetical problem rather than a settled identification.

---

### The iconographic context

What the ancient Near East supplies, and it is substantial.

**Composite guardian figures flanking entrances and thrones are ubiquitous** in
Mesopotamian, Syrian, Phoenician and Anatolian art: winged bulls, winged lions,
human-headed hybrids, sphinx forms. The Assyrian *lamassu* at palace gates is the
best-known example; the Phoenician winged sphinx appears on ivories and furniture
throughout the region.

**Thrones flanked by winged sphinxes are an attested Levantine royal form**, known
from ivory carvings and from the iconography of enthroned figures.[^4]

Against that background, the biblical cherubim are recognizable: **winged composite
guardian beings, positioned at boundaries and beside thrones, in a region where
that is exactly what such figures do.**

This is comparative context of the kind Chapter 5 established — a shared regional
visual vocabulary, not a borrowing to be traced from one artefact to another.

---

### The rule this chapter enforces

**Do not import later angelology backward.**

The elaborate celestial hierarchies of later Jewish and Christian tradition —
cherubim as a specific rank among seraphim, thrones, dominions and so forth —
belong to a developed systematic angelology whose major sources are late antique
and medieval, most influentially the treatise on the celestial hierarchy
attributed to Dionysius the Areopagite, a work of around the late fifth or early
sixth century CE.[^5]

**Reading that system back into 1 Kings is an anachronism of roughly fifteen
hundred years**, and it happens constantly, because the word is the same.

The Temple's cherubim are **furniture and architecture**: two large gilded objects
and a repeated decorative motif. They are not, in that text, a rank of angel with
a defined function in a celestial bureaucracy, because no such bureaucracy is in
view.

---

### The throne that is not described as a throne

One observation worth making, because it connects the objects to Chapter 7.

The inner sanctuary's two cherubim face the room with wings spread, meeting at the
center. Beneath and between them is the Ark.

**The arrangement is a throne** — a seat flanked by guardians, in the regional
royal idiom — **and the seat is unoccupied.** Nothing sits there. The text does not
describe an image of God, and the tradition is emphatic that there was none.

So the most sacred object arrangement in the building is a throne with nothing on
it, in a dark empty room nobody enters.

**That is a remarkably sophisticated piece of visual theology**, and whether it was
intended as such or is simply what results when an aniconic tradition borrows a
regional royal form and declines to fill it, the effect is the same and the effect
is what later readers encountered.
