# PART III — THE GEOMETRY OF THE TEMPLE

# CHAPTER ELEVEN
## Measurement

Every measurement in the Temple description is given in cubits, and **nobody knows
how long a cubit was.**

Not approximately — the range is not wild — but precisely enough to matter. At
least two standards are candidates, and they differ by roughly a handbreadth,
which compounds across a sixty-cubit building into a substantial difference in
actual size.

This is the first thing to establish in any discussion of the Temple's
proportions, because it sets a hard ceiling on what any metric claim can mean.

---

### The cubit problem

A cubit is, in origin, the length of a forearm — elbow to fingertip. That is a
real anatomical range, not a defined standard, and ancient societies formalised it
differently.

The Hebrew Bible itself distinguishes at least two, referring at one point to a
cubit "of a man" and at another to a measure that is a cubit and a
handbreadth.[^1] Ezekiel's measuring reed uses the longer standard explicitly.
Egyptian practice likewise distinguished a common from a royal cubit.

Proposals for the biblical cubit's length cluster in a range of roughly 44 to 52
centimetres, with the "long" or royal cubit running several centimetres above the
common one. **The uncertainty is on the order of ten to fifteen per cent.**

**What follows for this book**: any statement of the form "the Temple was X meters
long" carries that band, and any argument depending on a precise metric value is
resting on a number the sources do not supply. **Ratios, by contrast, are
unaffected** — sixty to twenty is three to one whatever the cubit was, which is
why Chapter 12 works in ratios and this one does not work in meters.

---

### The molten sea

Now the most discussed number in the Hebrew Bible after the ones in Daniel.

1 Kings 7:23 describes a great bronze basin: ten cubits from brim to brim, round,
five cubits high, "and a line of thirty cubits did compass it round about."

Diameter ten. Circumference thirty. **That implies a value for π of exactly 3.**

I computed the comparison rather than asserting it: the true circumference of a
circle of diameter ten is 31.4159…, so the text's figure is low by about 4.5 per
cent.[^2]

This has been noticed for as long as anyone has been reading the passage
arithmetically, and it has generated an enormous literature — apologetic,
polemical, and mathematical.

---

### Three ways to read it, and why the third matters most

**As rounding.** Thirty is a round number and the text is not a specification.
Ancient builders worked to practical tolerances and ancient writers rounded. This
is entirely sufficient as an explanation, requires nothing, and is probably
correct.

**As a Hebrew approximation of π.** Some have argued the passage encodes a more
sophisticated value through the spelling of the word for "line" in the verse —
a *qere/ketiv* variation whose gematria ratio is offered as yielding a closer
approximation. **This is a numerological claim and it fails the tests of Part XV**:
the procedure is selected after the answer is known, no control is run, and the
same technique applied to other verses would yield other numbers.

**As a description of a real object with a rim.** And this is the one worth
dwelling on. Three verses later the text says the basin's brim was "wrought like
the brim of a cup, with flowers of lilies," and that it was a handbreadth
thick.[^3]

**If the ten-cubit diameter measures the inner bore and the thirty-cubit line
measures around the outside of a thickened, flared rim, both figures can be
simultaneously accurate of the same object, and no approximation of π is involved
at all.** A version of this resolution appears in the Talmudic discussion and in
later harmonisations; it is not a modern apologetic invention.[^4]

**I am not asserting that this is the correct reading.** It is one of at least
three, and the rounding explanation is simpler.

What the case demonstrates is the methodological point this whole Part is built
on: **before treating a numerical anomaly as meaningful, ask whether a mundane
physical explanation dissolves it.** Here one does. The literature that treats the
molten sea as evidence of encoded mathematical knowledge — in either direction,
whether to praise or to ridicule the text — generally does not mention the rim.

---

### What the numbers are actually like

A summary before Chapter 12 does the ratios.

The Temple's stated dimensions are **small, round, and mostly multiples of ten**:
sixty, twenty, thirty, ten, five. The side chambers step five, six, seven. The
inner sanctuary is twenty cubed.

**These are the numbers of a builder's rule of thumb, not of a proportional
system.** They are what you get when you set out a rectangle by pacing and
doubling. Chapter 12 tests whether anything more sophisticated is present, using
the method Part XV formalises, and the answer is no.
