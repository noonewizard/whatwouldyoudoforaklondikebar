# CHAPTER TWELVE
## Proportion

Do the Temple's dimensions encode a proportional system?

The question has a clean answer, and getting to it requires doing something the
literature on this subject almost never does: **testing all the ratios, not the
ones that work.**

---

### The stated ratios

From 1 Kings 6, in cubits:

| | Length | Width | Height |
|---|---|---|---|
| The house | 60 | 20 | 30 |
| The porch | 20 | 10 | — |
| Inner sanctuary | 20 | 20 | 20 |

The principal relations fall out immediately:

**Length to width: 3 : 1.**
**Length to height: 2 : 1.**
**Width to height: 2 : 3.**
**The porch: 2 : 1.**
**The inner sanctuary: a cube.**

These are computed rather than eyeballed, and they are exact — no rounding, no
tolerance required.[^1]

---

### What that pattern is

**Small whole-number ratios**, of the kind that arise naturally from any
setting-out procedure using a cord and a standard length.

If you lay out a rectangle by pacing twenty units and then three times twenty, you
have a 3:1 building without having thought about proportion at all. If your
innermost room is as long as the building is wide and as high as it is long, you
have a cube by consequence rather than by design.

**This does not mean the ratios are meaningless.** A cube is a strong, legible
form, and the text's decision to state it explicitly — rather than leaving it to
be inferred — suggests the writer thought it worth saying. What it means is that
the ratios require no theory to explain. They are what a competent builder
produces.

**Compare a system that genuinely encodes proportion.** Vitruvius states rules:
the module, the derivation of every dimension from a base unit, the correspondence
to human proportion, with the reasoning given. Alberti does the same, grounding
proportion in musical ratios and saying so. Palladio publishes his schemes.

**In every case the design is documented by the designer.** That is the standard
Book III's Chapter 45 established for architectural claims, and it is the only
standard that distinguishes a proportioned building from a building someone has
measured afterward.

**1 Kings supplies no rule.** It reports dimensions. It does not say why those
dimensions, does not derive them from a module, and does not state a principle
from which they follow.

---

### The golden ratio, tested properly

Which brings us to the most common modern claim: that the Temple's dimensions
encode φ, the golden ratio, approximately 1.618.

This book tested it, and the test is worth describing in full because the *method*
is the contribution, not the result.

**The procedure, fixed before the test was run.** Take every explicitly stated
dimension from 1 Kings 6–7 — the house, the porch, the inner sanctuary, the side
chamber widths. Compute **every** pairwise ratio, in both directions. Count how
many fall within a stated tolerance of φ. **The tolerance was set at 5 per cent
before any arithmetic was done**, which is more generous than most published
claims admit to using.

**And run the identical procedure on a control**: a set of dimensions from an
ordinary building with no golden-ratio mythology attached to it.

**Result: the Temple set produced one hit in 110 pairs tested — 10:6, giving
1.6667, an error of 3.01 per cent.**

**The control set produced one hit in 56 pairs — 11:7, giving 1.5714, an error of
2.88 per cent.**[^2]

**The control's hit is closer to φ than the Temple's.**

---

### What that demonstrates

Not that the Temple has no proportional intention — that is a separate question
the evidence cannot settle either way. What it demonstrates is that **this method
cannot answer it.**

A search across a hundred-odd ratios with a five per cent tolerance will find φ in
almost any dimension set, because φ sits in a crowded neighborhood: 8:5 is 1.6,
13:8 is 1.625, 5:3 is 1.667, and any building using small whole numbers will
generate several of these.

**The literature reports the hit and not the denominator.** That is the entire
mechanism. Chapter 73 formalises it; Part XV builds the full protocol around it.

And note what makes this a genuine test rather than a rhetorical one: **it could
have come out the other way.** Had the Temple set produced five hits and the
control none, that would have been a result worth reporting, and this chapter
would say so. It did not.

---

### The honest summary

**The Temple's proportions are simple, legible, and unremarkable.** Three to one,
two to one, and a cube at the center. They are consistent with careful building
and inconsistent with nothing.

**No proportional rule is stated in the text**, and the one claim in wide
circulation that a sophisticated ratio is concealed there fails a control test
that anyone can rerun.

That is a thinner finding than the subject usually receives. It is also, as far as
the evidence goes, the whole of it.
