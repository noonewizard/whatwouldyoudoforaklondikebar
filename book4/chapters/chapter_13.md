# CHAPTER THIRTEEN
## Three, Five, Seven

The Temple's numbers are not mysterious, but they have four different kinds of
history, and separating them is most of the work.

For any recurring number in this material, ask: **is this meaning ancient, is it
biblical, is it later Jewish, is it Masonic, or is it modern invention?** The
answer is rarely the same across all five, and treating one as though it were all
of them is how a builder's convenience becomes a cosmic principle.

---

### Seven

**Biblical**: pervasive and genuinely structural. Creation in seven days. The
seventh day. The seven-branched lampstand. The seven-year Temple construction.
The dedication festival of seven days. Seven appears in the Temple narrative more
than any other number.

**Ancient Near Eastern**: also pervasive, and independently so. Seven recurs
across Mesopotamian and Ugaritic literature in ways that predate and surround the
Israelite material.

**Why**: the seven classical planets — the celestial bodies visible to the naked
eye that move against the fixed stars — and the lunar month's approximate
quartering. Both are observational facts available to anyone who looks up. The
seven-day week follows from them and spread very widely.

**This is the important point about seven, and it applies to most of this
chapter's numbers: its prominence is explained by something a person can see, not
by a doctrine that had to be transmitted.**

**Masonic**: seven liberal arts and sciences — the medieval *trivium* and
*quadrivium*, inherited from the Old Charges, which is a curriculum rather than a
mystery. Seven required to make a lodge perfect in some workings: a quorum rule.

**Modern esoteric**: seven chakras, seven rays, seven planes. These belong to
nineteenth- and twentieth-century systems and have no bearing on the biblical or
Masonic material despite constant conflation.

---

### Three

**Biblical**: the Temple's tripartite plan — porch, hall, inner sanctuary — and the
three-storey side chambers. The tripartite plan is, as Chapter 5 showed, a
regional architectural type.

**Structural**: three is the minimum number that makes a sequence, and a building
you pass through requires at least a beginning, a middle and an end. The preceding
volume in this series made this argument for ritual; it applies identically to
architecture. **A processional building cannot have fewer than three zones and be
processional.**

**Masonic**: three degrees, three principal officers, three lights. The preceding
volume established that these are entailed by practical arrangement — a room needs
stations, a graded system needs stages — rather than chosen for numerical meaning.

---

### Five

**Biblical, in the Temple**: five-cubit capitals on the pillars; the side chambers
beginning at five cubits; the molten sea five cubits high. These are dimensions,
not a motif.

**Architectural**: the five classical orders, which enter Masonic symbolism from
the Renaissance architectural literature — Serlio, Vignola — and are simply the
standard enumeration in that literature. **Five because Vignola said five**, not
because of any older tradition.

**Anatomical**: five fingers, five senses, the five points of a human figure with
limbs extended. This is Vitruvian material (Chapter 56) and it is why five-pointed
figures are easy for human beings to find meaningful.

---

### The test, stated once for the whole Part

For a number in a building or a ritual to be carrying a doctrine, it must be
**arbitrary** — it could have been otherwise and was not — and **stable**, appearing
consistently rather than varying, and **unexplained by any mundane source**.

Apply it:

**Seven**: explained by the planets and the week. Not arbitrary.
**Three**: entailed by processional architecture and by sequence. Not arbitrary.
**Five**: entailed by the architectural literature and by anatomy. Not arbitrary.
**Twelve** (Chapter 14): explained by divisibility and by lunations. Not arbitrary.

**None of the Temple's recurring numbers survives as arbitrary**, which means none
of them carries information about anyone's intentions beyond the practical.

---

### What the numbers do instead

A closing observation, because a chapter of negatives should say what is actually
going on.

The Temple's numbers do real work, and the work is **rhetorical rather than
cryptographic.** Seven years of construction says *this was done properly and
completely*. A cube says *this is finished and regular*. Three zones say *there is
an inside to this, and you are not in it yet*.

**These are legible to a reader without any key**, which is the opposite of a
code. A code conceals from those who lack the rule; the Temple's numbers
communicate to anyone who counts. The seven-year construction is not hiding
anything. It is making a claim about thoroughness that its original audience would
have received immediately.

That is what numbers in this literature overwhelmingly do, and Chapter 14 tests the
harder cases where someone has argued otherwise.
