# CHAPTER FIFTEEN
## Sacred Geometry

There are four different things called sacred geometry, they have almost nothing
to do with each other, and the confusion between them is worth more to the
popular literature than any of them is worth individually.

---

### One: documented historical proportional systems

**Real, well-evidenced, and not esoteric.**

Vitruvius sets out proportional principles in the first century BCE: the module,
the derivation of dimensions from a base unit, the analogy to the proportions of
the human body. Alberti grounds architectural proportion in musical ratios in
1452 and explains why. Palladio publishes his schemes with dimensions in 1570.

**In every case we know the buildings are proportioned because the architect wrote
a treatise saying how and why.** There is no decipherment involved. There is
reading.

This is the gold standard for any claim of designed proportion, and it is worth
naming clearly: **the rule exists independently of the measurement.**

### Two: medieval operative construction technique

Also real, and different in kind.

*Ad quadratum* and *ad triangulum* construction — deriving a building's
proportions by rotating squares within squares, or by equilateral triangles — are
documented techniques of medieval masons, attested in surviving lodge books and
setting-out drawings. The dispute at Milan Cathedral in the 1390s, in which the
masters argued over which system to use and the proceedings were recorded, is the
best-known case.[^1]

**These are construction methods**: ways of getting a building to come out right
with a cord, a square and a pair of compasses. They are not numerical symbolism,
and the surviving evidence does not suggest the masons thought their ratios
encoded doctrine. They thought they were building something that would stand up.

### Three: Masonic geometric symbolism

**Geometry praised, not geometry practised.**

The Old Charges call Masonry a geometrical science; Anderson elevates Euclid's
forty-seventh proposition; the working tools are instruments of measure. But no
geometric construction is performed in Craft ritual, no proportions are specified,
and the tools are used to *test* — is this true, is this level, is this upright —
rather than to generate.

The preceding volume established this and the finding is unchanged: a tradition
that praised geometry and performed none.

### Four: the modern sacred-geometry movement

Emerging substantially from the 1970s onward and popularised by works such as
Robert Lawlor's *Sacred Geometry: Philosophy and Practice* (1982), this asserts a
continuous esoteric geometric tradition from antiquity to the present, in which
specific figures and ratios carry metaphysical content recoverable by measuring
old buildings.[^2]

**It asserts continuity with categories one and two, and the documentary record
does not support the continuity in the form claimed.**

---

### Why the conflation is so productive

The move that makes this literature work is simple and almost always implicit.

**Category one is genuine and impressive.** Vitruvius, Alberti and Palladio are
real, their systems are documented, and their buildings are beautiful.

**Category four borrows category one's credibility** by using the same vocabulary —
proportion, ratio, harmony, the golden section — and applying it by a completely
different method: measuring afterwards rather than designing beforehand.

**The difference is the whole thing.** Palladio tells you the rule and then builds.
The retrospective measurer finds a ratio and infers a rule. **One is a
specification; the other is a search**, and Chapter 12's control test showed what
searches return.

---

### Applied to the Temple

The Temple belongs to none of the four categories cleanly, which is why it has
been claimed by all of them.

**It is not category one**: no rule is stated in 1 Kings. The text reports
dimensions and supplies no generative principle.

**It is not category two**: no construction technique is described beyond the note
about dressed stone.

**It became category three** in Masonic use, as an emblem of geometry's dignity
rather than as a worked example of it.

**And it is heavily claimed by category four**, where its dimensions — small, round,
and sufficiently numerous to search — are ideal raw material.

---

### What would count as evidence

Stated so the chapter's negative is falsifiable.

**A documented ancient text specifying a proportional rule for temple design** —
not reporting dimensions, but stating a principle from which they follow, as
Vitruvius does for Roman architecture. Something of the form *the width shall be
such that…*

**No such text survives for the First Temple.** 1 Kings reports; it does not
prescribe. Ezekiel measures; he does not derive.

That absence is not proof that no rule existed. It is the reason no rule can be
demonstrated, which is a different and more modest claim, and it is the one the
evidence supports.
