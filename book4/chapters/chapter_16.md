# PART IV — THE TEMPLE AND THE WORD

# CHAPTER SIXTEEN
## The Word Has a House

The preceding volume of this series ended with a man being told that a word was
unavailable, and with the observation that words are not the sort of thing that
can be lost — that what goes is the authority to speak, the setting in which
speaking counts, or the condition of the speaker.

**This Part is about the period when that setting had an address.**

---

### The house is for the name

Chapter 1 noted the formula and deferred it. It needs taking up properly now.

The Temple is not described as a house for God. It is described, repeatedly and
carefully, as a house **for the name** of God. Deuteronomy's characteristic phrase
is *the place which the LORD your God shall choose to put his name there*.
Solomon's dedication prayer works the same way: he has built a house for the name;
God's dwelling place is heaven; what is at the Temple is the name, and the
attention that goes with it.[^1]

Scholars have called this "name theology" and debated its date and its
theological motive at length. What matters here is its **structure**, which is
peculiar and precise: it locates something of God at a site without locating God
at the site, which is exactly the difficulty Solomon's own speech raises.

**A name is the right thing to put in a building**, if you want this result. A name
is not a body; it takes up no room; it can be present in a place without being
confined to it. And — the point that matters for this Part — **a name is an
instrument of address.** To put God's name in a house is to establish that this is
where God may be called.

---

### Presence as a problem

Three vocabularies do the same work in the Hebrew Bible and later Jewish thought,
and they should be distinguished rather than merged.

**The name** — *shem* — as above: what is placed at the site.

**The glory** — *kavod* — which fills the house at the dedication so that the
priests cannot stand to minister, and which departs in Ezekiel's vision before the
destruction.[^2] This is presence as a phenomenon: visible, overwhelming, and
capable of leaving.

**And later, the *shekhinah*** — the indwelling, a term of rabbinic development
rather than biblical vocabulary, whose history belongs to Chapter 52 and should
not be read back into 1 Kings.

**Three distinct concepts**, and popular writing tends to use whichever is
convenient. The name is *put*; the glory *fills* and can *depart*; the shekhinah
is a later theological term doing different work.

---

### What the Temple gave the word

Here is the argument this Part exists to make, stated plainly before its
chapters develop it.

The preceding volume established that a word requires four things to operate: the
word itself, the authority to use it, the setting in which using it counts, and
the condition of the speaker. Take away any of the last three and the word is
inert, however perfectly remembered.

**The Temple was the setting.** For as long as it stood, there was a place where
the divine name was spoken — once a year, by one man, in a dark room — and the
speaking had institutional standing because the room existed and the office
existed and the day existed.

**When the building fell, the setting went with it.** Chapter 17 takes up what
happened to the name; Chapter 84 takes up what happened to the institution.

What this chapter establishes is the relation: **a word and a building were, for a
period, parts of the same apparatus**, and the loss of one is not separable from
the loss of the other. The preceding volume asked what it means for a word to be
lost and answered that the string never is. This one adds the missing half: **the
address can be.**

---

### The direction of dependence

One observation that will matter in Part XVIII.

It is natural to assume that the building was primary and the word ancillary — that
the Temple was the important thing and the name it housed was its contents.

**The texts suggest the reverse.** The building is justified by reference to the
name, not the other way round. Solomon's speech spends its length on what the
house is *for*, and the answer is always relational: a place toward which prayer is
directed, a place where the name is invoked, a place God has agreed to attend to.

**The Temple is infrastructure for an act of address.** That is an unromantic way
to put it and it is, I think, what the sources actually say — and it explains
something Chapter 85 will need: why the loss of the building did not end the
practice. Infrastructure can be rebuilt elsewhere, or dispensed with, if what it
supported can be supported another way.

Prayer, as it turned out, could be.
