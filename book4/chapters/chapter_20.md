# PART V — THE BUILDERS

# CHAPTER TWENTY
## Hiram King of Tyre

There are two Hirams in the biblical Temple narrative and confusing them is the
oldest error in this subject.

This chapter is about the first: a Phoenician king, an ally, a supplier, and the
source of the single most useful piece of external corroboration in the entire
Solomonic story.

---

### What the text says

Hiram of Tyre appears first in connection with David, sending cedar and craftsmen
for a palace.[^1] Under Solomon the relationship becomes a formal treaty
arrangement: Tyre supplies cedar and cypress timber and skilled labor; Israel
supplies wheat and oil in annual quantities.[^2]

The arrangement is described in the register of commercial diplomacy, and it has
friction in it. At the end of twenty years Solomon transfers twenty cities in
Galilee to Hiram, who inspects them and is unimpressed — "and he called them the
land of Cabul," which the text glosses as a name of dissatisfaction.[^3]

**That detail is worth noticing.** An account inventing a glorious international
partnership does not usually include the partner sneering at the payment. The
awkwardness has the texture of something remembered rather than composed.

---

### The external evidence

And here the Temple narrative acquires something it has nowhere else: a foreign
chronology.

Josephus, arguing in *Against Apion* that Jewish antiquity is corroborated by
non-Jewish sources, quotes the Hellenistic historian **Menander of Ephesus**, who
had worked from Tyrian archives. Menander's king-list gives a Hiram, with a reign
length and a genealogy, and Josephus uses it to date Solomon's Temple.[^4]

**What this is**: a Greek historian's summary, quoted by a first-century Jewish
historian with an apologetic purpose, of a Phoenician archive nobody has seen
since.

**What it is not**: an inscription. Nobody has recovered the Tyrian king-list.
We have a quotation of a summary of a lost source, preserved by an author with a
stake in the answer.

**And it is still the best we have.** The chronology Menander supplies is broadly
compatible with the biblical dates for Solomon, and the compatibility is not
trivially explained — Menander was working on Tyrian regnal history, not on
Israelite chronology, and a fabricated agreement would have required a purpose
nobody has identified.

**Evidence level: E3.** Genuinely suggestive, not independently verifiable,
and considerably better than anything available for Solomon himself.

---

### Why Tyre matters architecturally

A point Chapter 5 anticipated and this chapter can now complete.

**The Phoenicians were the region's dominant builders and metalworkers**, and the
biblical account does not merely say so in passing — it makes them structurally
necessary. The timber is Tyrian. The craftsmen are Tyrian. The bronze work,
including the pillars and the sea, is executed by a man whose father was a Tyrian
bronzeworker.

**This is an account in which the Temple is substantially built by foreigners**,
and the text is entirely untroubled by it.

Two consequences follow.

**It explains the architecture.** Chapter 5 established that the tripartite
longitudinal plan is a Syro-Levantine regional type. A building erected with
Phoenician technical direction would be expected to follow regional practice, and
the biblical description does. **The comparative evidence and the textual account
agree**, which is a convergence worth noting in a subject where they so often do
not.

**And it complicates the later use.** Masonic tradition makes the Temple the
archetype of the Craft. The biblical Temple is, on its own account, a project
whose skilled labor was imported from a neighboring kingdom with a different
religion — which is a perfectly good subject for a fraternity of working builders
to admire, and is not the same thing as a sacred national construction carried out
by an initiated priesthood.

---

### The confusion with the craftsman

Stated plainly because the rest of the Part depends on it.

**Hiram king of Tyre is not a builder.** He supplies. He negotiates. He sends
people. At no point does the text describe him working.

**The craftsman is a different man with the same name**, introduced separately in
1 Kings 7, with a different parentage and a different role.

The Masonic figure of Hiram Abiff descends from the second. Chapter 22 traces how,
and Chapter 21 establishes who the second man was.
