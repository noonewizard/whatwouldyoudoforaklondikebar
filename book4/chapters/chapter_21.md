# CHAPTER TWENTY-ONE
## Hiram the Craftsman

He appears in two books, with two genealogies that do not match, and he does not
die.

---

### The Kings account

*And king Solomon sent and fetched Hiram out of Tyre. He was a widow's son of the
tribe of Naphtali, and his father was a man of Tyre, a worker in brass: and he was
filled with wisdom, and understanding, and cunning to work all works in
brass.*[^1]

Four things in one verse, and each matters.

**He is a widow's son.** A specific biographical detail, not a formula.

**He is of mixed parentage** — Israelite mother, Tyrian father — which places him
exactly at the cultural join the project requires.

**He is a bronzeworker**, and in Kings that is the whole of his craft. He casts the
pillars, the sea, the stands, the basins, the pots and shovels — an extensive
inventory of bronze, and nothing else.

**And he is described in the language of wisdom.** *Chokhmah*, *tevunah*,
*da'at* — the same vocabulary the text uses for Solomon's own gift and for
Bezalel's in Exodus. **Craft skill is presented as a species of wisdom**, not as a
lower faculty, and this is a genuine feature of the biblical treatment of
craftsmanship that the Masonic tradition later leaned on with some justification.

---

### The Chronicles account, and the discrepancy

2 Chronicles tells it differently, and the differences are not trivial.

**The name is Huram-abi.**[^2] **The mother is of the daughters of Dan**, not
Naphtali. **And the craft list expands enormously**: gold, silver, bronze, iron,
stone, timber, purple, blue, fine linen, crimson — and the ability to engrave and
to devise any device.

So: a different tribe, a different name-form, and a craftsman who has grown from a
bronze specialist into a universal artificer.

**This book reports the discrepancy rather than harmonizing it.** Harmonisations
exist — a mother of Danite origin married into Naphtali, or the reverse — and they
are possible. They are also exactly the kind of move this book's method is
suspicious of, since a harmonization that cannot be tested is a hypothesis dressed
as a reconciliation.

**The more economical reading is that Chronicles is doing something.** Its author,
writing later and with his own purposes, has assimilated the Temple's craftsman to
the model of Bezalel, the divinely gifted artificer of the Tabernacle in Exodus —
whose skills are similarly comprehensive and who is likewise filled with the
spirit of wisdom. The expansion of the craft list and the shift in tribe both move
Hiram toward that template.[^3]

**Which means the universal master craftsman is already a development within the
biblical material itself**, before any Masonic use of him at all. That is worth
knowing before Chapter 22.

---

### "Abi"

The philology, because the Masonic name comes from here and the derivation is
usually asserted rather than shown.

Chronicles gives **חורם אבי** — *Huram abi*. The second element is the ordinary
Hebrew word *av*, "father," with a first-person singular possessive suffix: **"my
father."**

In context it functions as **a title of honor or respect**, a recognized usage in
which a senior craftsman, counselor or official is called "father" by a superior
or a community. Joseph is called a father to Pharaoh; the usage is not unusual.[^4]

**So "Huram-abi" most economically reads as "Huram my master" or "Huram my
counselor" — a designation, not a surname.**

2 Chronicles 4:16 gives a related form, *Huram aviv*, "Huram his father," which
points the same way.

**How a title of address became a proper name is Chapter 22's subject**, and the
answer involves translators rather than conspirators.

---

### And he does not die

The most important fact in this chapter, and the easiest to overlook because it is
an absence.

**There is no murder.** No conspiracy, no refusal, no confrontation at the
entrances, no burial, no search, no raising. Hiram completes his work — the text
inventories it at length — and the narrative moves on to the Temple's dedication.

Nothing in Kings or Chronicles says what became of him.

**Every element of the Masonic third-degree legend beyond the name and the craft
is supplied from outside the Bible**, and Chapters 22 and 23 trace where from. The
biblical Hiram is a skilled foreigner who did an excellent job and went home, or
stayed, or died in bed. The text does not care, because the text is interested in
the bronze.
