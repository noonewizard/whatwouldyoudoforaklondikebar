# CHAPTER TWENTY-THREE
## The Three Ruffians

Three workmen want something the master will not give. They confront him at three
entrances in turn. He refuses each. The third kills him.

**None of this is in the Bible**, and this chapter is about where it came from and
what it is doing.

---

### The shape of the story

Strip the Masonic specifics and the narrative structure is bare and familiar:

**A threefold escalation.** Three confrontations, at three places, in sequence,
with the third fatal. Threefold repetition with variation on the third instance is
among the most widespread narrative structures in world storytelling — the folklore
literature treats it as a near-universal — and it requires no source because it is
how stories are told.[^1]

**A refusal under pressure.** The master is offered the chance to save himself and
declines.

**A concealment and a search.** The body is hidden; a party goes out; it is found.

**And a recovery that is not a resurrection.** The body is raised, and the raising
is the degree's climax — but the word is not recovered, and a substitute is
supplied instead.

**That last element is the one without an obvious parallel**, and the preceding
volume argued at length that it is the system's actual content: a ceremony whose
climax is an announcement of unavailability.

---

### The comparative claims

From the nineteenth century onward a large literature has identified Hiram Abiff
with dying-and-rising figures: Osiris above all, and also Tammuz, Adonis, Attis,
Mithras, Dionysus, Baldr, and Christ.

The master prompt for this volume is explicit that such identifications must be
demonstrated rather than asserted, and it is right. Apply the transmission
protocol this series has used throughout.

**Chronology**: passes trivially. All the candidates are older.

**Geographical contact**: not demonstrated for the specific case. The question is
not whether eighteenth-century Englishmen could have encountered classical
mythology — they could and did — but whether the composers of this degree drew on
it, which is a different claim requiring different evidence.

**Verbal or textual dependence**: **fails**. There is no distinctive shared
vocabulary, no quotation, no idiosyncratic detail crossing from any of the
proposed sources into the Masonic material. The names are biblical. The setting is
biblical. The tools are a working mason's.

**Institutional continuity**: fails. No organisation persisted.

**And the comparison is weak on its own terms.** Several of the standard
"dying-and-rising gods" have been argued not to die, or not to rise, or to do
neither in the primary sources as opposed to in the summaries. The category itself
was assembled by a method — Frazer's — that the discipline has substantially
abandoned, and the preceding volume noted the caution.[^2]

**Verdict: E5 to E6.** Not demonstrated; and the resemblance is to a narrative
shape so common that its recurrence carries little information.

---

### What the story is for, structurally

A more useful question than where it came from.

**The degree needed a reason for a word to be unavailable.** That is the design
problem the preceding volume identified: two degrees already conferred, a third
that merely conferred more would be redundant, and a third that withheld needed
an in-story explanation for the withholding.

**A murdered master supplies the cheapest complete one.** Not forgotten, not
mislaid, not reserved — ended with a person. Nothing more is required, and nothing
else in the plot needs explaining once that is in place.

**Which suggests the murder exists because the loss was wanted**, rather than the
loss following from a murder imported from elsewhere. The plot serves the
structure.

---

### The ruffians themselves

A note on the three, and on how little can be said.

They are named in Masonic ritual, and the names vary between jurisdictions and
periods — which is itself informative. **A stable transmitted tradition does not
vary in its proper names.** Variation of this kind is the signature of material
carried orally and locally, revised in transmission, which is what the
documentary record independently indicates.

**And the element-by-element dating remains a declared gap in this study**, as
Chapter 22 stated. The general 1720s horizon is secure. The individual components'
print debuts have not been systematically traced here, and the chapter says so
rather than implying a precision it has not earned.

---

### The honest summary

**A legend assembled in the 1720s, from biblical names, an existing Temple
setting, a near-universal narrative structure, and a design requirement that
something be unavailable at the end.**

That is less exotic than Osiris and it is what the evidence supports. It is also,
as the preceding volume argued and this one has no reason to revise, not a
deflation: a thing assembled from ordinary materials has been performed hundreds of
thousands of times across three centuries and has reliably affected the people it
was performed on.

**Being able to date something is not the same as having explained it away.**
