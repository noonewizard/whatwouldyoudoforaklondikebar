# CHAPTER TWENTY-FOUR
## The Master Builder

Why a builder?

Most initiatory traditions centre a priest, a king, a prophet, or a god. Masonry
centres a man who worked in bronze, and the choice is not obvious.

---

### The operative answer

The dullest explanation is substantially correct and should be stated first.

**Freemasonry descends, institutionally and nominally, from stonemasons' lodges.**
The Scottish evidence of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries — the Schaw
Statutes, the lodge minutes — concerns actual working masons organising an actual
trade. The Old Charges are craft documents with a craft legendary history.

**A trade's hero is a tradesman.** Guild traditions across medieval Europe
attached themselves to patron figures and legendary founders drawn from their own
occupation. A masons' tradition centring a master builder is not mysterious; it is
what one would expect, and the surprise would be the reverse.

That covers the *choice*. It does not cover what the figure became.

---

### What a builder makes available

Four things the figure of a master builder supplies that a priest or a king does
not, and this is why the choice turned out to be so productive.

**Competence that is checkable.** A builder's work stands or falls — literally. The
preceding volume made this point about ritual competence and it applies with more
force to craft: a badly cut stone is visibly badly cut, and no authority can
declare otherwise. **A tradition organised around a craftsman inherits a model of
knowledge in which claims are testable by their results**, which is an unusual and
valuable thing for a symbolic system to have at its centre.

**Knowledge that is transmitted by apprenticeship.** Craft skill cannot be
acquired from a book; it must be shown, by a person, to a person, over time. This
is tacit knowledge in Polanyi's sense, and the preceding volume argued that it is
precisely what Masonic ritual actually transmits and why publication of the
ceremonies did not destroy the institution.[^1]

**Work that is never finished.** A building under construction is the natural
emblem of an incomplete process, and Chapter 89 develops this. A priest's function
is complete each time he performs it; a builder's is complete only when the
building is, and the Temple in Masonic symbolism is never finished.

**And a death that removes something irreplaceable.** If knowledge lives in a
person rather than a text, then the death of the person is a real loss of
knowledge. **This is the feature the third degree exploits**, and it is available
because the figure is a craftsman. A murdered scribe leaves his writings. A
murdered master takes his hands with him.

---

### Succession as the actual subject

Which points at what the legend is really about, underneath the murder.

**The problem the third degree dramatises is the transmission of authority across
a break.**

A master dies without passing on what only he held. The work must continue. The
institution has to decide how to proceed without the thing it has lost — and what
it does is adopt a declared substitute and carry on, which Chapter 17 showed is
also what a community does when the Temple falls and the Name can no longer be
spoken.

**That is a succession crisis rendered as a story**, and succession crises are the
ordinary business of institutions rather than an exotic mystery. Every guild,
every priesthood, every dynasty, every partnership faces the question of what
happens when the person who knows dies.

Masonry's answer, embedded in its central ceremony, is unusually honest: **you do
not get the thing back; you get a placeholder, and you are told it is one, and the
work continues.**

---

### The builder and the Temple

The two halves of this book's title meet here.

**The Temple supplies the setting that makes the builder legible.** A master
craftsman murdered on an ordinary building site is a crime story. The same figure
at the construction of the Temple is something else, because the building he is
working on is the one the entire Western tradition treats as the paradigm of what
building can mean.

**And the builder supplies the human scale the Temple otherwise lacks.** Chapter 3
established that the biblical description is an inventory — materials, dimensions,
gold. It has no people in it to speak of. The legend puts a man inside the
inventory, and gives the reader somebody to be.

**Neither element works as well alone**, which may be the best available
explanation for why this particular combination, assembled by unknown hands in the
1720s, has outlasted every competitor produced in the same period. The French high
degrees of the following decades generated hundreds of alternatives. Very few
survive. This one does.
