# CHAPTER TWENTY-SIX
## The Seal of Solomon

A six-pointed star made of two interlaced triangles has meant a great many
different things, and it has not meant them continuously.

This chapter is about how a shape acquires meanings — and about why a shape is
unusually bad evidence for a transmission claim.

---

### The shape's problem

**A hexagram is easy to draw and easy to arrive at independently.**

Two overlapping equilateral triangles; or the natural figure produced by extending
the sides of a regular hexagon; or what results from a compass-and-straightedge
exercise any apprentice performs. It appears as decoration in cultures with no
contact, in periods with no connection, on objects with no religious function at
all.

**The half-of-everything rule applies with force.** When a motif is this easy to
generate, its appearance in two places is not evidence that one place learned it
from the other. It is evidence that people draw with compasses.

This is the chapter's governing caution, and it disposes of most of what is
claimed for the figure.

---

### The uses, kept apart

**Ancient and late-antique decorative use.** Hexagrams appear on objects and in
architectural ornament across the ancient Mediterranean and Near East without any
demonstrable consistent meaning. Some are decorative. Some may be apotropaic. The
evidence rarely permits deciding which.

**Magical use — and here the name enters.** In late-antique and medieval magical
literature, both Jewish and Islamic, a "seal of Solomon" appears as a device of
binding and protection, consistent with the Testament of Solomon's ring. **In much
of this material the figure so named is a pentagram, not a hexagram**, and the
usage is unstable. The name attaches to more than one shape.

**Islamic use.** *Khātam Sulaymān*, the seal of Solomon, is a recognized motif in
Islamic art and architecture, appearing decoratively and in talismanic contexts.
Solomon (Sulaymān) is a prophet in Islam with a substantial legendary tradition of
command over jinn and animals, grounded in the Qur'an.[^1]

**Jewish use as a communal emblem.** The hexagram's adoption as a specifically
Jewish identifying symbol — the *Magen David*, Shield of David — is **late**. Its
use in this role becomes established in the early modern period, notably in
Prague, and it acquires its modern near-universal status as the emblem of Jewish
communal identity in the nineteenth century, in the context of emancipation and
the search for a symbol comparable to the cross.[^2]

**This is the point most often got wrong**, in both friendly and hostile
literature: the Star of David is not an ancient Jewish symbol of continuous use.
Its career as the emblem of a people is a few centuries old.

**Renaissance and modern occult use.** Absorbed into the correspondence systems of
Western esotericism, assigned meanings within those systems, and — from the
nineteenth century — given the interpretations most modern readers have
encountered.

**And Masonic use**, where the hexagram appears in some contexts and degrees, with
explanations that vary by jurisdiction and period, which by the disagreement
principle indicates a symbol that arrived without a settled meaning.

---

### What the sequence shows

**Six uses, none continuous with the others, connected by a name rather than by a
tradition.**

The name "Seal of Solomon" is doing the work. It is applied, in different periods
and communities, to different shapes with different functions, and its application
in one context is not evidence of derivation from another.

**A symbol with a stable name and an unstable meaning is the signature of
independent adoption**, not of transmission. The preceding volume established the
principle with the Masonic letter G; the hexagram is the same phenomenon at larger
scale.

---

### The inference this chapter forbids

*The hexagram appears in ancient magical contexts; the hexagram appears in modern
Jewish and Masonic contexts; therefore a magical tradition has been transmitted.*

**Every step of that is separately unsupported.** The ancient usage is unstable in
both shape and meaning. The Jewish communal use is early modern and arises for
reasons of identity rather than magic. The Masonic use is variable and late.

**And the shape is too easy to draw for its recurrence to carry information.**

What can be said, and it is the whole of it: **the name of Solomon has been
attached to protective and binding devices for a very long time, across several
religious traditions, and the devices so named have not been the same device.**
