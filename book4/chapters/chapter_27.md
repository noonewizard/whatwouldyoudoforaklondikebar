# CHAPTER TWENTY-SEVEN
## The Key of Solomon

There is no book called *The Key of Solomon*. There is a family of manuscripts,
copied across several centuries in several languages, which share a title, share
some contents, and disagree about the rest. Treating that family as a book — as a
single work with an author, a date, and a doctrine — is the first and most
consequential error a reader can make with this material.

This chapter is about the family, and about the mechanism that produced it.

---

### What actually survives

The Latin *Clavicula Salomonis* is attested in manuscript from roughly the
fifteenth century onward, with the great bulk of surviving copies dating from the
sixteenth to the eighteenth. There are French, Italian, Latin, English, and
Hebrew versions. They are not translations of one another in any clean sense.
Chapters appear and vanish; the sequence of operations differs; the pantheon of
spirit names differs; the instructions for preparing instruments differ in ways
that would matter enormously to anyone actually trying to follow them.

Behind the Latin tradition lies a Byzantine Greek one — the so-called
*Hygromanteia* or *Magical Treatise of Solomon* — which shares structural
features with the later Latin material: planetary hours, prepared instruments,
conjurations of named spirits. The relationship between the Greek and Latin
families is a live scholarly question rather than a settled one, and this book
does not need it settled. What matters is the direction of the arrow: the
tradition is late antique and medieval, and it grows.

The nineteenth-century English text most readers encounter is S. L. MacGregor
Mathers's *The Key of Solomon the King*, published in 1889 and compiled from
manuscripts then held in the British Museum — chiefly French and Latin copies of
the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Mathers produced an edited composite.
He said so. Readers have persistently forgotten that he said so.

The *Lemegeton*, or *Lesser Key of Solomon*, is a separate seventeenth-century
compilation of five originally independent texts, of which the first, the
*Goetia*, is by far the best known. Its catalog of seventy-two spirits is
closely related to the list in Johann Weyer's *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*,
appended to his *De praestigiis daemonum* (1563; the demon-list appears in later
editions from 1577). Weyer was a physician arguing that accused witches were ill
rather than guilty, and he printed the list partly to expose it. A skeptic's
exhibit became a practitioner's directory. This is a recurring pattern in the
history of magic and worth naming: **publication for refutation is publication.**

There is also the *Ars Notoria*, a thirteenth-century text attributed to Solomon
that is not a spirit-commanding manual at all. It is a system of prayers,
invocations, and diagrams for acquiring knowledge — grammar, rhetoric,
philosophy — rapidly and without study. It matters here because it shows that
"Solomonic" was not a synonym for "demonic." It was a synonym for **knowledge
granted rather than earned**.

---

### Why Solomon

Attribution in the medieval magical tradition is not a claim about authorship. It
is a claim about warrant. The question a copyist faces is not "who wrote this?"
but "on whose authority may this be used?"

Solomon was an extraordinarily good answer, for four reasons that compound.

**First, the scriptural grant is explicit and it is about knowledge of the
natural world.** 1 Kings 4:29–34 gives Solomon wisdom "and largeness of heart,
even as the sand that is on the sea shore," and then specifies its content: he
spoke of trees, of beasts, of fowl, of creeping things, and of fishes. This is a
description of comprehensive natural knowledge. It says nothing about command. But
it establishes that Solomon's knowledge was *given*, not acquired — which is
precisely the claim a magical text needs to make about itself.

**Second, the deuterocanonical Wisdom of Solomon extends the inventory.** Its
seventh chapter has Solomon receive knowledge of "the constitution of the world,
and the operation of the elements," of "the circuits of years and the positions of
stars," of "the natures of living creatures, and the furies of wild beasts," of
"the violences of winds, and the reasonings of men," of "the diversities of
plants, and the virtues of roots." A reader looking for scriptural warrant for a
book of correspondences between hours, planets, plants, and spirits will find
this passage extremely convenient. It is not about magic. It is about wisdom as
encyclopedic penetration of a created order. But it is one short step from there
to a book organized by planetary hours.

**Third, Solomon is a king, and therefore a commander.** The grimoire operator's
posture toward spirits is juridical: he binds, compels, licenses to depart. That
posture requires a legitimating precedent of someone who legitimately commanded
non-human beings. Chapter 25 traced how that precedent was assembled.

**Fourth — and this is the one most often missed — attribution was protective.**
To copy a book of conjurations in fifteenth-century Europe was dangerous. The
theology faculty of the University of Paris condemned a list of magical
propositions in 1398; the *Speculum astronomiae* attributed to Albertus Magnus had
already, in the thirteenth century, named books circulating under Solomon's name
among the necromantic works a Christian should reject. Under that pressure, a
title page reading *Clavicula Salomonis* does two things at once. It borrows the
authority of a biblical king, and it declines to name a living man.

Pseudepigraphy, here, is not fraud in the modern sense. It is the ordinary
grammar of textual authority in a world where authority is inherited rather than
argued.

### What follows for our question

Three things follow, and they will all be load-bearing in Chapter 29.

The first is chronological. Every text in this family is medieval or later. The
Greek material is late antique at the earliest. Nothing in it is contemporary
with, or close to, the period in which a Jerusalem temple was built.

The second concerns direction. These texts invoke Solomon. They do not transmit
Solomon. The traffic runs from the legend to the manual, not from any practice of
Solomon's to the manual — and we have no evidence of the latter to run.

The third concerns the Temple. For all that these books bear Solomon's name, the
Temple itself plays a remarkably small role in them. The circles are drawn on the
ground, in a solitary place, at a chosen hour. The operative geography of the
Solomonic grimoire is not architectural. It is portable.

That is an awkward fact for the inference this Part is building toward, and it is
one we will hold onto.
