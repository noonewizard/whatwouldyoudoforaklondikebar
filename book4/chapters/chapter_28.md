# CHAPTER TWENTY-EIGHT
## Solomon and the Spirits

Most confusion about Solomon and magic comes from a single avoidable mistake:
treating four different Solomons as one man.

They have the same name. They do not have the same evidentiary status, the same
date, or the same function. Collapsing them produces sentences that sound like
history and are not. Keeping them apart costs nothing and clarifies almost
everything.

---

### Solomon One: the historical figure

What can be said about a tenth-century BCE Judahite king named Solomon was set
out in Part II and will not be re-argued. The short form: a monarch of that name
is plausible; the scale and splendour attributed to him by the biblical text are
disputed, sometimes sharply; the archaeological record for a tenth-century
Jerusalem of imperial scope is thin and contested; and no extrabiblical text
names him. He is a figure we approach through a narrative composed centuries
after him.

**He has no attested connection to magic of any kind.** Not a hostile one, not a
favourable one. The category does not arise.

### Solomon Two: the biblical and legendary wisdom king

This Solomon is a literary character with an extensive afterlife. He is wise, he
judges between two mothers, he answers the Queen of Sheba's hard questions, he
speaks of trees and beasts, he builds. Later Jewish and Christian tradition
elaborates him steadily.

The elaboration is where the spirits arrive — and it arrives, importantly, in
answer to a question the biblical text itself raised.

1 Kings 6:7 says the house was built of stone made ready before it was brought
there, "so that there was neither hammer nor axe nor any tool of iron heard in
the house, while it was in building." Read plainly, this is a statement about the
quietness and sanctity of the site: the noisy work happened at the quarry. But it
is the kind of detail that generates questions. *How* was the stone cut, if not
with iron?

The Babylonian Talmud supplies an answer. In the tractate Gittin (68a–b) — and
with related material in Sotah — Solomon needs the *shamir*, a creature or
substance capable of cutting stone without iron, and to obtain it he must first
capture and bind the demon Ashmedai. The narrative is elaborate, comic in places,
and ends badly for Solomon, who is displaced from his throne by the demon he
captured. It is not a manual. It is aggadah: exegetical storytelling that
explains a textual peculiarity by narrating around it.

This matters more than almost anything else in this Part, so it is worth stating
flatly. **The demonic material attached to the Temple's construction did not
arrive as a memory of how the Temple was built. It arrived as an explanation of a
verse about how it was not built.**

### Solomon Three: the Solomon of the magical manuals

This is the Solomon of the *Testament of Solomon*, the Greek *Hygromanteia*, the
*Clavicula*, and the *Lemegeton*. He possesses a ring, he commands by name, he
binds and licenses. Chapters 25 and 27 traced the sources.

Two features distinguish him sharply from Solomon Two. He is **operative** — the
texts exist to be used, not merely read. And he is **transferable** — the whole
point of a manual is that its procedures work for someone who is not Solomon.
Legend makes Solomon exceptional; the grimoire makes him replicable.

There is a parallel and partly independent Islamic tradition of Sulaymān, whose
Qur'anic portrait includes command over jinn: in Sūrat Sabaʾ (34:12–13) they work
for him, making what he wills of chambers, statues, basins and fixed cauldrons;
in Sūrat al-Anbiyāʾ (21:81–82) devils dive for him; in Sūrat al-Naml (27) his
hosts comprise jinn, men and birds. Later Islamic magical literature develops this
extensively — the seal or ring of Sulaymān, the *khātam*, is a standing element of
it. This tradition is not a branch of the Latin grimoires and should not be
described as one; it is a substantial parallel development from overlapping
scriptural raw material, with its own chronology and its own internal debates
about legitimacy.

### Solomon Four: the modern occult Solomon

From the late nineteenth century, Solomonic material is edited, printed, and
incorporated into new systems: Mathers's *Key* (1889), the Mathers–Crowley
*Goetia* (1904), and the wider absorption of grimoire material into
ceremonial-magical orders and, much later, into popular occultism and games,
films, and fiction.

Solomon Four is not a continuation of Solomon Three. He is a **reception** of it,
mediated by printing, by translation choices, by editorial compositing, and by
the theoretical commitments of the editors — who generally believed they were
recovering a coherent ancient system, and who edited accordingly.

### Why the distinction does work

Keep the four apart and a set of otherwise confusing claims resolve cleanly.

*"Solomon was a magician."* Of Solomon Three and Four: true by definition, since
that is what those figures are. Of Solomon Two: partly, in late and specific
strands. Of Solomon One: unsupported, and not merely unproven — there is no
evidence to weigh in either direction.

*"There is an ancient Solomonic magical tradition."* Ancient depends on the
Solomon. The *Testament* is plausibly third- to fifth-century CE in its surviving
form; Josephus attests first-century practice attributing itself to Solomon. That
is genuinely old. It is also a thousand years after Solomon One.

*"The grimoires preserve Temple secrets."* This requires a chain from Solomon One
to Solomon Three. No such chain has been demonstrated, and Chapter 29 will show
what the evidence actually indicates instead.

*"Freemasonry inherited Solomonic magic."* Chronologically this would require
Masonic bodies to have drawn on Solomon Three or Four. Part VII takes up what
Masonic documents actually say about Solomon, and the answer will surprise
readers who have been told otherwise in both directions.

### A note on tone

It is easy to write about grimoires in one of two registers: the credulous, which
treats them as survivals of a lost science, or the dismissive, which treats them
as superstition and moves on. Both registers make the same mistake. They fail to
ask what the texts were *for*.

These books were copied, at real personal risk, by people who wanted something —
knowledge, protection, treasure, love, relief from fear, mastery over a world that
mastered them. The demons of the *Goetia* teach astronomy and rhetoric and reveal
hidden things; they are, among other things, an inventory of what a person in that
world lacked and wished for. Read that way, the Solomonic corpus is a remarkably
direct document of human desire.

That is a historical claim, and it can be supported. It requires no position
whatsoever on whether anything answered when the circle was drawn.
