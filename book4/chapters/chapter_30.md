# PART VII — FROM TEMPLE TO FREEMASONRY

# CHAPTER THIRTY
## The Temple Enters Masonry

Ask when Solomon's Temple became central to Freemasonry and you will get two
confident answers. One says it was always central, because Freemasonry descends
from the builders of the Temple. The other says it was invented in the eighteenth
century by gentlemen playing at antiquity.

Both are wrong, and they are wrong in an instructive way: each takes a single
snapshot of a long process and calls it the whole.

This chapter does what neither does. It lays the documents out in order and asks
what each one actually says.

---

### The rule we are following

A document is evidence for the period in which it was written. That is the rule of
direction, and it has governed every chapter of this book. A 1723 text describing
events of 950 BCE is a 1723 text. It tells us, reliably, what people in 1723
believed and wished to say. It tells us nothing directly about 950 BCE.

Applied here, the rule produces a question with a real answer: *when does the
Temple start appearing in craft documents, and how much weight does it carry when
it does?*

---

### The earliest craft documents

**The Regius Poem** (British Library, Royal MS 17 A I), conventionally dated to
around 1390, though a date in the earlier fifteenth century has also been argued,
is the oldest known document of the English masonic tradition. It is a poem of
moral and practical instruction in rhymed couplets, and it contains a legendary
history of the craft.

That history does not begin with Solomon. It runs through Euclid, geometry, and
Egypt, and arrives in England under King Athelstan. Solomon is not its organizing
figure. The Temple is not its founding event.

**The Cooke Manuscript** (British Library, Additional MS 23198), dated to roughly
1410–1450, is longer and gives a fuller legendary history — and here the Temple
does appear. Cooke's narrative runs from the children of Lamech through Nimrod and
Abraham to Euclid, and includes Solomon's Temple, with a master mason described as
the son of the King of Tyre. Solomon is present, and the Temple is a station on
the route.

But notice its position. It is one episode in a chain, and the chain's weight
falls elsewhere — on Euclid and geometry as the craft's intellectual patrimony,
and on the transmission of the craft to England. The Temple is in the story. It is
not yet the story.

**The later Old Charges**, of which well over a hundred versions survive from the
late sixteenth century onward — the Grand Lodge MS No. 1 of 1583 is the earliest
dated example — reproduce and vary this legendary history. Solomon's Temple
remains a station. The pattern is stable across a remarkable span of copying: the
craft's dignity is founded on geometry, its antiquity on a chain of patrons, and
its arrival in England on Athelstan.

**The Scottish material** runs on a partly separate track. The Schaw Statutes of
1598 and 1599, issued under William Schaw as Master of Work to the Scottish crown,
regulate lodges as working institutions. They are administrative documents, and
what they regulate is a trade. The earliest known Scottish catechisms — the
Edinburgh Register House Manuscript of 1696 and the closely related texts that
follow it — record question-and-answer material associated with the Mason Word.
This is the first documentary foothold for anything resembling a ritual of
recognition.

---

### What the chronology shows

Set out plainly, the sequence is this.

*By the early fifteenth century*, Solomon and the Temple are present in the
craft's legendary history, as one episode among several, in a narrative whose
organizing principle is geometry.

*Through the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries*, that legendary history is
copied and varied without fundamental reorganization, while in Scotland the lodge
develops as an institution with its own recognition practices.

*In the 1720s and 1730s*, something changes in scale. Anderson's *Constitutions*
of 1723 gives the Temple a treatment far more extended than anything in the Old
Charges, and Samuel Prichard's *Masonry Dissected* of 1730 — whatever its motives,
and they were not friendly — prints a three-degree system in which a legend set at
the Temple's construction occupies the culminating position.

*Across the remainder of the eighteenth century*, the Temple's role expands
further: in the lodge's symbolic furniture, in the elaboration of additional
degrees, and in the Royal Arch, referenced in Irish sources by the 1740s.

The honest summary is neither "always" nor "invented." It is **present early,
peripheral for three centuries, and elevated to centrality in a
documentable forty-year window.**

---

### Why this matters more than it looks

Two consequences follow, and both cut against popular accounts in both
directions.

**Against descent.** If Freemasonry descended from the Temple's builders, the
Temple would not be a late-arriving emphasis in its own documents. The earliest
craft texts would foreground it. They do not. They foreground Euclid.

**Against pure invention.** If the Temple were an eighteenth-century fabrication,
it would not appear in a manuscript of around 1410. It does. Anderson did not
invent the association. He inherited it, and he amplified it enormously.

There is a third consequence, less often noticed and more interesting than
either. Something happened in the early eighteenth century that made a
construction legend the right vehicle for what Freemasonry was becoming. That is
not a question about origins at all. It is a question about *fit* — and the
remaining chapters of this Part, and then the whole of Part IX, are about what
that fit consisted in.

---

### A standing caution

Everything above concerns English and Scottish documents, and after 1717 chiefly
those of one jurisdiction among several. The Grand Lodge of Ireland (1725), the
Grand Lodge of Scotland (1736), the rival Antients Grand Lodge (1751), and the
Continental jurisdictions that developed from the 1730s onward did not move in
lockstep, and their ritual and symbolic emphases diverged, sometimes sharply.

No account of "when the Temple entered Masonry" is true of Freemasonry as such.
Freemasonry as such has never existed as a single body with a single ritual. Where
this book says something about Masonic practice, it will say whose.
