# CHAPTER THIRTY-ONE
## Anderson

James Anderson's *The Constitutions of the Free-Masons*, published in London in
1723 and substantially revised in 1738, is the most consequential document in the
history of the Masonic Temple. It is also one of the most misread.

It is misread in both directions. Defenders have treated its historical narrative
as history. Critics have treated it as fabrication, and stopped there. Neither
reading asks the question that makes the document interesting: what kind of text
did Anderson think he was writing?

---

### What the book contains

The 1723 *Constitutions* is not primarily a history. It is a constitutional
document: it contains the Charges of a Free-Mason, general regulations for the
governance of lodges, and songs. The historical narrative is a preface — a
traditional history placed at the front, as the Old Charges had always placed
one.

That narrative runs from Adam, through the antediluvian patriarchs, Noah, the
builders of Babel, Egypt, Abraham, Moses, and so to Solomon — and Solomon's Temple
receives a treatment out of all proportion to anything in the earlier craft
documents. Anderson then carries the line forward through the architecture of
Greece and Rome, the Gothic period, and the revival of classical building in
England, arriving at his own present and the newly organized Grand Lodge.

The 1738 second edition expands the history considerably, adds material on the
Grand Lodge's own early years, and revises some of the Charges. Where the two
editions differ, they must be cited separately; running them together is a common
and avoidable error.

---

### The genre question

Anderson was a Presbyterian minister with scholarly pretensions and a taste for
genealogy. His historical preface behaves the way such prefaces behaved in his
period: it establishes the dignity of an institution by placing it in a line of
descent.

This is a recognizable literary form with recognizable conventions, and modern
readers consistently apply the wrong standard to it. Guild and corporate
traditional histories of the era routinely traced their crafts to biblical or
classical founders. A company of smiths might claim Tubal-cain; a company of
weavers, some suitably ancient patron. Nobody involved understood these claims as
archival findings, and nobody was deceived in the way a modern reader imagines.
The function was honorific.

Anderson's innovation was not the form. It was the scale, the fluency, and — this
matters — the integration. He took a chain of stations and made it a continuous
narrative with a governing idea: that the craft of building is the transmission of
geometry, that geometry is a divine science, and that the Temple is its supreme
historical realization.

That last move is the hinge of this book. In the Old Charges, geometry is primary
and the Temple is a station. In Anderson, **the Temple becomes the place where
geometry was most fully expressed** — and therefore the natural center of a craft
that understands itself as geometry's inheritor.

---

### What Anderson did with the Temple

Three specific things, each with consequences.

**He gave it scale.** The building receives sustained descriptive attention,
drawn largely from the biblical accounts and from contemporary reconstructive
scholarship, of which the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries had produced
a great deal. Anderson's Temple is not a name in a list. It is a building the
reader is invited to picture.

**He gave it a workforce.** The organization of the Temple's builders — the
numbers, the grades, the officers — is elaborated in a way that makes the Temple's
construction site look structurally like a Masonic institution. Whether Anderson
intended the resemblance as retrojection or as recognition is not recoverable from
the text. The effect is the same either way: a reader in 1723 encountering both
descriptions could hardly fail to see one in the other.

**He placed it at the center of a chain that leads to the reader.** This is the
decisive rhetorical act. The Temple is not merely described; it is described as
*ours*.

---

### What Anderson did not do

He did not invent the association between Masonry and Solomon. The Cooke
Manuscript predates him by some three centuries.

He did not, in the 1723 *Constitutions*, give the reader the legend of the murder
of the Temple's chief architect. Whatever the state of that legend in lodge
practice at the time — and the evidence is genuinely thin — it is not the
*Constitutions'* subject. The earliest widely circulated printed account of a
three-degree system containing it is Prichard's *Masonry Dissected* in 1730, a
hostile exposure, and the relationship between that publication and what lodges
were actually doing in the 1720s remains a live historiographical problem rather
than a settled one.

And he did not produce a document that his own institution regarded as beyond
revision. The 1738 edition revises him; later jurisdictions revised him further,
and the American, Irish, Scottish, and Continental traditions each developed their
own constitutional texts. Treating the 1723 *Constitutions* as Freemasonry's
scripture misrepresents how Masonic bodies have actually used it.

---

### The honest assessment

Anderson's history is not reliable as history, and no serious Masonic scholar of
the last century and a half has claimed otherwise. Masonic historiography itself
produced the critique: the "authentic school" associated with the transactions of
Quatuor Coronati Lodge, from the 1880s onward, subjected the traditional history
to precisely the kind of documentary scrutiny this book applies, and found it
wanting. That is worth stating clearly, because the assumption that Freemasonry
has protected its origin myth from examination is simply false. Freemasons
dismantled it.

But unreliable as history is not the same as worthless as evidence. As evidence
for what a particular group of men in London in 1723 wanted their institution to
be, the *Constitutions* is a first-rate source — and what it shows them wanting is
specific.

They wanted an institution whose authority came from a science rather than a
creed. They wanted a lineage that crossed confessional boundaries in a country
that had spent the previous century tearing itself apart over them. And they
wanted a founding building that everyone in Christian and Jewish Europe already
revered, that no living church owned, and that no longer stood.

The last of those is not a small point. A Temple that still stood would have had
custodians, and custodians would have had opinions. A destroyed Temple was
available.
