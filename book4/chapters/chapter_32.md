# CHAPTER THIRTY-TWO
## The Old Charges

Behind Anderson stands a manuscript tradition, and it is a large one. Something
over a hundred versions of what Masonic scholarship calls the Old Charges survive,
ranging from the early fifteenth century to the eighteenth, in rolls, booklets,
and lodge minute books. They are the single best evidence we have for what the
English craft said about itself before 1717.

This chapter reads them for one thing: what they say about Solomon.

---

### The shape of the document

A typical Old Charge has three parts.

It opens with an **invocation** — in the great majority of pre-1723 copies, an
explicitly Trinitarian one, calling on the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost.
This is worth registering before anything else, because it fixes the documents'
religious setting. These are Christian texts produced in a Christian craft, and
the non-confessional formulation of Anderson's first Charge is a departure from
them, not a continuation.

It continues with a **legendary history** of the craft, tracing geometry and
masonry from antiquity to England.

It closes with the **charges** themselves: practical and moral obligations.
Be true to God and Holy Church. Be true to your master. Do not take work you
cannot perform. Do not supplant a fellow. Do not harbour thieves. Pay your
fellows truly. Call one another brother or fellow. These are the rules of a trade
that has to work with people it cannot supervise, and they are the least
mysterious and most consistently transmitted part of the whole corpus.

---

### Where Solomon appears

In the legendary history, and in a particular structural position.

The history's spine is geometry. It generally begins with the children of Lamech
and the preservation of the sciences on two pillars against flood and fire, passes
through Nimrod and the tower, reaches Abraham, and arrives at Euclid in Egypt, who
is credited with organizing the craft and giving it its charges. From Egypt the
craft travels — and Solomon's Temple is a station on that travel.

The Cooke Manuscript's version is the earliest substantial one. Solomon builds
the Temple; the King of Tyre sends him materials and a master mason, described as
the King of Tyre's son; and masons are confirmed in their charges there.

Two features of this passage deserve close attention.

**First, the Temple is a stage on a route, not a source.** The craft does not
begin at the Temple. It arrives there, already possessing its charges and its
geometry, from Egypt. Solomon employs masonry; he does not found it. This is the
exact reverse of the relationship that later popular accounts assume.

**Second, and more striking: the master mason's name is unstable.** Across the
Old Charges corpus the figure appears under a remarkable spread of names — forms
transmitted variously as Aynon, Aynone, Dyon, Ajuon, Benaim, and others still,
with copyists evidently working from exemplars they could not always read. The
variation was catalogued in detail by the twentieth century's documentary Masonic
scholars, Douglas Knoop and G. P. Jones among them, in their editions and studies
of the early manuscripts.

He is not, in these documents, called Hiram Abiff.

---

### What the unstable name tells us

A great deal, if we apply the disagreement principle carried from the preceding
volumes.

When sources agree closely on a name, the name was carried. When sources disagree
wildly on a name while agreeing on the role, **the role was carried and the name
was not.** What the Old Charges transmit is the position — there was a master
mason at the Temple, sent by the King of Tyre — and the name attached to that
position is textual noise accumulating over generations of copying.

This has a direct and important consequence. The figure who occupies the centre of
the third degree in the eighteenth century and after cannot be shown, on this
evidence, to descend from an operative tradition preserving his name. The name in
the operative documents is precisely the part that did not survive intact. Chapter
22 took up the name Hiram Abiff directly and in detail; what the Old Charges
establish here is what was and was not available to be inherited.

---

### What is absent

It is worth stating the absences as plainly as the presences, because the
absences are evidence.

There is no death of the master mason. No murder, no search, no recovery. The
narrative element that would later become Freemasonry's dramatic centre is not in
these documents.

There is no lost word.

There is no sustained architectural symbolism — no interpretation of the Temple's
dimensions, no doctrine of its pillars, no reading of its chambers as stages of an
inner ascent. The Old Charges describe a building being built and craftsmen being
charged.

There is no magic, no Kabbalah, no sacred geometry in the modern occult sense. The
geometry of the Old Charges is Euclid's: a practical and dignified science, the
foundation of the trade's claim to be a liberal art rather than mere labour.

---

### Reading the corpus honestly

The temptation with the Old Charges runs in two directions, and both should be
resisted.

The first is to mine them for hints — to read the two pillars of Lamech's children
as Jachin and Boaz in disguise, or the Euclid material as a coded transmission. The
texts do not support this, and the method that finds such things will find them in
any corpus large enough. Chapter 15 explained why.

The second is to dismiss them as a fanciful pedigree attached to a builders' union.
This underrates what they actually accomplished. For three centuries, a trade with
no central institution, no printing, and limited literacy maintained a shared
account of who its members were and what they owed one another, copied by hand,
read aloud at the making of a mason. That is a functioning tradition. It simply is
not the tradition the Temple legend was later taken to prove.

What the Old Charges give us, in the end, is a baseline. We know what the craft
said about Solomon before the eighteenth century, and we know how much of the
later structure was not yet there. Anything that appears afterward has to be
accounted for by what happened afterward — which is the subject of the next
chapter.
