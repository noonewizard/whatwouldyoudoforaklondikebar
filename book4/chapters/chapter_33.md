# CHAPTER THIRTY-THREE
## Eighteenth-Century Masonry

The century between 1717 and 1813 is when Freemasonry became the thing this book
is about. It acquired grand lodges, degrees, rivals, a literature, a continental
existence, and enemies. It also acquired the Temple as an organizing symbol rather
than a historical footnote.

This chapter is the institutional and documentary frame for that process. It is
deliberately dry. The interpretation comes afterward; the facts have to be on the
table first.

---

### Institutions

The **Premier Grand Lodge of England** is conventionally dated to 1717, when four
London lodges are said to have met at the Goose and Gridiron and constituted a
grand lodge. The evidence for this event is weaker than its prominence suggests:
the account comes from Anderson's 1738 *Constitutions*, twenty-one years after the
fact, and the Grand Lodge's own continuous records begin in 1723. Some historians
have argued that 1721, when the Duke of Montagu became Grand Master, marks the
better-documented beginning of the institution as an effective body. The 1717 date
is not fabricated; it is simply later-attested than a reader of most accounts
would assume.

The **Grand Lodge of Ireland** is attested from 1725, the **Grand Lodge of
Scotland** from 1736. Scottish lodges, it must be said, long predate their grand
lodge — some with continuous records running back into the seventeenth century,
which makes Scotland's documentary situation genuinely different from England's.

In 1751 a rival English body was constituted, calling itself the **Antients** and
its competitor the **Moderns** — a piece of polemical naming that has confused
students ever since, since the Antients were the newer organization. Their
Constitutions, Laurence Dermott's *Ahiman Rezon* (1756), argued that the Premier
Grand Lodge had innovated and that the Antients preserved older practice. The two
bodies competed for six decades before uniting in 1813 as the **United Grand Lodge
of England**, after which a Lodge of Reconciliation worked to harmonize the rival
ceremonies.

Every one of those bodies had its own practice. There was no period in the
eighteenth century at which "Masonic ritual" named one thing.

---

### The documentary record of ritual

We have three kinds of evidence, each with its own problems.

**Manuscript catechisms**, mostly Scottish and Irish, beginning with the Edinburgh
Register House Manuscript of 1696 and continuing through a small group of related
texts over the following two decades. These are the earliest direct evidence for
question-and-answer recognition material.

**Printed exposures**, beginning with short newspaper and pamphlet pieces in the
early 1720s and reaching their most consequential form in Samuel Prichard's
*Masonry Dissected* (1730), which went through many editions and was answered,
attacked, and pirated. Later in the century came *Three Distinct Knocks* (1760)
and *Jachin and Boaz* (1762), which were commercially successful and widely
reprinted.

**Lodge minutes, bylaws, and grand lodge records**, which say when and where
things happened and how much was spent, and are silent about content.

The exposures are the richest source and the most treacherous. They were written
to sell, often by men hostile to the Craft or expelled from it, and there is no
guarantee that any of them accurately records what any particular lodge did. What
gives them evidentiary weight is convergence: independent exposures, produced
across decades and jurisdictions, agree on broad structural features, and those
features are consistent with what later attested practice contains. Scholarship
treats them as usable with caution, and so does this book.

From these sources one structural fact emerges clearly. **A two-grade system gives
way to a three-degree system in the 1720s.** Prichard's 1730 text is the first
widely circulated printed account of three degrees, with a legend set at the
Temple occupying the third. The process by which this happened — whether the third
degree was elaborated from existing material, imported, or composed — is not
settled, and honest treatments say so.

---

### Expansion

The eighteenth century then multiplied degrees at a startling rate.

In the British Isles and Ireland, the **Royal Arch** appears in the documentary
record by the 1740s: a reference to a Royal Arch procession at Youghal in 1743 and
Fifield Dassigny's *Serious and Impartial Enquiry* of 1744 are the usual early
citations. The Antients incorporated it; the Moderns accommodated it through a
separate chapter organization from the 1760s. The Articles of Union of 1813 would
declare pure Antient Masonry to consist of three degrees including the Royal Arch
— a formula that settled a jurisdictional dispute rather than a historical
question.

On the Continent, Freemasonry arrived in the 1720s and 1730s and developed
differently. The Chevalier Ramsay's oration of 1736–37 proposed an origin among
crusading knights rather than operative builders — a claim with no documentary
support whatever, and enormous influence. From the 1750s the Rite of Strict
Observance, under Karl Gotthelf von Hund, asserted a descent from the Knights
Templar and a system of unknown superiors; the Convent of Wilhelmsbad in 1782
effectively repudiated that claim, which had by then already reshaped European
Masonry's self-image.

The number of degrees worked somewhere in Europe by the end of the century runs
into the hundreds.

---

### Opposition

Freemasonry acquired organized opposition early. Pope Clement XII's bull *In
eminenti apostolatus specula* of 1738 prohibited Catholic membership, and later
papal documents reaffirmed the prohibition. Secular authorities in several states
restricted or banned lodges at various points. The exposure literature was itself
a form of hostility, and the conspiracy literature that would follow the French
Revolution — the subject of Part XVI — was building its materials during this
period.

---

### What the frame shows

Three things, and they govern the rest of this Part.

**The Temple's rise is contemporaneous with institutional formation.** It is not a
survival that the new institutions inherited intact; it is an emphasis that grew
as the institutions grew.

**Diversity is the baseline, not the exception.** Anything said about Masonic use
of the Temple has to specify whose Masonry, when.

**The critical evidence is public.** Nothing in this chapter comes from a
disclosure. It comes from printed books, published constitutions, dated minutes,
and papal bulls — a paper trail that any reader can follow, and which has been
followed, with exceptional rigor, by Masonic historians themselves.
