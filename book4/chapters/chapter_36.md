# CHAPTER THIRTY-SIX
## East and West

Book III examined orientation as a general feature of ritual space. This chapter
does something narrower and, for this book's central question, more revealing: it
compares the lodge's east–west axis with the Temple's, and finds them pointing
opposite ways.

---

### The Temple's axis

The biblical description places the Temple's entrance on the east. One approached
from the east, passed between the pillars at the porch, entered the hall, and
proceeded west to the inner sanctuary — the Holy of Holies at the far western
end.

Ezekiel's visionary temple is explicit about the eastern gate and about the glory
of God entering by it. The directional logic is consistent across the accounts:
**the entrance is east, the most sacred point is west, and movement toward
holiness is movement westward.**

This arrangement is not unusual for the region. Levantine and Syrian temples of the
second and first millennia BCE frequently share the long axial plan with entrance
and innermost room at opposite ends, and the 'Ain Dara temple in northern Syria,
discussed in Chapter 5, is the closest structural comparandum we have.

---

### The lodge's axis

In the English lodge arrangement, and in most jurisdictions derived from it, the
Worshipful Master is placed in the East. The Senior Warden is in the West. The
candidate enters from the west or north-west. The East is the place of the
lodge's presiding authority and of light.

The sacred pole is therefore **reversed**. In the Temple, one moves east to west
toward the holiest place. In the lodge, the place of greatest dignity is the east,
and the candidate approaches it from the west.

This is not a small discrepancy, and it is not usually noticed. If the lodge were
modelled on the Temple's plan, the Master would sit in the West.

---

### Where the lodge's east actually comes from

Three candidate explanations, with their evidence.

**The solar explanation** is the one Masonic instruction itself generally gives:
the sun rises in the east, the east is the source of light, and light is the
governing metaphor of the degrees. This is internally coherent and requires no
Temple at all. It is also extremely widespread — the association of east with
dawn, origin, and illumination is close to a human universal, and by the
half-of-everything rule of Chapter 9, a feature that common carries almost no
information about descent.

**The ecclesiastical explanation** is the strongest historically. Christian
churches in Western Europe were conventionally oriented with the altar and
sanctuary at the east end and the congregation to the west, facing east. Anyone
in eighteenth-century Britain who had ever attended a service had this
arrangement in their bones: authority and holiness in the east, the people
approaching from the west. The lodge's arrangement matches the parish church
exactly, and matches the Temple not at all.

**The Temple explanation** requires either ignoring the reversal or reinterpreting
it, and Masonic lectures in various jurisdictions have done both, with
explanations that differ from one another — a pattern Chapter 8 taught us to read
as evidence that the arrangement arrived *before* its justification.

---

### What this case demonstrates

This is a small finding with a large methodological payoff, and it is worth
naming.

A tradition can attribute a feature to a source that did not supply it, sincerely,
for centuries, without anyone noticing that the feature points the wrong way.
There is no deception here. The lodge is oriented as its members' religious
architecture was oriented, because that is what a room looks like when you are
building one; and the Temple attribution attached itself afterward, because the
Temple was the frame in which the whole room was understood.

That is the general mechanism this Part is tracing. Features accumulate for local
and practical reasons; a symbolic universe then absorbs them and supplies them
with meanings. The meanings are real — they are used, taught, and experienced —
but they are not the features' causes.

Which means the right question to ask of any Masonic symbol is never only *what
does it mean?* It is also *when did it arrive, and what was it doing before it
meant this?*
