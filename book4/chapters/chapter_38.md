# CHAPTER THIRTY-EIGHT
## The Pillars

Of everything in a Masonic lodge, the two pillars have the strongest claim to be
genuinely Temple-derived. They are in the biblical text. They are named there.
They stand at the entrance there, and they stand at the entrance in the lodge.

They are also the site of this book's most consequential overlay claim — the
assertion that they are the Kabbalistic pillars of Severity and Mercy, and that
Freemasonry has therefore carried a Kabbalistic doctrine from the Temple. This
chapter runs that claim through the full protocol.

First, the pillars themselves.

---

### What the text says, and where it disagrees with itself

1 Kings 7:15–22 describes two bronze pillars cast by the craftsman from Tyre, set
up at the porch of the house, with capitals or chapiters decorated with network,
wreathed chains, lily-work, and pomegranates. The one on the right is named Jachin;
the one on the left, Boaz.

The dimensions are given three times in the Hebrew Bible, and they do not agree.

| Source | Pillar height | Chapiter height | Circumference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Kings 7:15–16 | 18 cubits | 5 cubits | 12 cubits |
| 2 Kings 25:17 | 18 cubits | **3 cubits** | — |
| 2 Chronicles 3:15 | **35 cubits** | 5 cubits | — |
| Jeremiah 52:21–22 | 18 cubits | 5 cubits | 12 cubits |

Three sources give eighteen cubits; Chronicles gives thirty-five. Two give a
five-cubit chapiter; 2 Kings gives three. The usual proposals are that Chronicles
reports the pair's combined length, that a scribal error has occurred, or that
different sources measured differently. There is no consensus, and this book does
not need one.

What the disagreement establishes is worth far more than a resolution would be.
**The Temple's own biblical measurements were textually unstable before any
esoteric tradition touched them.** Any argument that depends on the precise
dimensions of the pillars — and several do — is building on a number the tradition
never agreed about.

The names are less disputed but not undisputed. *Jachin* is plausibly from a root
meaning to establish; *Boaz* is often read as containing the element for strength.
A long-standing scholarly proposal is that the names are the opening words of
dynastic formulae inscribed on the pillars — something on the order of "he will
establish [the throne of David forever]" — which would make them abbreviations of
sentences rather than names. It is a proposal, not a finding, and should be
labeled as one.

---

### What they were for

Nobody knows. The pillars were free-standing; they held up nothing. The serious
candidates include commemorative or dynastic monuments, cultic markers defining the
threshold, stands for fire or incense, and representations of something in the
divine order — a cosmic gateway, in one common reading.

The comparative evidence is genuine and should be stated in its favor. Paired
free-standing pillars or columns at temple entrances are attested in the Levantine
and Syrian architectural repertoire, and Herodotus reports two pillars, of gold and
of emerald, in the temple of Heracles at Tyre — a relevant datum, since the
Jerusalem pillars were cast by a Tyrian craftsman. This is the kind of comparative
support Chapter 5 found for the Temple's plan generally: it does not tell us what
the pillars meant, but it establishes that they belong to a known architectural
tradition rather than standing alone.

---

### When the pillars enter Masonry

Not in the Old Charges. Chapter 32 found no developed pillar symbolism there; the
two pillars in the craft's legendary history are Lamech's children's pillars
against flood and fire, which are a different pair entirely and have nothing to do
with Jachin and Boaz.

The Temple pillars appear in the early catechism and exposure material of the
eighteenth century, firmly enough that by 1762 a commercially successful exposure
could be titled *Jachin and Boaz* and expect the names to be recognized. Their
place at the lodge's entrance, and their treatment as objects of explanation,
belongs to that century.

How did they get there? By the least exotic route available. **They are in the
Bible, and the men involved read the Bible.** A fraternity organized around the
Temple, looking in 1 Kings for architectural features to use, will find two named
pillars at the entrance and will use them. No transmission is required, because no
transmission is needed: the source was in every parish church in England.

---

### The eight questions

Now the Kabbalistic claim: that Jachin and Boaz are the pillars of Severity and
Mercy on the Tree of Life, and that this identification is ancient and
transmitted.

*When does the connection first appear in Masonic contexts?* The nineteenth
century.

*Who made it?* The chain Book III established: Éliphas Lévi from 1854, Albert Pike
in *Morals and Dogma* (1871), and then the Golden Dawn from 1888, whose ritual
furniture and whose tarot iconography — the two pillars lettered B and J flanking
the High Priestess in the 1909 Waite–Smith deck — carried the identification into
general occult currency.

*What source documents it?* Lévi's works, *Morals and Dogma*, and Golden Dawn
papers. No eighteenth-century Masonic source states it.

*Was the person making the claim a Mason?* Pike, yes, and a very senior one in one
jurisdiction of one rite. Lévi, no. The Golden Dawn figures included Masons, but
they were making the claim inside a body that was not Masonic.

*Was the Kabbalistic system available to be borrowed?* Yes, abundantly — Knorr von
Rosenroth's *Kabbala Denudata* had been in print since 1677, and Christian
Kabbalah had been a European intellectual current since Pico and Reuchlin.
Availability is emphatically not transmission, but it does explain how a
nineteenth-century writer could make the connection without inheriting it.

*Is there evidence of actual transmission into eighteenth-century Craft ritual?*
**None.** This is the decisive answer.

*Is the interpretation modern?* Yes.

*Is it accepted, disputed, or speculative?* Speculative, and recognized as such by
documentary Masonic scholarship.

---

### Verdict, and what it does not mean

**E5: a nineteenth-century interpretive overlay on an eighteenth-century biblical
borrowing.** The pillars are genuinely from 1 Kings. The Kabbalistic reading of
them is genuinely from Lévi and his successors. Both facts are solid; the claim
that joins them into an ancient transmission is not.

Two things this verdict does not establish.

It does not make the Kabbalistic reading worthless. An interpretation invented in
1854 can be illuminating, and this one has been generative for a great many people.
It is simply not old, and the people who use it should know when it was made.

And it does not make the pillars' Masonic use shallow. Two pillars at a threshold,
one named for establishment and one for strength, marking the point at which a
person passes from outside to inside — that is a good symbol, and it was a good
symbol in Jerusalem, at Tyre, and in an upper room in Georgian London, for reasons
that have nothing to do with the *sefirot* and do not need them.
