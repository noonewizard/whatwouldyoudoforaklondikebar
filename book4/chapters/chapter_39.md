# CHAPTER THIRTY-NINE
## The Three Great Lights

In the English-derived jurisdictions, the Three Great Lights of Freemasonry are
the Volume of the Sacred Law, the Square, and the Compasses. They are the objects a
candidate is shown, and around which a great deal of instruction is organized.

None of the three is a furnishing of Solomon's Temple.

That is the finding of this chapter, and because it will sound like a debunking,
it is worth saying immediately what it is not. It is not a claim that the emblems
are arbitrary or late inventions. Two of them are the working tools of the actual
trade from which Freemasonry descends, and their moral use is very old indeed. The
finding is narrower and more precise: whatever these objects are doing in a lodge,
they did not get there from 1 Kings.

---

### The term itself is unstable

Before anything else, a caution about the phrase.

"Three Great Lights" has not always denoted the same three things. In earlier
usage, and in some traditions still, *lights* referred to illumination — candles,
or the lodge's notional windows, placed at particular points of the compass — and
the Bible, square, and compasses were classed as the lodge's **furniture** rather
than its lights. The modern English arrangement, in which Volume, Square, and
Compasses are the Great Lights and the sun, moon, and Master of the lodge are the
Lesser, is a standardization, and much of that standardization belongs to the
period after the Union of 1813 and the work of harmonizing rival ceremonies.

American, Scottish, Irish, and Continental practices differ, sometimes
substantially. This is a place where saying "in Freemasonry, the Three Great
Lights are…" without a jurisdiction is simply an error.

---

### The Square

The square is a working tool for testing a right angle, and its use as a metaphor
for moral rectitude is far older than Freemasonry and far more widespread.

The Greek tradition has the *tetragōnos anēr*, the four-square man — an image from
Simonides that Plato discusses in the *Protagoras* and that Aristotle echoes in
discussing the man who bears fortune well. Chinese moral literature uses the
carpenter's square and compasses as a standing figure for correct conduct. English
has "on the square" and "a square deal" as ordinary idiom.

This distribution is exactly what the half-of-everything rule of Chapter 5 warns
about.
A tool that every builder owns, producing a figure that every culture with
right-angled buildings needs, generating a metaphor that any language might reach
for — such a symbol carries almost no information about lines of descent. Its
independent invention is not merely possible; it is expected.

There is a genuine artefact worth mentioning, with its caveats. A brass square
found at Baal's Bridge in Limerick in 1830, bearing a date in the first decade of
the sixteenth century and an inscription about living with love and care upon the
level by the square, is often cited as early evidence of the moral use of the
tool in a building context. Its authenticity is generally accepted; what it proves
about the organized craft is more modest than it is usually made to bear.

### The Compasses

The compasses are the instrument of the circle, and therefore of geometry — the
science the Old Charges made the craft's foundation. Their Masonic moral use
concerns limit: the circumscription of desires within due bounds.

They too have an independent iconographic life. The compasses in the hand of God
as creator — the divine geometer measuring out the world — is a medieval and early
modern commonplace, visible in illuminated manuscripts and later in Blake's
*Ancient of Days*. Again: available to everyone, requiring no transmission.

### The Volume of the Sacred Law

Here the jurisdictional variation is at its widest and the Temple connection at
its weakest.

English lodges of the eighteenth century placed a Bible in the lodge. The later
formulation "Volume of the Sacred Law," permitting the scripture of the
candidate's own faith, is a development responding to Freemasonry's spread beyond
Christian Europe. Some jurisdictions require a Bible specifically; some permit
other volumes; and the Grand Orient de France, from 1877, ceased to require the
presence of a volume of sacred law or a declaration of belief in a Supreme Being
at all — which is precisely why it and the English-derived jurisdictions have not
recognized one another since. That schism, over this object, is the clearest
possible demonstration that there is no single Masonic practice to describe.

As for the Temple: the First Temple as described in 1 Kings had no scriptural
volume on display. The Ark contained tablets; a codex of scripture in a ritual
setting is an anachronism for the period, and the Torah scroll's liturgical
centrality belongs to a much later religious world.

---

### What the Lights actually are

Two working tools of a medieval and early modern building trade, and the sacred
book of the society in which the fraternity formed.

That is a perfectly coherent set. It says: *this is a craft, its instruments carry
moral meaning, and it is conducted under an acknowledgement of the divine.* It is
the natural emblem-set for what Freemasonry was in 1723 — a trade's institutional
descendant, moralizing its own equipment, in a Christian country.

It is not a set assembled from Ezekiel. And noticing that is the same lesson as
Chapters 36 and 37: **the lodge's contents came from the lodge's actual history,
and the Temple was the frame that received them.** The frame is genuine, and it
did real work. It simply was not the source.
