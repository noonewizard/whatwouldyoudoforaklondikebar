# CHAPTER FORTY-TWO
## The Inner Temple

If the body is a temple, then there is an interior, and the interior has a
condition. This chapter concerns what tradition has said about that condition and
about what may be done to it.

---

### The claim in its strong and weak forms

The weak form says: moral and spiritual life can usefully be *described* using
architectural language. Foundations, structure, ornament, ruin, restoration. This
form is almost unobjectionable and almost trivially true. Metaphors are useful.

The strong form says something more: that the inner life is *actually structured*
— that it has parts standing in fixed relations, that those parts can be built or
neglected in a determinate order, and that the architecture is not merely a way of
speaking but a description of how a person is put together.

The traditions this book examines have mostly asserted the strong form. It is
worth knowing which one is being asserted at any moment, because they carry
different burdens of proof.

---

### The Christian interior

Augustine's *Confessions* interiorized Christian spirituality decisively, and the
spatial language is everywhere in it: memory as a vast hall, the self as a country
to be entered, God as more inward than one's own inmost part. Later Christian
writers built on this with explicit architecture. Teresa of Ávila's *Interior
Castle* organizes the spiritual life as a series of dwelling-places within a
castle, entered successively.

Note the structure of Teresa's device, because it recurs everywhere in this
material: **a set of rooms, an order of passage, and a center.** That is a temple
plan, functionally, whatever it is called. Court, hall, innermost sanctuary. The
Temple's own arrangement — a graded sequence of increasing restriction toward a
single inaccessible center — is unusually well suited to describing an interior
life, and traditions with no contact with one another have reached for that shape
independently.

### The Masonic interior

Masonic usage is more modest in one respect and more demanding in another.

More modest: it does not generally claim visionary access to inner rooms. The
language is of work, conduct, and character.

More demanding: it insists on the work being *unfinished and ongoing*. The
Masonic inner temple is not a place to be entered; it is a structure to be raised
over a lifetime, by a person who will not see it completed.

The phrase used across several jurisdictions for what is being built — a house not
made with hands, eternal in the heavens — is Paul's, from 2 Corinthians, and it
belongs to the Christian inheritance rather than to any operative secret. Its
Masonic use is an appropriation of a scriptural formula, made in a context where
everyone present would recognize it.

---

### What is actually being claimed about a person

Strip the architecture away and the claim underneath is this: **a person's
character is cumulative, has structure, and can be worked on deliberately.**

Each of those three is contestable, and each has a genuine defense.

*Cumulative*: what one does repeatedly changes what one is disposed to do. This is
the oldest claim in virtue ethics — Aristotle's, that we become just by doing just
acts — and it is also, in a different vocabulary, one of the better-supported
findings about habit formation.

*Structured*: some traits depend on others. Courage without judgment is
recklessness; generosity without prudence is ruin. Traditions that organize the
virtues hierarchically are asserting dependency relations, and the assertion is
not absurd.

*Workable*: deliberate practice can alter disposition. This is the load-bearing
claim, it is the one most often overstated, and Chapter 45 will take it up with
the appropriate caution.

---

### The honest limit

Here is what the inner-temple language cannot do, and where it becomes a liability.

It can make the self sound more orderly than any self is. A building has a plan; a
person does not. A building's parts do not have opinions about the renovation.
The architecture metaphor smuggles in coherence, intentionality, and a single
builder — and the most reliable finding of the last century of psychology is that
people are considerably less unified than they experience themselves as being.

It can also make failure sound like collapse. If character is a structure, then a
lapse is structural damage, and the metaphor supplies no vocabulary for the
ordinary human condition of being partly good, inconsistently, most of the time.

Traditions that use this language well know this and compensate — which is
precisely why the Masonic emphasis falls on the *rough* stone and the *unfinished*
work rather than on the completed edifice. The next two chapters take up that
emphasis, which is the tradition's best idea and the one it has defended most
carefully.
