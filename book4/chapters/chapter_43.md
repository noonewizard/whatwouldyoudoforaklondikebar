# CHAPTER FORTY-THREE
## The Rough Ashlar

An ashlar is a squared building stone. The word is a technical term of the trade,
not a symbol, and it entered Masonic usage the way *lodge* and *warden* did: it was
already the working vocabulary.

In the lodge, two ashlars are shown: one rough as it came from the quarry, one
dressed and true. The rough one stands for the candidate.

---

### The metaphor's actual content

Book II examined the general structure of transformation symbolism. What the
ashlar adds, specifically, is a set of commitments that most transformation
metaphors do not carry — and they are worth separating out, because they are
unusual.

**The material is adequate already.** A rough ashlar is not bad stone. It is good
stone in an unworked state. Nothing is added to it and nothing is substituted for
it; the finished block is made entirely of what was there at the quarry. This
distinguishes the metaphor sharply from metaphors of rebirth, purification, or
substitution, which imply that something must be replaced. The Masonic image is
subtractive.

**The work is removal.** What the mason does is take material away. There is no
way to put a corner back.

**Therefore the work is irreversible and requires judgment.** A stone can be
over-dressed. This is a real hazard of the actual trade, and it is an unusually
honest feature for a moral metaphor to have retained: the possibility of ruining
something by working it too hard.

**And the stone does not dress itself.** This is where the metaphor gets
complicated, and the tradition has never entirely resolved it. If the candidate is
the stone, who is the mason? Masonic instruction uses the tools as things the
candidate is given, which makes him both stone and workman — a strain the image
cannot really bear, and which every tradition using it has had to fudge.

---

### The historical route

The rough and perfect ashlars as a paired emblem belong to the eighteenth-century
development of the lodge's symbolic furniture, appearing in exposure and
catechism material and then stabilizing on the tracing boards of the nineteenth
century. In English practice the pair was joined by a third stone in some
arrangements; jurisdictions differ on the number, the names, and the placement,
and the differences are old enough that no single account should be given as
standard.

There is no biblical rough ashlar. The nearest biblical material runs in a
different direction entirely: 1 Kings 6:7 has the Temple's stone made ready
*before* it arrived at the site, dressed at the quarry, so that no tool was heard
in the house. In the biblical account the dressing happens elsewhere and the
building receives finished stone. In the Masonic image the rough stone is present
in the lodge, which is to say present at the Temple, being worked.

This is another quiet reversal, of the same kind Chapter 36 found in the lodge's
orientation, and it tells the same story. The emblem came from the trade, where
rough and dressed stone are the daily facts of the work. It was then placed inside
the Temple frame, which accepted it, and the frame's own text was not consulted.

---

### The rejected stone is a different metaphor

A frequent conflation deserves correcting. Psalm 118's stone which the builders
rejected, become the head of the corner — taken up in the gospels and in 1 Peter,
and enormously important in Christian theology — is not the rough ashlar. It is a
finished stone whose *value was misjudged*. The point of that image is reversal of
estimation, not transformation of material.

Masonic and popular writing sometimes runs them together, producing an emblem that
means both "you are unfinished" and "you are undervalued." These are different
consolations, and a person needs to know which one is being offered.

---

### Why the image survives

Because it locates the difficulty correctly.

Most people, asked what stands between them and being better, will not say they
lack information. They will say something about effort, consistency, and time —
and about the gap between knowing what a better version of themselves would look
like and being able to produce it.

The rough ashlar addresses exactly that gap and offers no shortcut for it. It does
not say the stone is secretly already finished. It does not say the right knowledge
will finish it. It says: here is the material, here are the tools, the work is
slow, it is yours, and you can get it wrong.

That is not a flattering promise, which is probably why it has lasted.
