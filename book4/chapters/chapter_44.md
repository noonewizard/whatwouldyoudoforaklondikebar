# CHAPTER FORTY-FOUR
## The Perfect Ashlar

The second stone is dressed square, its faces true, its angles right. It can be
tested: applied to a square, it either fits or it does not. It can be built with,
because a true stone sits on other true stones and the wall stands.

This chapter asks what that image claims about a finished human being, and whether
the claim is one anybody should want.

---

### Testability is the point

The distinctive feature of the perfect ashlar is not perfection. It is
**verifiability**.

A dressed stone's quality is not a matter of opinion. The square is applied; the
result is public; the mason who dressed it badly cannot argue. This is a real
property of the actual trade, and it is the property the emblem imports.

The moral claim underneath is therefore stronger than it first appears. It is not
that a person should aspire to be good. It is that there are **standards against
which conduct can be tried**, that the trying is possible, and that its result is
not private. Masonic instruction makes this explicit in its use of the tools: the
square tries the work, the level establishes equality, the plumb tests
uprightness. Each is an instrument of assessment, not of production.

That emphasis is unusual. Most inner-transformation traditions locate the
verification inside — in an experience, a state, a certainty. The Craft's tools
locate it outside, in something that can be applied to the work by someone else.

---

### What the stone is for

A perfect ashlar is not an object of contemplation. It is a component. Its whole
purpose is to be built into a wall alongside other stones, where it will be
invisible and will bear load.

This is the part of the emblem most often lost in modern retellings, which tend to
present the perfect ashlar as self-realization — becoming one's finest self. The
trade image says something considerably less individualistic. **The point of being
true is that others can build against you.** A stone that is true only in isolation
has failed at the one thing stones do.

Read that way, the pair of ashlars is not a doctrine of personal development at
all. It is a doctrine of fitness for common work: you are being dressed so that
you can be joined to others without leaving a gap.

---

### The objection, stated fairly

There is a serious objection to this image and it should not be waved away.

Uniform blocks are interchangeable. A wall does not want stones with
idiosyncrasies. If the moral ideal is a component that fits flush against its
neighbours, then the metaphor's logic runs toward conformity — toward an
institution that dresses its members until their edges match, and calls the
matching virtue.

This is not a hypothetical danger. Fraternal and initiatory organizations have in
fact produced conformity, deference to hierarchy, and a strong incentive to
resemble the men already inside. Any honest account of what the ashlar metaphor
licenses has to admit that "be shaped until you fit" is among the things it can be
made to mean.

Two things are worth saying in response, neither of them a full defence.

The first is that the tradition's own emphasis falls overwhelmingly on the *rough*
stone. The unfinished condition is where the candidate is placed and where the
instruction dwells. A tradition genuinely organized around producing identical
finished blocks would spend its time on the finished block, and this one does not.

The second is that the tools tell against it. A square tests an angle, not a
resemblance. The standard invoked is geometric and impersonal — it is the same for
every stone and it is not set by the other stones. Whatever conformity such a
system produces, it is not licensed by its own instruments.

---

### The honest position

The perfect ashlar is a good emblem with a real failure mode, and the failure mode
is not a misreading — it is available in the image itself.

The most defensible way to hold it is the way the tradition mostly does hold it:
as a direction rather than a destination. Nobody in a lodge is shown a man and told
that he is the perfect ashlar. The stone sits there as a standard, permanently
unoccupied, while everyone present understands themselves to be the other one.

That arrangement — a visible standard, an acknowledged distance from it, and tools
for closing the distance that everybody has been given — is the whole of the
Craft's moral psychology in three objects. Whether it works is the question
Chapter 45 has to face.
