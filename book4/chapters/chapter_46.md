# CHAPTER FORTY-SIX
## Architecture Changes Behavior

Popular writing about sacred architecture makes a strong claim: that buildings act
on the people inside them, shaping thought and feeling by their proportions. The
claim is usually made without evidence and usually believed.

Something in the neighbourhood of it is true. Considerably less than is usually
asserted, and for reasons that have very little to do with proportion.

This Part goes through the relevant research literature honestly, which means
reporting what has held up, what has not, and where the honest answer is that
nobody knows. Book II examined ritual's psychological machinery; this Part examines
*space*, and the two are deliberately kept apart because the evidence for them is
of different quality.

---

### Start with what is not in dispute

Buildings determine what is physically possible. They decide who can see whom, who
must pass whom, where a person can stand, how far a voice carries, what is
visible from the door, and how long it takes to get from one place to another.

These are not psychological effects. They are constraints, and they are absolute.
A room in which the seating faces one point produces an audience; a room with
seating in a circle does not, whatever anyone intends. A corridor with a single
route produces encounters. A building with one entrance produces a threshold.

This is the least glamorous claim in this Part and by a wide margin the
best-supported. Roger Barker's mid-century work on behavior settings documented
that the observable behaviour of people in a given place is often better predicted
by the place than by the individuals, and the general point has been absorbed
into environmental psychology, urban design, and architecture practice.

Applied to the lodge: the arrangement Part VIII described has behavioural
consequences that require no symbolism at all. The candidate enters at one point,
under supervision, and moves along a prescribed path in the presence of everyone
else. Attention converges on him because the geometry converges on him. Nobody has
to be primed.

---

### Where the evidence gets thinner

Beyond constraint, three further claims are commonly made, in descending order of
support.

**That environments affect mood and restoration.** Reasonably supported. The
research on restorative environments — the Kaplans' attention restoration theory,
and Roger Ulrich's much-cited 1984 report in *Science* that surgical patients with
a window view of trees recovered faster than those facing a brick wall — has
sustained decades of follow-up work on natural settings, daylight, and views. The
Ulrich study itself is small and has the limitations of its period, and it should
be cited as a landmark rather than as a demonstration. The broader finding has
held.

**That environments affect the *style* of cognition.** Weakly supported, and this
is where caution is most needed. Chapter 49 takes the best-known example — ceiling
height — in detail, because its evidential history is instructive.

**That specific proportions produce specific psychological or spiritual states.**
Unsupported. There is no body of research establishing that a room in a 2:1 ratio
does anything a room in a 1.9:1 ratio does not. Claims of this kind circulate
widely in writing on sacred geometry and are, as far as this book has been able to
determine, entirely assertion. Chapter 12's computations on the Temple's own
proportions are relevant here: the building's ratios are simple and integral, and
there was no evidence that they encoded anything beyond the ordinary conventions of
Levantine temple design.

---

### The replication problem, stated up front

Anyone citing psychology in 2026 has an obligation to say which psychology.

Over the past fifteen years, large replication efforts have found that a
substantial fraction of published findings in social and cognitive psychology do
not reproduce at their original effect sizes, and that the subfield hit hardest
was **social priming** — the study of how incidental environmental cues shape
subsequent judgement and behaviour. Several famous results in that area have
failed well-powered replication attempts.

This matters enormously here, because the claim "the building shapes your thinking"
is, in its experimental form, a social priming claim. It belongs to the most
damaged neighbourhood of the field.

The consequence for this book is a rule, applied for the rest of Part X: **a
finding in this area is reported as established only if it is a constraint, a
large and repeatedly observed effect, or has survived direct replication.**
Everything else is reported with its status attached. Where a finding has failed
replication, this book says so, including when the finding would have been useful.

---

### Embodiment, with the same caution

The broader claim that abstract thought is structured by bodily and spatial
experience — that we understand more as up, importance as weight, intimacy as
closeness — comes from Lakoff and Johnson's work on conceptual metaphor and is
supported by a very large body of linguistic evidence. Languages really do
systematically map abstract domains onto spatial ones.

That linguistic finding is robust. What is far less robust is the experimental
programme that grew from it, in which manipulating a bodily state was claimed to
shift an abstract judgement. Several of that programme's signature results have not
replicated well, including some that were, for a decade, textbook examples.

So the defensible version is: **our vocabulary for inner life is thoroughly
spatial, and that is a real and consequential fact about how humans represent
things.** The undefensible version is that holding a warm cup makes you a warmer
person.

The Temple sits squarely in the defensible version. It is a spatial vocabulary for
things that have no shape — a way of giving extension, sequence, and inside-ness to
matters that possess none. Whether that changes anyone is a further question. That
it is how the language works is not in doubt.

---

### What this Part will and will not conclude

It will conclude that the lodge's effects on the people in it are real, and that
most of them are explicable by constraint, attention, ceremony, and social
context — that is, by things Book II already analysed — rather than by properties
of the architecture as such.

It will not conclude that sacred space is inert. The next chapter takes up the one
mechanism where the architectural contribution is both specific and genuinely
supported: what a building does to where people look.
