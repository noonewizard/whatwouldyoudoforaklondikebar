# CHAPTER FORTY-SEVEN
## The Architecture of Attention

The most defensible thing a building does to a mind is decide what it looks at.

This is not a subtle effect and it is not controversial. Visual attention is
limited, it is directed, and it is powerfully influenced by the physical
arrangement of the visible world. A building that controls sightlines controls
attention, and control of attention is the substrate of nearly everything else
that happens in a room.

---

### Three mechanisms

**Occlusion.** What cannot be seen is not attended to. This is trivially true and
architecturally enormous. The Temple's graded plan — porch, hall, inner sanctuary,
each concealed from the one before — meant that the number of people who could see
the innermost space was almost zero. Concealment is not merely a restriction on
access. It is a permanent allocation of attention to the boundary rather than to
what lies beyond it.

**Convergence.** Geometry that funnels sightlines to a single point puts an object
or a person at the focus of everyone's visual field simultaneously. Axial plans
do this; so does any long approach. The effect is not mystical. It is that
twenty people are looking at the same thing and each can see that the others are.

**Sequence.** A building that must be traversed determines the order in which
things are encountered, and order matters for interpretation. What is seen first
frames what is seen next. A plan with a fixed route is, functionally, an argument
with a fixed order of premises.

None of these requires a psychological theory. They follow from optics and from
the fact that people have one visual system and cannot look in two directions.

---

### Why this is the strongest case in Part X

Because it survives the objection that undoes most claims in this territory.

The objection is: *how do you know it's the building and not the ceremony?* For
mood effects, priming effects, and cognitive-style effects, the objection is often
fatal — the building and the occasion are confounded, and disentangling them
requires experiments that mostly have not been run.

For attention it does not apply, because the mechanism is physical. If a wall
stands between a person and an object, the person does not see the object. No
alternative explanation is needed and none is available.

---

### The Temple as an attention machine

Read the biblical description with this in mind and it becomes, among other
things, a specification for the distribution of visual access.

The courts are public. The hall is restricted. The innermost room is entered by one
person on one day of the year. At every stage the building subtracts viewers, and
at the last stage it subtracts nearly all of them.

The standard interpretation of this arrangement is graded holiness, and that is
surely right as a description of what the arrangement *meant*. But the
architectural fact underneath the meaning is simpler: **the Temple was designed so
that almost nobody could see the thing it was for.**

That design has a consequence which no amount of theology can remove. Where access
is restricted, description substitutes for sight, and description is transmitted by
people. A building of this kind necessarily produces a class of persons who have
seen and can tell — which is to say, it produces authority as a by-product of its
plan.

### The lodge as an attention machine

The lodge does the same work with far cruder means and achieves something
structurally similar.

There is a door, and a person stationed at it. There is a room whose interior is
not visible from outside. There is a prescribed path along which a candidate
moves, at a pace set by others, while the rest of the room watches. Where
something is to be seen, it is shown, and the showing is an event with an
officer attached to it.

None of this is architecturally impressive. A tavern's upper room has no axis, no
sanctuary, and no occlusion beyond a closed door. What the lodge does is
**substitute procedure for architecture**: where the Temple used walls to control
what could be seen, the lodge uses officers, sequence, and instruction. The
functional result overlaps considerably.

This is worth dwelling on, because it explains something otherwise puzzling. The
lodge's Temple symbolism is not an attempt to reproduce a building's appearance. It
is, whether or not anyone intended it so, a reproduction of a building's *effects*
by non-architectural means.

---

### The limit of the claim

Controlling attention is not the same as controlling minds, and the distinction
should be held firmly.

A room that makes everyone look at one thing has determined the input. It has not
determined what anybody makes of it. The candidate looking at the emblem the
Deacon is indicating may be moved, bored, sceptical, or thinking about the journey
home. Attention is a channel; it is not content.

What attention does supply is **shared reference**: everyone present knows what
everyone present is looking at. Book II argued that this common knowledge is a
large part of what makes collective ritual effective — not that participants have
the same experience, but that they know they are having their experiences of the
same thing at the same time.

A building's most powerful psychological contribution, on the evidence, is not
that it induces a state. It is that it guarantees a common object.
