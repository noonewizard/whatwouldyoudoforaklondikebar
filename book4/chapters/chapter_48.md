# CHAPTER FORTY-EIGHT
## Thresholds

Every tradition in this book makes something of doorways. The Temple's porch and
its pillars; the veil before the inner sanctuary; the lodge's guarded door; the
candidate's entrance. Crossing is treated as consequential.

There is a body of psychological research that appears to explain why. It is
frequently cited in popular writing on ritual and architecture, usually without
its qualifications. This chapter supplies the qualifications, because they are the
interesting part.

---

### The doorway effect

In the mid-2000s, Gabriel Radvansky and colleagues reported a striking finding:
people who walked through a doorway were worse at remembering something they had
been holding in mind than people who walked an equivalent distance without
crossing a threshold. The effect appeared in virtual environments and in real
rooms, and it acquired a memorable name — walking through doorways causes
forgetting.

The theoretical account is event segmentation, developed principally by Jeffrey
Zacks and colleagues: the mind parses continuous experience into discrete events,
and a boundary between events makes information from the preceding event less
accessible. Doorways are unusually good event boundaries. On this account, the
threshold does not have magical properties; it is simply a very salient cue that
one situation has ended and another begun.

Event segmentation itself is on solid ground. It has a large literature, converging
behavioural and neuroimaging evidence, and it explains a range of findings beyond
this one.

### The qualification

The doorway effect specifically has had a harder time.

Subsequent work found the effect to be **substantially more conditional than the
early reports suggested** — dependent on memory load, on the demands of the task,
and on the particular paradigm. At least one well-publicized replication attempt,
using both immersive virtual reality and real environments, failed to reproduce the
effect under low memory load and reported it only when the task was made
demanding. [SOURCE VERIFICATION REQUIRED: the specific publication details of this
replication are cited from memory and should be checked before quotation.]

The fair summary is this. Event segmentation at boundaries: well supported.
Doorways as effective boundaries: supported. A robust, general, large memory
decrement from walking through any door: **not established, and probably not
true.**

This is exactly the kind of finding that should be reported with its history
rather than with its headline. A book arguing that thresholds matter has an
obvious interest in the strong version of this result, which is a reason to state
the weak version clearly.

---

### What survives, and it is enough

Strip out the contested memory decrement and a solid claim remains: **a threshold
is a cue that an event boundary has occurred, and humans use such cues to parse
experience.**

That is sufficient for everything this book needs.

A ritual that wants a participant to experience a discontinuity — to feel that what
happens now is not continuous with what happened before — needs to supply
boundary cues. A door is the most available one in the built world, and it is
reinforced when crossing it is slowed, conditioned, or contested. A door one may
simply walk through is a weak boundary. A door at which one is stopped, questioned,
and admitted is a strong one.

This is precisely what both the Temple's graded access and the lodge's guarded
entrance do. They are not exploiting a fragile laboratory effect. They are
amplifying an ordinary perceptual fact by making the crossing *difficult*.

---

### The veil, and the threshold that cannot be crossed

The Temple's innermost boundary deserves separate treatment because it is a
different kind of object.

A door is a boundary one may cross. The veil before the Holy of Holies was a
boundary one may not — for almost everybody, at almost every time. Its function
was not to segment an experience but to make an experience permanently
unavailable.

An uncrossable threshold does something a crossable one cannot. It creates a
region that exists in the mind only as description, and descriptions of
inaccessible regions are unusually stable, because nothing can contradict them.
This is the same mechanism Chapter 47 identified in the building's attention
economy, and it is the mechanism underlying Book III's central argument about the
Lost Word.

Freemasonry, notably, has no uncrossable threshold. Every door in the system is
one a member may eventually pass. Whatever the tradition inherited from the Temple,
it did not inherit the veil — and Part XIII will argue that this single structural
difference explains more about the character of Masonic secrecy than any amount of
speculation about content.

---

### A note on method

This chapter has spent a third of its length weakening a finding that would have
supported its argument. That is deliberate, and the reason is worth stating.

A book that cites psychology to explain ritual is only as trustworthy as its
weakest citation. The doorway effect is the single most commonly cited experimental
result in popular writing about thresholds and ritual, and it is cited almost
everywhere in its strongest, least defensible form. Repeating it that way here
would have been easy and would have made the chapter more persuasive.

It would also have meant that a reader who later discovered the replication
history would be right to discount everything else in the book.
