# CHAPTER FIFTY-ONE
## The Human Brain in Sacred Space

This chapter has a rule, and the rule is stated before the content so that the
reader can hold the chapter to it.

**No claim will be made that any architectural feature activates any brain
region.** Not the temporal lobe, not the parietal lobe, not a "God spot." Claims of
that form are made constantly in popular writing about sacred architecture, and
they are, without exception known to this book, either unsupported or built on
research that has not survived scrutiny.

What follows is the neuroscience that is actually relevant, which turns out to be
about something other than mysticism — and to be, for this book's argument, far
more interesting.

---

### First, what has failed

Two prominent claims should be dealt with plainly, because omitting them would be
the kind of concealment this book has forsworn.

**The "God helmet."** Michael Persinger reported from the 1980s onward that weak
transcranial magnetic fields applied over the temporal lobes produced sensed
presences and experiences with religious character, and the work was widely
publicized as a neuroscience of religious experience. In 2005, a Swedish group led
by Pehr Granqvist attempted a double-blind replication and did not reproduce the
effect; what predicted participants' reported experiences was their suggestibility,
not the magnetic field. The exchange that followed did not rescue the original
claim. **This is a failed replication of a famous result, and it should be treated
as such.**

**Neuroimaging of contemplatives.** Andrew Newberg and Eugene d'Aquili's imaging
studies of meditating Buddhists and praying Carmelite nuns from the late 1990s
onward are constantly cited as showing the brain basis of mystical experience. The
studies are small — a handful of participants — and the inferential leap from
regional blood-flow differences during a self-initiated practice to conclusions
about the nature of religious experience is very large. Later work by other groups
has been more careful and correspondingly less dramatic. The honest status is
**E4 to E5: suggestive, small, and routinely overinterpreted in the popular
retelling.**

There is no identified brain region for religious experience. The lesion and
imaging literature on self-transcendence is real and worth watching, and it does
not support a localizationist claim.

### What is solid: the brain is a spatial instrument

Now the part that holds up, and it holds up as well as anything in systems
neuroscience.

In 1971 John O'Keefe and Jonathan Dostrovsky reported cells in the rat hippocampus
that fired when the animal was in a particular location — place cells. In 2005 the
Moser laboratory reported grid cells in the entorhinal cortex, firing in a regular
hexagonal lattice across an environment. Head-direction cells and boundary-sensitive
cells were added to the picture. The 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine
went to O'Keefe and to May-Britt and Edvard Moser for this work.

Alongside it runs the finding that the same system is deeply involved in memory.
The hippocampus is required for forming new episodic memories, and Eleanor
Maguire's studies of London taxi drivers, beginning in 2000, found structural
differences in posterior hippocampus associated with years of navigational
experience.

The short version: **the neural machinery humans use to remember things is
substantially the machinery evolved to know where they are.** Place and memory are
not separate faculties that happen to interact. They run on shared hardware.

### The method of loci

This is where the neuroscience meets the subject of this book directly.

The memory technique known since antiquity as the method of loci — placing items to
be remembered at locations along an imagined walk through a familiar building, and
recalling them by walking through it again — is one of the few mnemonic techniques
with unambiguous experimental support. It is what competitive memory performers
use. A 2017 study led by Martin Dresler, published in *Neuron*, trained novices in
the technique and reported both large performance gains and changes in functional
connectivity patterns toward those seen in memory athletes.

The technique works because it exploits the architecture-memory coupling described
above. A building is exactly the kind of structure the hippocampal system handles
well: bounded, sequenced, traversable, stable.

And this is not a modern rediscovery imposed on the past. The art of memory was a
standard part of rhetorical training from antiquity through the Renaissance, using
buildings — frequently, explicitly, temples and theatres — as the storage
structure. William Schaw's statutes to the Scottish masons in 1598 and 1599
reference the art of memory as something masons were expected to practise. This is
a documented sixteenth-century link between an organized building trade and a
technique for storing knowledge in imagined architecture, and it is one of the more
remarkable facts in this book's evidence.

### Awe, with its error bars

One further area is worth reporting because it is the most likely candidate for a
genuine architectural effect.

Dacher Keltner and Jonathan Haidt proposed in 2003 that awe is elicited by
perceived vastness together with a need for cognitive accommodation — encountering
something that does not fit one's existing frames. Subsequent research has
associated awe experiences with a diminished sense of self, altered time
perception, and, in some studies, increased prosocial behaviour.

Vast enclosed spaces are, by that definition, close to purpose-built awe elicitors.
If any part of the popular claim about sacred architecture is going to be
vindicated, this is where.

The honest caveats: the awe literature is young; effect sizes are generally modest;
several findings come from small samples and laboratory inductions using
photographs or video rather than buildings; and it belongs, in part, to the same
methodological neighbourhood as the results Chapter 49 treated cautiously. Rate it
**E3 to E4: a real and replicated phenomenon whose specific effects are still being
established.**

### What this means for the Temple

Take the solid findings and apply them.

A building is not a device for producing mystical states. It is a device for
**organizing memory** — and humans are extraordinarily good at remembering things
that are attached to places, in an order, inside a structure.

This reframes the whole of the Temple's afterlife. The reason a building that no
longer exists could carry twenty-five centuries of accumulated meaning is not that
its proportions encoded anything. It is that a described architecture gives the
human memory system exactly the scaffold it works best with: named parts, fixed
relations, a route through them, and a centre.

Solomon's Temple is a set of rooms in which a civilization stored things. Courts,
hall, sanctuary; two pillars, a sea, a stair; an outside, an inside, and an
innermost. Each of those is a locus, and each locus has held different contents in
different centuries — moral, mystical, magical, fraternal.

That is why the building could be lost without the Temple being lost. The plan is
the mnemonic, and a plan survives its stones.

It is also the strongest thing the cognitive sciences have to offer this book's
central question, and it is worth noticing what kind of answer it is. It does not
say the Temple is powerful because of what it encodes. It says the Temple is
powerful because of what it *affords* — and what it affords is remembering.
