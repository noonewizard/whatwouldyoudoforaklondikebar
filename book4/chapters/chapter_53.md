# CHAPTER FIFTY-THREE
## The Temple in Christian Kabbalah

In the late fifteenth century, Christian scholars began to study Kabbalah — and to
do something to it that changed its afterlife permanently. This chapter traces
what they did specifically to the Temple.

---

### The chain, briefly

Book III established the transmission chain and this book does not re-derive it.
Giovanni Pico della Mirandola's *Conclusiones* of 1486 asserted that Kabbalah, read
correctly, confirmed Christian doctrine. Johannes Reuchlin developed the programme
in *De verbo mirifico* (1494) and *De arte cabalistica* (1517). Christian Knorr von
Rosenroth's *Kabbala Denudata* (1677–84) made a substantial body of Kabbalistic
text available in Latin, and became the principal conduit through which later
European occultism accessed the material.

The essential character of the enterprise should be stated plainly, without either
hostility or apology. **Christian Kabbalah was an appropriation with a thesis.**
Its practitioners studied Jewish texts sincerely and often with real learning, and
they read them in order to find Christian doctrine inside. What they produced is
therefore a genuine European intellectual tradition and not a transmission of
Jewish Kabbalah, and treating the two as one thing has caused a great deal of
confusion downstream.

---

### What they did with the Temple

Three moves, of increasing consequence for this book.

**Christological reading.** The Temple's furnishings, dimensions, and rites were
read as types of Christ and the Church — an interpretive practice already ancient
in Christianity, to which the Kabbalistic apparatus added a layer of numerical and
letter-based technique. This was theologically conventional and made no new claims
about architecture.

**Harmonic proportion.** This is the distinctive contribution. The Venetian
Franciscan Francesco Giorgi's *De harmonia mundi* (1525) set out a comprehensive
theory of the world as a harmonically ordered structure, in which musical ratios,
the proportions of the Ark and the Temple, and the design of buildings all
participate in one system of number. Giorgi was not writing abstractly: in 1535 he
was consulted on the proportions of the church of San Francesco della Vigna in
Venice, and produced a memorandum specifying them on harmonic principles.

That episode deserves emphasis because it is rare. **Here is a documented case of
a Christian Kabbalist applying a Temple-derived proportional theory to an actual
building under construction, with the document surviving.** Most claims about
esoteric architecture have nothing of the kind behind them. This one does, and it
should be cited where the vaguer claims should not.

**Reconstruction as argument.** The Jesuit Juan Bautista Villalpando's
commentary on Ezekiel, published in Rome between 1596 and 1604, reconstructed the
visionary temple as a classical building and argued that the classical orders
themselves originated there. Chapters 3, 4 and 7 examined the reconstruction, and
Chapter 67 returns to it. Its place here is as the culmination of the Christian
Kabbalist impulse: if the Temple was designed by divine instruction, and if design
is number, then the Temple must contain the perfect system of proportion — and
therefore the architecture Europe already admired must descend from it.

---

### What this produced, and what it did not

It produced a durable European conviction that **the Temple is the source of
architectural knowledge.** That conviction is the immediate intellectual background
to Anderson's history, which makes exactly this claim in a fraternal register a
century later, and it circulated far beyond anyone who could have read Reuchlin.

It did not produce a demonstrable transmission of Kabbalistic doctrine into
eighteenth-century Craft ritual. Chapter 38 ran the eight-question protocol on the
most promising candidate — the pillars — and found the Kabbalistic identification
nineteenth-century. The Christian Kabbalist Temple is part of the general European
intellectual weather in which Freemasonry formed. It is not shown to be inside
Freemasonry's ritual.

The distinction is exactly the one Chapter 9 insisted on. **Availability is not
transmission.** *Kabbala Denudata* sat in libraries from 1677; men who founded
lodges in 1717 could in principle have read it; a few of them very likely had. That
establishes possibility. It establishes nothing about what was actually put into a
ceremony, which is a question that can only be answered by documents about the
ceremony.

---

### A caution about the word

One terminological point, carried from Book III and worth repeating because so
much depends on it.

*Kabbalah*, *Cabala*, and *Qabalah* are conventionally used in scholarship to
distinguish the Jewish tradition, the Christian appropriation, and the modern
occult system respectively. The spellings are not universally observed, and no
writer should be held to them as though they were law. But the underlying
distinction is real and matters more than any spelling: **three different bodies of
material, with different dates, different purposes, and different relationships to
one another.**

Most of what popular writing calls "Kabbalistic symbolism in Freemasonry" belongs
to the third category, and the third category was assembled after 1854. Chapter 54
takes it up.
