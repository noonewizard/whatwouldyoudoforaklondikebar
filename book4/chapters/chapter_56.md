# PART XII — THE TEMPLE IN WESTERN ESOTERICISM

# CHAPTER FIFTY-SIX
## Renaissance Architects

The Renaissance is where the Temple stops being only a building in scripture and
becomes a problem in architectural theory. This chapter covers the theorists who
made it one — and corrects a claim about them that has become nearly universal and
is nearly entirely false.

---

### What Renaissance proportional theory actually was

Leon Battista Alberti's *De re aedificatoria*, composed around 1450 and printed in
1485, is the founding text of Renaissance architectural theory. Its account of
beauty turns on *concinnitas*: a condition in which nothing can be added, taken
away, or altered without harm. Beauty, for Alberti, is a relation among parts, and
the relation is numerical.

The numbers he specifies are **whole-number ratios**, and their justification is
musical. The intervals that sound consonant — the octave at 2:1, the fifth at 3:2,
the fourth at 4:3 — are ratios of small integers, and Renaissance theory held that
the same ratios govern visual harmony. Andrea Palladio's *Quattro Libri*
dell'Architettura (1570) gives sets of recommended room proportions, and they are
integer ratios of this kind.

Rudolf Wittkower's *Architectural Principles in the Age of Humanism* (1949)
established this as the scholarly account of Renaissance proportion, and it has
held.

### The golden ratio claim, and why it is wrong

Here is the near-universal modern belief: that Renaissance architects designed to
the golden ratio, φ, approximately 1.618, and that this reflects an ancient
tradition reaching back through the Temple to Egypt.

Almost every element of that belief is false, and the falsity is datable.

**Luca Pacioli's *De divina proportione*** (Venice, 1509), with Leonardo's
illustrations of polyhedra, is invariably cited as the proof. It is a genuine
treatise on the extreme and mean ratio and on its mathematical and theological
significance — Pacioli finds it a fitting emblem of the divine because it is
irrational, unique, and unchanging. But its architectural section follows
Vitruvius, and Vitruvian proportion is based on **whole-number modules and the
proportions of the human body, not on φ.** Pacioli does not propose the golden
ratio as a rule for designing buildings. The book has been made to say something
it does not say.

**The idea that φ is a universal law of beauty is nineteenth-century.** Adolf
Zeising proposed it as such in the 1850s. Matila Ghyka popularized it for the
twentieth century in the 1930s. Le Corbusier's Modulor made it a modern design
program. Every retrospective discovery of φ in Greek and Renaissance buildings
postdates that proposal — which is the classic signature of a method finding what
it was built to find.

**And the measurements do not support it.** Chapter 73 reports this book's own
computation on the Temple's dimensions in full, including the control condition,
and the control performed better than the target. The general finding in the
literature on measured buildings is the same: with enough dimensions and enough
tolerance, φ appears at the rate chance predicts.

This matters here for a reason beyond debunking. **Renaissance proportional theory
and the Temple's own proportions are consistent with each other, and both are
inconsistent with the golden-ratio story.** Chapter 12 found the Temple's stated
ratios to be 3:1, 2:1, and 2:3 — small integers, exactly the kind Alberti and
Palladio recommend. The genuine continuity between the Temple and Renaissance
architectural theory runs through whole-number harmony, and the golden ratio is an
intruder in both.

### Francesco di Giorgio and the body

Francesco di Giorgio Martini's architectural treatises, from the later fifteenth
century, contain the drawings most relevant to Chapter 41: human figures
superimposed on church plans and elevations, with the body's proportions
generating the building's.

This is the Vitruvian doctrine taken with full seriousness and worked out on
paper. It is also, importantly, *productive* rather than interpretive — these are
drawings made to design with, not readings of an existing building's hidden
meaning.

The distinction between designing by a principle and discovering a principle in
something already built runs through this entire Part. Francesco di Giorgio is on
the first side. The people who later measured Chartres looking for the same
principles are on the second, and the second is much harder to do honestly.

### Why the Renaissance cared about the Temple

Because it needed an origin.

Renaissance architecture defined itself by return: to Vitruvius, to Rome, to a
classical practice recovered after a long interruption. That is an origin story,
and origin stories compete. A Christian culture recovering a pagan architectural
inheritance had an obvious motive to establish that the inheritance was not
originally pagan.

The Temple solved the problem. If Solomon's Temple was designed under divine
instruction, and if the classical orders descended from it, then classical
architecture was not a pagan importation but a recovery of sacred knowledge — and
a Christian architect could use it without apology.

Chapter 67 examines the most ambitious version of that argument, Villalpando's.
What matters here is the motive, because it is the same motive Chapter 31 found in
Anderson: **a tradition seeking legitimacy locates its origin in the Temple,
because the Temple was the one building that everyone agreed God had specified.**

Two centuries and a different kind of institution apart, the same move, for the
same reason.
