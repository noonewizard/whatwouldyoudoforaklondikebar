# CHAPTER FIFTY-SEVEN
## Christian Cabala and Solomon

Chapter 53 examined what Christian Kabbalists did with the Temple. This chapter
takes the man: Solomon himself, and what the Renaissance and early modern period
wanted from him.

The answer is not primarily magical, and that is the surprise.

---

### Solomon as the figure of knowledge

The prevailing Renaissance Solomon is the Solomon of 1 Kings 4 and the Wisdom of
Solomon: the king who knew the natures of plants and beasts, the constitution of
the world, the operation of the elements. Chapter 27 showed how that portrait
served grimoire attribution. It served something else far more consequentially.

**Francis Bacon's *New Atlantis*, published posthumously in 1627, names its
research institution Salomon's House.** It is a college of natural philosophers
with laboratories, instruments, gardens, and a division of intellectual labor,
dedicated to the knowledge of causes and the enlargement of human empire over
nature. It is the single most influential fictional institution in the history of
science, and the Royal Society's founders explicitly looked to it.

Note carefully what has happened. The scriptural Solomon who spoke of trees and
beasts has become the patron of **organized empirical investigation**. This is the
same verse that licensed the grimoires, producing the opposite institution.

That divergence is one of the most instructive facts in this book. A single
scriptural warrant, read by different people with different purposes, generates
both a tradition of spirit-commanding manuals and a program for experimental
science. The warrant did not determine the outcome. The readers did.

---

### Solomon as the model king

The second Renaissance Solomon is political, and he is everywhere.

To call a monarch a new Solomon was standard panegyric: it claimed wisdom,
justice, magnificence, and divine favor in one figure. James VI and I was hailed
as a British Solomon, and the comparison was made at his funeral. Solomon's
judgment between the two mothers was a stock subject for the decoration of
courtrooms and town halls across Europe, where its point was jurisdictional — this
is where wise judgment is given.

The architectural expression of this is the **Escorial**, built for Philip II of
Spain between 1563 and 1584 under Juan Bautista de Toledo and Juan de Herrera. Its
Solomonic program is not a modern interpretation: the identification of Philip
with Solomon and of the building with the Temple was explicit in its own period,
in its iconography and in the writings surrounding it. The Jesuit Temple
reconstruction that Chapter 67 examines was produced in that Spanish court orbit,
with royal support.

So the early modern Solomon is a figure of governance and of building, invoked by
states. This is a very long way from the magician of Chapter 25, and it was far
more publicly important.

---

### What the Christian Cabalists added

Onto these two Solomons — the natural philosopher and the model king — the
Christian Kabbalist tradition laid a third: Solomon as possessor of a *system*.

The move is characteristic. If wisdom is knowledge of the world's structure, and
if the world's structure is numerical and linguistic, then Solomon's wisdom was
knowledge of the numbers and the letters. From there it is a short step to
believing that his Temple encoded them, and a shorter one to believing that a
sufficiently learned reader could recover the encoding.

This is where the Renaissance made its distinctive contribution to everything that
follows in this book. It did not invent Temple symbolism; that was ancient. It
supplied the conviction that **the Temple is a system to be decoded** — and it
supplied the techniques, drawn from Kabbalistic letter and number practice, with
which people would try.

Chapters 71 through 76 test those techniques on Temple material directly, with
controls, and report the results whatever they are.

---

### Keeping the Solomons distinct

Chapter 28 distinguished four Solomons. The early modern period adds two more, and
the list is worth consolidating, because conflating any two of them produces a
false history:

*The historical king* — barely recoverable.
*The biblical wisdom figure* — the source of all the rest.
*The legendary spirit-commander* — late antique onward.
*The grimoire operator* — medieval and later.
*The patron of natural philosophy* — Renaissance and early modern, leading to
Bacon.
*The model of sacral kingship* — Renaissance and early modern, leading to the
Escorial.

The last two are the ones Freemasonry's founding generation actually inherited
from the culture around them. When Anderson wrote his Temple history in 1723, the
Solomon available to an educated Londoner was principally the wise king and the
great builder — Bacon's patron of knowledge and the courts' model of justice.

He was not, for those readers, primarily a magician. The magical Solomon was
present in the culture, but he was disreputable, and there is no evidence that he
is the Solomon the *Constitutions* had in mind.

That is worth stating clearly, because a great deal of later writing has assumed
the reverse.
