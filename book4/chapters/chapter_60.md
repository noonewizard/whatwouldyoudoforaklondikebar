# CHAPTER SIXTY
## Albert Pike

More false statements about Freemasonry trace to Albert Pike than to any other
source, and almost none of them are things Pike said.

Getting him right requires separating four things: who he was, what he actually
wrote, what authority he actually had, and what was fabricated and attributed to
him. This chapter does all four, because the confusion among them is the single
largest obstacle to discussing Masonic symbolism honestly.

---

### Who he was

Albert Pike (1809–1891) was born in Boston, went west, and made a career in
Arkansas as a lawyer, newspaper editor, and poet. He served as a Confederate
brigadier general during the American Civil War, with a command involving Native
American troops, and his conduct and resignation were the subject of controversy at
the time. These facts are stated here because a biography that omits them is not a
biography, and because Pike's Confederate service is a real and legitimate reason
for the sustained modern objection to public monuments honouring him.

From 1859 until his death he was Sovereign Grand Commander of the Supreme Council
of the Ancient and Accepted Scottish Rite for the Southern Jurisdiction of the
United States. In that capacity he rewrote the Rite's degree rituals, which he
found inherited in a thin and incoherent condition, into the extended philosophical
form the Southern Jurisdiction subsequently used.

---

### The authority question, which settles most of the confusion

**Pike had no authority over Freemasonry.**

He had authority within one appendant rite, in one of two American jurisdictions of
that rite, in one country. He had none over Craft Masonry anywhere — not over the
Grand Lodges of the American states, not over the United Grand Lodge of England,
not over Scotland, Ireland, or any Continental body. The Scottish Rite is entered
only by men who are already Master Masons, and the overwhelming majority of Masons
worldwide have never taken its degrees.

Every claim of the form "Freemasonry teaches X, as Albert Pike wrote" is therefore
malformed before its content is examined. It is like citing the constitution of one
club as the doctrine of every club.

---

### What *Morals and Dogma* actually is

Published in 1871 and issued to members of his jurisdiction, *Morals and Dogma of
the Ancient and Accepted Scottish Rite of Freemasonry* is a vast commentary
organized by degree, drawing on comparative religion, philosophy, and esoteric
literature.

Three features of the book itself are routinely suppressed by those who quote it.

**It is explicitly a compilation.** Pike says so. He states that he has quoted
freely from many authors, often without attribution, and that large portions are
not original. Later scholarship has documented extensive unacknowledged borrowing,
including from Éliphas Lévi — which is to say that a substantial part of the book
most often cited as the doctrine of Freemasonry is a translation of a French
occultist whose relationship to Freemasonry was brief and whose system was
assembled in the 1850s.

**It disclaims authority.** The volume carries the statement that everyone is free
to reject what he finds in it, and that the Supreme Council does not vouch for
every statement. It was issued as material for reflection, not as a creed.

**It is expository, not prescriptive.** Pike describes what various traditions have
held at enormous length. Sentences describing the beliefs of ancient sects are
routinely quoted as though they were Pike's own confession of faith, and this
accounts for a large proportion of the anti-Masonic quotation literature.

---

### The fabrication

The most widely circulated Pike quotation — a supposed instruction to the Supreme
Councils of the world identifying the Masonic god as Lucifer — is a forgery.

It was manufactured by Gabriel Jogand-Pagès, writing as Léo Taxil, as part of an
elaborate anti-Masonic hoax conducted from the mid-1880s. Taxil publicly confessed
the entire fabrication in Paris in April 1897, at a press conference he had
convened, and explained in detail how he had constructed it and why. He was
explicit that the material was invented.

The confession is a matter of public record. The fabricated quotation has
nevertheless continued in circulation for well over a century and is still
reproduced today. Chapter 78 uses it as this book's worked case study in how a
fabrication outlives its retraction.

There is a genuine passage in *Morals and Dogma* using the word Lucifer in its
literal Latin sense of light-bearer, in a discussion of how a term was used in
antiquity. It is not the Taxil text, it does not say what the Taxil text says, and
conflating them is the mechanism by which the hoax survives.

---

### What Pike actually contributed

Setting the distortions aside, the substantive assessment.

Pike's contribution was **synthesis on an enormous scale**: he took the Scottish
Rite's degrees and attached to them a comparative-religious commentary that treated
Freemasonry as the inheritor of a universal ancient wisdom.

That framing is not supported by the evidence this book has assembled. The
documentary chronology of Parts VII and VIII shows a trade tradition acquiring a
Temple emphasis in the eighteenth century, not a transmission from the ancient
mysteries. Pike's method was that of his era's comparative religion — parallels
assembled across cultures and read as evidence of common descent — and that method
has not survived scholarly scrutiny, in Masonic contexts or anywhere else.

He should be read as a nineteenth-century synthesist of real learning, considerable
ambition, and characteristic period assumptions, writing for one rite. Read that
way he is interesting. Read as the voice of Freemasonry he is a fiction — one
constructed largely by his opponents, and then, unfortunately, embraced by some of
his admirers.
