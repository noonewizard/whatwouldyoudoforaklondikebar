# PART XIII — THE TEMPLE, EMPIRE, AND POWER

# CHAPTER SIXTY-ONE
## Temple and Kingship

A temple in the ancient Near East was not built by a religion. It was built by a
king, and it said so.

This chapter sets out what temple-building did politically, because the political
function is the best-attested thing about ancient temples and it is almost entirely
absent from the symbolic traditions that descend from them.

---

### The template

Victor Hurowitz's comparative study of building accounts established that the
biblical narratives of the Temple's construction follow a pattern widely attested
across the ancient Near East. Its elements recur with remarkable consistency:

A divine command or sanction, often through a dream or oracle. A king chosen for
the task. Materials gathered from distant places, with their distance emphasized.
Skilled craftsmen, frequently foreign. A period of construction, with duration
stated. A dedication festival, with sacrifices and assembly. The deity taking up
residence. A blessing on the king and his line.

The Gudea cylinders from Lagash, around 2100 BCE, give one of the fullest
versions: a ruler receives a dream, seeks its interpretation, obtains materials
from remote regions, builds, and dedicates. The 1 Kings account of Solomon's Temple
contains every element.

This is not a claim that 1 Kings borrowed from Gudea. It is a claim about genre:
**temple-building accounts were a recognised form with a standard shape, and the
biblical account is written in that form.** That matters for how we read it. The
elements a modern reader finds most striking — the divine sanction, the foreign
craftsman, the emphasis on cost — are the genre's conventions, not distinctive
features requiring special explanation.

---

### What the building did for the king

Four things, all of them practical.

**It demonstrated capacity.** A monumental building is an unfakeable display of
command over labour, materials, transport, and time. Everyone who sees it knows
what it cost.

**It localized the deity.** A god with an address is a god whose favour has a
place, and whoever controls the place mediates the favour.

**It concentrated revenue.** Temples in the ancient world were economic
institutions — receiving offerings, holding treasure, sometimes lending. A central
sanctuary centralizes flows.

**It made the dynasty's claim architectural.** In the Jerusalem case the
arrangement is explicit in the text: the Temple stood within the royal precinct,
adjacent to the palace complex, on the king's acropolis. It was, institutionally,
a royal sanctuary. The prophetic literature knows this and can use it as a
reproach — Amos's confrontation at Bethel turns on the shrine being the king's.

The biblical narrative attaches a dynastic promise to the building programme
directly: David is told his son will build the house and his throne will be
established. Temple and dynasty are two aspects of one claim.

---

### Who may build

The most revealing detail in the biblical account is a prohibition.

David wants to build and is not permitted to. The reasons given differ between
sources — 2 Samuel has the deity declining the offer and reversing the direction of
house-building, while Chronicles supplies the explanation that David had shed much
blood. That the reason varies is itself informative: the prohibition was
transmitted and the justification was supplied later.

What the prohibition establishes is that **building was a privilege, not merely a
project.** The right to build had to be conferred. This is precisely the logic of
authorization that runs through every institution in this book — the warrant that
must be shown before the work may begin, and that later traditions would
reproduce as charters, charges, and constitutions.

---

### Herod, and the legitimation problem

The clearest case in the whole record is the Second Temple's expansion.

Herod the Great was not of Davidic descent, was of Idumean ancestry, and ruled by
Roman appointment. His legitimacy was contested throughout his reign. He undertook
the most ambitious building programme in the region's history, and its centrepiece
was a total reconstruction of the Temple on a vastly enlarged platform.

The political reading is not cynical and it is not speculative. A ruler with a
legitimacy deficit built the thing that legitimacy is made of. The enlarged
platform he raised is, in its retaining walls, the single most substantial physical
remnant of any of this — the only part of the whole subject a person can still put
a hand on.

---

### What the symbolic tradition dropped

Here is the point worth carrying forward.

Every tradition examined in this book takes the Temple as a symbol of something
inward, moral, cosmic, or transformative. **None of them takes it as a symbol of
royal power**, which is what it demonstrably was.

The politics did not survive the transposition. When the building became a text,
and the text became a symbol, the levy and the taxation and the dynastic claim
fell away, and what remained was a sanctuary. Chapter 45's rough ashlar and
Chapter 52's heavenly palaces have no kings in them.

This is worth noticing without sneering at it. Traditions select. But a book
tracing what the Temple came to mean has an obligation to record what it meant
first, and what it meant first was: *I can build this, and you cannot.*
