# CHAPTER SIXTY-TWO
## Jerusalem

The Temple was in a particular place, and the place has had a history of its own.
This chapter gives it, because the arguments of the next chapter require it and
because almost every claim in this book's later Parts assumes some version of it.

---

### The site

Jerusalem sits on a limestone ridge in the Judean hills, away from the coastal
route, without a river, and without obvious agricultural advantage. Its water came
from the Gihon spring in the valley below the eastern ridge, and the earliest
settlement — the City of David — occupies that narrow spur, downhill from the
platform that would later hold the Temple.

The city is not where a city should be. Its significance is not geographical in
origin.

Its name appears before any Israelite presence: the Egyptian execration texts of
the early second millennium refer to a place plausibly identified with it, and the
fourteenth-century BCE Amarna correspondence includes letters from a ruler of
Urusalim to the Egyptian court. It was a settled place with a ruler and a name
several centuries before the events 1 Kings narrates.

---

### A compressed chronology

*Tenth century BCE (traditional)*: David's capture of the city and Solomon's
building, as narrated. Parts II and III examined what the archaeology does and does
not support, and the honest answer was: less than the narrative describes, and the
dispute is live.

*586 BCE*: Babylonian destruction. The Temple is burned, the city walls broken,
and a substantial portion of the population deported. This event is attested in
biblical and Babylonian sources and is not disputed.

*From 538 BCE*: return under Persian authority and the building of the Second
Temple, completed around 516. It was a modest structure by comparison, and the
biblical account records the older generation weeping at the difference.

*Second century BCE*: Seleucid desecration, the Maccabean revolt, rededication,
and Hasmonean independence.

*From 20 BCE*: Herod's reconstruction and platform expansion, on the scale
described in Chapter 61. The work continued for decades after his death.

*70 CE*: Roman destruction at the end of the First Jewish Revolt. The Temple is
burned and not rebuilt.

*135 CE*: after the Bar Kokhba revolt, the city is refounded as the Roman colony
Aelia Capitolina, and Jewish access is restricted.

*Fourth century onward*: Christian Jerusalem. Constantinian building — the Church
of the Holy Sepulchre above all — reorients the city's sacred geography westward.
The Temple platform is left in ruins, and Christian writers treated the ruin as
theologically meaningful.

*638*: the city comes under Muslim rule. The platform is cleared and built on.
The Dome of the Rock is completed in 691–92 under the caliph 'Abd al-Malik; the
al-Aqsa Mosque follows on the southern end of the platform.

*1099*: Crusader conquest, with a massacre. The platform's buildings are converted
to Christian use; the Templars take their name from their quarters there.

*1187*: Saladin's reconquest and the restoration of Islamic use.

*1535–42*: the present city walls are built under the Ottoman sultan Suleiman.
Almost every photograph of the Old City shows a sixteenth-century Ottoman wall.

---

### Three traditions, one place

The compression above makes the essential point visible. This is a site of
continuous, overlapping, and frequently contested sacred significance for Judaism,
Christianity, and Islam, each of which has held it, built on it, lost it, and
returned to it.

For Judaism it is the site of the Temple, the direction of prayer, and the centre
of a hope for restoration.

For Christianity it is the place of the crucifixion and resurrection, and — in a
theological move with long consequences — the Temple's ruin was read as
confirmation of a supersession.

For Islam it is *al-Quds*, the earliest direction of prayer before the change to
Mecca, and the site associated with the Prophet's night journey and ascent.

These are not competing descriptions of one meaning. They are three religious
systems for which the same ground carries different and central significance.

---

### Jerusalem as an idea

The city detached from its geography early and thoroughly.

The New Testament's closing vision describes a new Jerusalem descending from
heaven, measured, gated, and without a temple — because, the text says, the Lord
is its temple. Medieval European world maps frequently place Jerusalem at the
centre of the inhabited earth. Pilgrimage produced reproductions: chapels built to
the measurements of the Holy Sepulchre, calvaries and sacred mounts laid out to
allow the journey to be performed at home.

**By the time Freemasonry took up the Temple, Jerusalem had been a portable idea in
Europe for a thousand years.** Anderson's readers did not need to have been there;
nobody had been there. They had inherited a city that functioned, as the Temple
did, as a place one could locate oneself in without travelling.

That is the condition in which the next chapter's subject has to be approached —
and it is also the reason the next chapter requires more care than any other in
this book.
