# CHAPTER SIXTY-THREE
## Temple Mount

This chapter concerns a place where a book about symbols meets a subject that is
not symbolic at all.

What follows is descriptive. It sets out what is physically there, what is known
archaeologically, what the site means to the traditions that hold it sacred, and
what the arrangements governing it have been. **It takes no position on questions
of sovereignty, access, or political status, and it makes no recommendation about
any of them.** Those questions are real, they are contested by people with
genuine and deeply held commitments, and they are outside the competence of a book
about the history of a symbol.

Readers who want this book to arrive somewhere on them will not find it doing so,
and that is deliberate.

---

### Terminology

The site is known as the Temple Mount — in Hebrew *Har ha-Bayit*, the Mount of the
House — and as al-Haram al-Sharif, the Noble Sanctuary, and as the al-Aqsa Mosque
compound, since in Islamic usage *al-Masjid al-Aqsa* can denote the whole enclosure
rather than only the congregational mosque at its southern end.

**The choice among these names is itself understood as a statement**, and no
neutral term exists. This book uses "the platform" where a physically descriptive
term will serve, and otherwise names the site as the tradition under discussion
names it. That convention is a way of avoiding an implicit claim, not a way of
resolving one.

---

### What is physically there

A raised, roughly trapezoidal platform of about thirty-five to thirty-seven acres,
built out from the natural ridge by massive retaining walls and fill. In its
present extent it is Herodian: the enlargement undertaken from around 20 BCE more
than doubled the earlier enclosure, and the engineering involved is the largest
work of its kind surviving from the ancient world.

The retaining walls stand. The section of the western retaining wall accessible
for prayer is the Western Wall. Courses of Herodian masonry, with their
characteristic marginal drafting, are visible at several points, and individual
stones in the lower courses are of extraordinary size.

On the platform stand the Dome of the Rock, completed in 691–92 under the caliph
'Abd al-Malik and among the oldest surviving Islamic monuments anywhere; the
al-Aqsa Mosque at the southern end, founded in the eighth century and rebuilt
repeatedly after earthquakes; the Dome of the Chain; and a considerable number of
Mamluk and Ottoman structures, fountains, and arcades.

Beneath the Dome of the Rock lies the outcrop of bedrock known in Jewish tradition
as the Foundation Stone and in Arabic as al-Sakhra.

### What is known archaeologically, and what is not

This section is short, because the honest answer is short.

**No archaeological excavation has been conducted on the platform.** Everything
known about it comes from other sources: study of the retaining walls; the shaft
and tunnel surveys conducted by Charles Warren in the 1860s; excavation alongside
the walls outside the enclosure; historical description, principally the Mishnah's
tractate Middot and Josephus; and the Temple Mount Sifting Project, which from 1999
has sorted fill removed from the platform during construction work and recovered
artefacts from disturbed and therefore unstratified contexts.

The consequence is stark. **No identified physical remains of either Temple
building have ever been found.** What survives is Herod's platform, not Herod's
Temple, and nothing of Solomon's at all.

Two Herodian objects from the Temple precinct are exceptions worth naming, because
they are the closest thing to direct evidence that exists. A stone bearing an
inscription referring to the place of trumpeting was recovered in excavations at
the southwest corner. And a Greek inscription warning non-Jews against passing the
barrier into the inner courts — one of the warning stones Josephus describes — was
found in the nineteenth century, with a fragment of a second later. These are real
artefacts from the Temple's own precinct, and they tell us about its access rules,
which is fitting for the argument of Chapter 47.

Where on the platform the Temple stood is not established. The most widely held
proposal places the Holy of Holies at the rock beneath the Dome; others have
argued for positions north or south of it. **None can be tested without excavation,
and excavation is not going to happen.** Anyone who states the location with
confidence is stating a preference.

### The site in three traditions

For Judaism, it is the site of both Temples, the focus of prayer, and the object of
a hope of restoration. A widespread rabbinic position holds that Jews may not
ascend the platform, on grounds of ritual purity and uncertainty about the precise
location of the most sacred areas; the Israeli Chief Rabbinate has maintained such
a prohibition, and it is contested by some.

For Islam, it is the third holiest site, the earliest direction of prayer before
the change to Mecca, and the site associated in tradition with the Prophet's night
journey and ascent. It has been in continuous Muslim religious use, with
interruptions, since the seventh century.

For Christianity, the platform's significance is largely historical and
typological; Christian sacred geography in Jerusalem centres on the Church of the
Holy Sepulchre, and the Byzantine period deliberately left the platform in ruins.

---

### Arrangements

Religious sites in Jerusalem have been governed since the nineteenth century by an
arrangement commonly called the Status Quo, deriving from Ottoman decrees of the
1850s, which fixed existing rights and practices at the major shrines and has been
carried forward, with modifications, by successive authorities.

Since 1967 the platform has been under Israeli security control, with religious
administration by an Islamic Waqf under Jordanian auspices. Under the arrangement
as generally described, non-Muslim visitors may enter at designated times and
non-Muslim prayer on the platform has been prohibited. The interpretation and
application of these arrangements are the subject of continuing and serious
dispute, and incidents at the site have repeatedly had consequences well beyond
Jerusalem.

This book records that this is so. It does not adjudicate it.

---

### Two clarifications this book can make

There are two points where the history this book has assembled bears directly on
claims about the site, and on these it can be useful.

**First: movements advocating the construction of a Third Temple exist, and they
are a small minority position.** Organizations preparing vessels and training for a
restored service are real and can be described factually. They do not represent
mainstream Jewish religious opinion, the majority of which either defers
restoration to a messianic future or holds the entire question to be not presently
actionable.

**Second: there is no Masonic programme to rebuild the Temple, and the claim that
there is rests on a category error.** Every finding of Parts VII through IX points
the same way: the Masonic Temple is a symbolic and ritual structure, explicitly
identified as such in Masonic instruction itself, and its central image is an
*unfinished* building that is understood not to be completable. No Masonic
jurisdiction has a policy, a fund, a plan, or a stated aspiration concerning
construction on the platform. Chapter 77 audits the specific claims and their
sources.

The distinction matters because a symbol read as a plan is the mechanism by which
a great deal of harm gets done — and because at this particular site, the cost of
that error is not borne by the people who make it.
