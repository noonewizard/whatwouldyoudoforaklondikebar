# CHAPTER SIXTY-FOUR
## Empire Builds Temples

Every empire in the region this book covers built sanctuaries, and several of them
built sanctuaries out of the Jerusalem Temple's destruction. The sequence is worth
following, because it shows what a temple is politically when the politics are
visible rather than inferred.

---

### Rome, and the spoils

The Roman treatment of the Temple after 70 CE was not incidental destruction. It
was a program.

The spoils were paraded in Rome. The triumphal arch erected for Titus, which
stands today at the edge of the Forum, carries a relief of the procession with the
menorah and the other vessels of the Temple visibly carried. Vespasian's Templum
Pacis, dedicated in 75, housed the captured treasures — a Roman temple of peace
furnished with the contents of a destroyed Judean one.

Most pointedly, the half-shekel that Jews throughout the empire had paid annually
for the maintenance of the Temple was not abolished. It was redirected. The
*fiscus Judaicus* required the same payment, from the same people, for the temple
of Jupiter Capitolinus in Rome.

That is the clearest statement of what a temple meant to a state that has ever been
made, and Rome made it in accounting. The revenue stream was the institution;
divert the stream and the institution is transferred.

After the Bar Kokhba revolt, Hadrian refounded the city as Aelia Capitolina with a
temple to Jupiter, completing the substitution architecturally.

---

### Monumentalism as argument

Augustus was credited with the boast that he found Rome brick and left it marble.
Whether or not he said it, the program it describes was real: a regime
established by civil war reconstructing its capital in permanent materials.

The logic is consistent wherever it appears. **Monumental architecture is a claim
about duration made by institutions that are not durable.** It is expensive
precisely because expense is the message — a regime that can immobilize this much
labor and stone in a form that cannot be quickly undone is asserting that it
expects to be here when the building is old.

The assertion is usually wrong, and the buildings frequently outlive the regimes by
centuries, which is why they end up meaning something different to everyone who
inherits them.

Later examples follow the pattern without needing much comment. Justinian's Hagia
Sophia, completed in 537, was the largest interior in the world for centuries; a
tradition — attested well after the fact rather than contemporaneously, and so to
be reported as tradition — has him declaring on entering that he had outdone
Solomon. Solomon remained the benchmark even for an emperor who had exceeded him,
which is itself the point.

European states built parliaments and capitols in classical dress and courts and
banks in temple form. Twentieth-century authoritarian regimes built at scale
explicitly to project permanence; Albert Speer's account of designing with an eye
to how the structures would look as ruins is the theory made embarrassingly
explicit, though Speer's own memoirs are self-serving and should be used with
care.

---

### What this means for the symbol

Two consequences.

**The first concerns what the Temple was for its neighbors and conquerors.** It
was an asset. Its destruction was a transfer, its revenue was a prize, and its
replacement on the same ground by another god's house was a statement everyone
understood. None of this is hostile interpretation; it is what the Roman sources
themselves describe with satisfaction.

**The second concerns why the symbolic tradition could grow so large.** A building
that was destroyed and whose site was built over cannot be visited, and it
therefore cannot be measured against anybody's account of it. Rome, by completing
the erasure so thoroughly, removed every constraint on what the Temple could later
be said to have been.

That is not what Rome intended. The intention was to end a cult by ending its
address. What actually happened is that the cult's categories detached from the
address and went everywhere — into the synagogue, into the Christian church, into
mystical ascent literature, into architectural theory, and eventually into a
tavern's upper room in London.

**An empire can demolish a building. It has no way to demolish a plan.** This is
the argument Chapter 51 reached from the cognitive side and Chapter 29 from the
theological one, and imperial history supplies the third route to it.
