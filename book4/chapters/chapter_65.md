# CHAPTER SIXTY-FIVE
## Masonic Buildings

Freemasonry has built a great deal. There are Masonic halls in nearly every country
where the Craft has existed, some of them among the largest fraternal buildings in
the world, and a substantial number are architecturally distinguished.

This chapter asks a simple question about them, and the answer is the most direct
piece of evidence in the whole book for what the Masonic Temple actually is.

**How many of them look like Solomon's Temple?**

---

### The survey

*Freemasons' Hall, Great Queen Street, London.* The United Grand Lodge of England
has occupied the site since 1775. The present building, constructed between 1927
and 1933 as a memorial to the Masons who died in the First World War, is a large
Art Deco composition in Portland stone with a monumental tower.

*Freemasons' Hall, George Street, Edinburgh.* The Grand Lodge of Scotland's
headquarters, of the early twentieth century, in a restrained classical manner
appropriate to its New Town setting.

*Masonic Temple, Philadelphia.* Built 1868–73 to James Windrim's design, the
exterior is Norman Romanesque. Inside are seven lodge rooms, decorated
respectively in Egyptian, Ionic, Corinthian, Renaissance, Oriental, Gothic, and
Norman styles.

*Detroit Masonic Temple.* Completed in the 1920s to George D. Mason's design; the
largest Masonic building in the world, in a Neo-Gothic idiom, with a theatre, a
ballroom, and a great many lodge rooms.

*The House of the Temple, Washington.* Headquarters of the Scottish Rite's
Southern Jurisdiction, built 1911–15 to John Russell Pope's design. It is modelled
on the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus — a Greek tomb.

*Elsewhere.* Egyptian Revival is unusually common in American Masonic and
associated funerary architecture. Colonial and Latin American halls generally
follow local civic conventions. European lodge buildings vary with national
practice.

---

### The finding

Of the major Masonic buildings in the world, **effectively none is an attempt to
reproduce Solomon's Temple.**

They are Art Deco, Norman, Gothic, Greek Revival, Egyptian Revival, Beaux-Arts,
and — very often — indistinguishable from the civic architecture of their date and
place. The Philadelphia building offers seven styles in one structure and
Solomonic is not among them. The Scottish Rite's headquarters, built by a
jurisdiction whose degrees are saturated with Temple imagery, copies a tomb in
Asia Minor.

There is no shortage of means or ambition here. These are expensive, deliberate,
architect-designed buildings commissioned by institutions with money. Villalpando's
reconstruction had been in print for three centuries and the biblical dimensions
are in every Bible. If the Temple had been an architectural programme, someone would
have built it.

---

### Why not

Because the Temple was never an architectural programme. It was a ritual and
mnemonic frame, and Parts VIII and X established what that means.

A frame does not need to be reproduced in stone, because it is not doing its work
through the stone. The lodge becomes the Temple when the ritual declares it and
the members accept the declaration — a process that works identically in a
Palladian hall, a Gothic revival pile, a rented room, or a shipping container. What
the ritual requires from the building is enclosure, a controllable door, and enough
space to move a candidate about in. Everything else is the architecture of the
period, chosen for the ordinary reasons buildings are chosen: cost, fashion,
civic dignity, and what the architect proposed.

The Masonic building programme is therefore evidence *against* the strongest
version of the Temple claim and *for* the version this book has been developing.
A tradition that genuinely believed itself the custodian of the Temple's design
would have built the design. A tradition that uses the Temple as a declared
setting for a ceremony builds whatever the city builds, and calls the room inside
it the Temple.

---

### What the buildings do say

Something, though not what is usually supposed.

They are **public**. This deserves emphasis, because it sits awkwardly with the
image of a secret society. Masonic halls are generally prominent, named, on main
streets, with the institution's identity on the facade. An organization concealing
its existence does not put a large building with its name on it opposite the town
hall.

They are **civic**. Their idiom is the idiom of courthouses, libraries, and
banks — the architecture of respectable institutional life in their period, which
is what Freemasonry largely was in the places and times it built most.

And they are, many of them, **now too large**. Membership in most Western
jurisdictions has declined sharply since the mid-twentieth century, and a great
many of these buildings have been sold, subdivided, converted to theatres and
event spaces, or demolished.

There is an irony in this that the tradition might find instructive rather than
merely painful. An institution organized around a destroyed temple and an
unfinished building has spent the last century watching its own temples pass out
of its hands. Whatever else the symbol was, it turns out to have been a better
description of the institution's situation than anyone intended.
