# CHAPTER SIXTY-SIX
## Imagined Reconstructions

For two thousand years, people have drawn Solomon's Temple. None of them had seen
it. All of them were working from the same handful of texts.

The results do not agree, and the disagreement is the most useful evidence in this
Part. It tells us precisely how much the sources determine, which is: considerably
less than the confidence of any individual reconstruction suggests.

---

### The textual basis

Everything derives from four bodies of material, and they are not consistent with
one another.

**1 Kings 6–7** gives the plan and principal furnishings of the first Temple.
**2 Chronicles 3–4** gives a partly parallel account with some different numbers,
as Chapter 38 documented for the pillars. **Ezekiel 40–43** describes a visionary
temple in great dimensional detail — a different building, described for a
different purpose, which is not the first Temple. **The Mishnah's tractate
Middot**, with Josephus, describes the *second* Temple, in its Herodian form, from
within living memory of people who had seen it.

A reconstruction must therefore choose. Ezekiel supplies the most numbers and
describes the wrong building; Middot describes a building that existed and is the
wrong Temple; 1 Kings is the right Temple and is incomplete. Every reconstruction
in history is an argument about how to combine them, and the arguments are not
resolvable.

---

### The sequence

**Medieval Jewish exegesis** produced the earliest systematic attempts. Rashi's
eleventh-century commentaries are accompanied by explanatory diagrams in the
manuscript tradition, and Maimonides treated the Temple's structure
systematically in the *Mishneh Torah*.

**Medieval Christian exegesis** followed. Nicholas of Lyra's early
fourteenth-century commentary included Temple diagrams that were reproduced in
printed Bibles for centuries, making them among the most widely seen images of the
building in Europe.

**The sixteenth century** brought scholarly reconstruction on a new scale, notably
in Benito Arias Montano's contributions to the Antwerp Polyglot Bible of 1568–73.

**Villalpando**, in Rome between 1596 and 1604, produced the most influential
reconstruction ever made. Chapter 67 is devoted to it.

**Models** appear in the seventeenth century, and they matter more than their
obscurity suggests. Jacob Judah Leon, an Amsterdam rabbi known thereafter as Leon
Templo, built a large scale model of the Temple in the 1640s, published a
description of it, and exhibited it — in Amsterdam and later in London, where it
attracted considerable attention. Gerhard Schott's enormous Hamburg model of the
1690s was built to serve, among other things, as an opera set, and toured.

By the eighteenth century the Temple was a **public exhibition object**: something
a literate person in a European city might have paid to walk around.

---

### The claim about Leon, stated carefully

Leon Templo's model and engravings are frequently asserted to have influenced
Masonic Temple imagery, and the assertion has a plausible route: the model was in
London, it was well known, and its imagery circulated.

Plausible is not demonstrated. What would be required is a Masonic document showing
a specific feature derived from Leon's reconstruction rather than from the shared
biblical source or from Villalpando's much more widely reproduced engravings. This
book has not found such a demonstration, and the claim should be rated **E4:
possible, repeatedly asserted, not evidenced.**

---

### What the disagreements show

Set the reconstructions beside one another and the pattern is immediate.

**Each one looks like the architecture of its own period.** Medieval diagrams are
schematic in the medieval manner. Villalpando's Temple is a Renaissance classical
palace. Eighteenth-century engravings are Palladian. Nineteenth-century
reconstructions, once Assyrian and Egyptian material began to be excavated, start
to look Near Eastern — and twentieth-century ones, once 'Ain Dara and comparable
sites were known, look like Syrian long-room temples.

That last shift is the only one driven by evidence rather than by taste, and it is
the reason Chapter 5's comparative findings carried weight.

**And each one is confident.** The engravings do not hedge. They give elevations
and sections and measurements, and they look exactly as authoritative as an
architectural drawing of a building one could go and visit.

This is the mechanism this Part exists to describe. **A drawing removes the
uncertainty that the text contains.** The text says the house was sixty cubits by
twenty; the drawing has to decide what the roof looked like, and once it has
decided, the roof is simply there, and the reader who sees only the drawing has no
way of knowing which parts were given and which were supplied.

Every argument in this book about the Temple's proportions, alignments, or hidden
geometry has to reckon with that. A great many such arguments have been conducted
on reconstructions rather than on the texts — which means they have measured
somebody's guess.
