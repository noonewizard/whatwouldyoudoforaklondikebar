# CHAPTER SIXTY-SEVEN
## The Temple of Villalpando

Between 1596 and 1604, a Jesuit mathematician and architect published in Rome
three folio volumes that determined what Europe saw when it imagined Solomon's
Temple, and did so for the next two hundred years.

The reconstruction is wrong. It is wrong in an interesting way, its wrongness was
argued for rather than assumed, and it shaped the visual imagination that
Freemasonry's founding generation inherited.

---

### The work

Juan Bautista Villalpando, working initially with his fellow Jesuit Jerónimo
Prado, produced a commentary on the book of Ezekiel with an extensive apparatus on
the city and Temple of Jerusalem. Prado died during the project; Villalpando
completed the architectural volumes.

The work was produced under Spanish royal patronage. Philip II, whose Escorial was
itself a Solomonic project (Chapter 57), supported it, and the intellectual
climate of that court — where the identification of the Spanish monarchy with
Solomon's was explicit — is its immediate context.

Its physical form matters. These are large, lavish, engraved folios: plans,
elevations, sections, perspectives, details of the orders. They were reproduced,
copied, adapted, and pirated for two centuries.

---

### The argument

Villalpando's reconstruction rests on three claims, and they have to be taken in
order to see what he was doing.

**First: Ezekiel's visionary temple is a description of Solomon's actual Temple.**
This is the foundational move and it is the fatal one. Ezekiel 40–43 provides by
far the most detailed dimensional material available, and Villalpando needed
dimensions. So he identified the vision with the building — treating the prophet
as having been shown, in vision, the structure that had stood.

Modern scholarship does not accept this. Ezekiel's temple is a visionary and
programmatic structure, differing from the first Temple in plan, scale, and
arrangement; it describes something not yet built, in a book concerned with
restoration. Building the Solomonic Temple from it is building the wrong thing.

**Second: the Temple was designed under divine instruction, and therefore its
proportions are perfect.** This follows from his theology and was not controversial
in his context.

**Third: the classical orders originated at the Temple.** Here is the ambitious
claim. If the Temple was divinely proportioned, and if the orders of Greek and
Roman architecture embody the perfection of proportion, then the orders must
descend from the Temple — and Greek architecture is a degraded memory of
revelation.

Villalpando's engravings accordingly render the Temple as a monumental classical
building: colonnaded courts, entablatures, orders correctly deployed, the whole
thing recognizably of a piece with the finest architecture of sixteenth-century
Rome.

---

### The circularity

The argument's structure is worth setting out plainly, because it is an
exceptionally clear specimen of a mechanism this book has encountered repeatedly.

Villalpando believed the classical orders were perfect. He believed the Temple was
perfect. He therefore reconstructed the Temple using the classical orders. He then
presented the resulting reconstruction as evidence that the orders came from the
Temple.

The conclusion was assumed in the drawing and then read off the drawing. Nothing
external constrained any step: Ezekiel does not specify orders, capitals,
entablatures, or any classical element whatever. Villalpando supplied them from his
conviction and recovered his conviction from what he had supplied.

Chapter 72 is devoted to this mechanism under the name of retrofitting. Villalpando
is its monument — and, importantly, he was not being dishonest. He was reasoning
from premises he held sincerely, in a period whose standards he met. That is what
makes him the right case study. **Retrofitting does not require bad faith. It
requires only a conviction and an underdetermined object.**

---

### Why it mattered anyway

Because the pictures were magnificent, and pictures travel.

For two centuries, Europeans who thought about Solomon's Temple were, largely,
thinking about Villalpando's engravings or derivatives of them. When eighteenth-
century Masonic writers and artists invoked the Temple, the image available to
their audience descended from those plates. Chapter 66's point applies with full
force: a drawing removes uncertainty, and this drawing removed a very great deal of
it, in a direction that was wrong.

It also did something more specific and more consequential for this book. **It
established that the Temple was the origin of architectural knowledge** — not as a
devotional sentiment but as an argued scholarly thesis, in Latin, with plates, from
a Jesuit mathematician. Chapter 31 found Anderson making structurally the same
claim for the craft of building in 1723. Anderson need not have read Villalpando to
inherit the idea; by then it was in the air that educated Europeans breathed.

---

### The one who dissented

Villalpando's reconstruction was not universally accepted, and its most formidable
critic is the subject of the next chapter. Isaac Newton rejected the classical
Temple, worked from different sources, and reached a different plan — for reasons
that reveal a great deal about both men and about what the Temple was for in the
early modern mind.
