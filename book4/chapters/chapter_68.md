# CHAPTER SIXTY-EIGHT
## Newton's Temple

Isaac Newton spent a substantial part of his life on the Temple of Solomon. This
is true, it is often stated in a misleading way, and the accurate version is more
interesting than the misleading one.

---

### What survives

**Published.** *The Chronology of Ancient Kingdoms Amended*, issued in 1728, the
year after Newton's death, contains a chapter on the Temple with a plan and a
discussion of its dimensions.

**Separately published.** A dissertation on the sacred cubit of the Jews appeared
in English in 1737. Its concern is metrological: establishing the length of the
cubit used in the Temple's construction.

**Unpublished.** A far larger body of manuscript material survives, principally in
the collections dispersed at the 1936 Sotheby's sale of Newton's non-scientific
papers — now chiefly in the Yahuda collection at the National Library of Israel
and in the Babson collection. It includes extensive work on the Temple, on
prophecy, on chronology, and on theology, and much of it remained essentially
unexamined until the later twentieth century.

Newton learned enough Hebrew to work with the Mishnah's tractate Middot directly,
rather than relying on Latin intermediaries. Whatever one makes of the project,
the scholarly effort was real.

---

### What he was actually doing

Not looking for a hidden geometrical code, and not, on any evidence this book has
found, looking for anything esoteric in the modern sense.

Newton's Temple work serves two purposes that are continuous with the rest of his
non-scientific labor.

**Chronology.** Newton believed the received chronologies of antiquity were wrong
and had inflated the age of pagan civilizations at the expense of the biblical
record. Correcting them required fixed points, and the Temple — a datable structure
in a datable reign, described in a text he trusted — was a fixed point.

**Prophecy.** He read the prophetic books as an encoded account of the history of
the Church, to be decoded by establishing the correct correspondence between
prophetic figures and historical events. The Temple, as the setting and vocabulary
of a great deal of prophetic imagery, had to be understood accurately for the
decoding to work.

The sacred cubit dissertation is a good illustration of the method. Establishing
the cubit's length is not mysticism; it is an attempt to fix a unit so that
dimensions given in ancient texts can be converted into a known measure and
compared with one another.

**He rejected Villalpando's reconstruction**, working instead from Ezekiel and
Middot on his own terms and producing a plan that does not dress the building in
classical orders.

---

### What he did not find

This is the part worth dwelling on.

The most formidable mathematician then living studied the Temple's dimensions for
decades, with access to the texts, the languages, and the reconstruction
literature, and with every motivation to find profundity in the numbers.

He came away with a cubit length and a chronology.

No golden ratio. No encoded constant. No geometrical revelation. Newton's Temple
conclusions are metrological and historical, and where he made claims that later
scholarship rejects — the chronology has not survived — they are wrong in the
ordinary way that seventeenth-century historical work is wrong, not in the manner
of a suppressed discovery.

Anyone proposing that the Temple's measurements encode a mathematical secret should
reckon with this. It is not decisive; absence of a finding by one investigator
proves nothing in general. But it is the strongest available piece of negative
evidence, because the investigator was Newton, the effort was enormous, and the
result is on the record.

---

### The Masonic claim

Newton is regularly described as a Freemason. **There is no evidence that he was
one**, and the chronology makes it implausible: he died in March 1727, a decade
after the traditional founding of the Premier Grand Lodge, at an age and in a
condition that make an initiation in his last years unlikely and unattested.

What is true, and is the grain of fact the claim grows from, is that Newton's
scientific circle and the early Grand Lodge overlapped in personnel.

John Theophilus Desaguliers was Newton's demonstrator of experiments at the Royal
Society, a significant Newtonian popularizer, and the third Grand Master of the
Premier Grand Lodge in 1719. William Stukeley, the antiquary, was a Newton
associate who wrote memoirs of him, and recorded his own initiation as a Mason in
1721 — noting, revealingly, that he was drawn to it by curiosity about the
mysteries of the ancients.

These are genuine connections and they establish something real: **early
speculative Freemasonry and the Newtonian scientific establishment drew on the same
London social world.** That is a useful historical finding about the milieu in
which the Grand Lodge formed.

It is not evidence about Newton, and it certainly is not evidence that Newton's
Temple studies had a Masonic motivation. The Temple work predates the Grand Lodge,
proceeds from purposes documented in his own manuscripts, and requires no Masonic
explanation. Supplying one would be exactly the error this book's method exists to
prevent: taking an overlap of milieu for a transmission of content.

---

### What the case demonstrates

Two things, and they are the reason this chapter is in the book.

**The Temple was a serious scholarly object in the early modern period, not a
fringe one.** Newton, Villalpando, Arias Montano, and a long line of others treated
its reconstruction as legitimate learned work, on a par with chronology and
textual criticism. The modern association of Temple-measurement with crankery is a
later development.

**And serious inquiry into it produced ordinary results.** That is the finding.
When the Temple's dimensions were studied by someone with the mathematical
capacity to find anything that was there, what turned up was a unit of length and
a disputed chronology — which is what you get when you measure a building
description carefully and honestly.
