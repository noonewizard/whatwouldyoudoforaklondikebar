# CHAPTER SIXTY-NINE
## The Temple in Enlightenment Thought

Freemasonry was assembled in the same decades, in the same cities, and to a
considerable degree among the same people as the early Enlightenment. Chapter 68
found the Royal Society and the Grand Lodge sharing a social world. This chapter
asks what that world thought the Temple was.

---

### Not a period of secularization

The first correction, and it governs everything else.

The eighteenth century did not set religion aside in favor of reason. It argued
about religion incessantly, with reason as the instrument. Newton spent more
manuscript pages on prophecy and chronology than on physics. Biblical chronology
was a live scholarly field. Natural theology — the argument from the world's design
to a designer — was the period's most characteristic intellectual product, and it
made God into an architect as a matter of course.

The Temple fits this world precisely. It is a building described in scripture,
with dimensions, commissioned by a king famous for wisdom, and widely believed to
have been designed under divine instruction. It is simultaneously a religious
object and an object of measurement — and the eighteenth century liked nothing
better than an object that was both.

---

### Three things the period wanted from it

**A chronological anchor.** Ancient chronology was genuinely unsettled, and
synchronizing biblical, Egyptian, Greek, and Assyrian records was a major scholarly
program. The Temple's construction, tied to a reign, was a fixed point people
argued over. Chapter 68 showed Newton doing exactly this.

**An origin for architecture.** Villalpando's thesis, that the classical orders
descend from the Temple, circulated widely in a period intensely interested in
where architectural rules come from. The eighteenth century's architectural
theorists debated origins constantly — the primitive hut, the classical orders,
the Gothic — and a divinely specified building was a strong candidate.

**A model of rational religion.** This is the subtlest and the most important for
this book. The Temple's design was held to be reasonable: proportioned, ordered,
and intelligible. A religion whose central building could be measured and
understood was a religion compatible with a century that wanted faith to be
reasonable.

Anderson's first Charge, asking for the religion in which all men agree, belongs to
that program — and the Temple was an unusually good emblem for it, being
pre-Christian, shared, and specified in numbers rather than in doctrines.

---

### The Temple and the deist question

A caution is needed here, because the territory is frequently overclaimed in both
directions.

Early Grand Lodge Masonry has been described as a vehicle for deism, and as a
bulwark against it. The documents support neither strongly. Anderson's Charge is
brief and its interpretation has been argued about ever since; the Old Charges it
replaced were explicitly Trinitarian, as Chapter 32 noted, and the change is real
and significant. What it was *for* is less clear than confident accounts on either
side suggest.

The most defensible reading is institutional rather than theological. A fraternity
recruiting in a country that had recently fought over religion, and that was
about to spread across confessional lines, needed a basis for admission that did
not require settling those questions. Whether the men who drafted it held deist
convictions is a separate question from what the formula was designed to
accomplish, and the second is answerable while the first largely is not.

---

### Sacred geometry, before it became what it is now

The phrase is modern, and the eighteenth-century version of the idea is worth
distinguishing from the twentieth-century one.

For the eighteenth century, geometry's sacredness lay in its being **the science
of order** — the discipline by which a rational creator's world could be measured
and understood, and by which buildings could be made well. This is the geometry of
the Old Charges, of Euclid, of practical construction, elevated into a liberal art.
It is what Masonic instruction means by geometry: the letter G stands, in most
accounts given within the tradition itself, for geometry, understood as the noblest
of sciences.

The modern usage — sacred geometry as a system of hidden ratios encoding spiritual
truths, with φ at its center — is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century construction,
as Chapter 56 dated it, and it is a different idea wearing an older phrase.

Confusing them produces a specific and common historical error: reading
eighteenth-century Masonic enthusiasm for geometry as evidence of an occult
numerical doctrine, when what it evidences is admiration for Euclid.

---

### What the century handed on

A Temple that was scholarly, measurable, architecturally foundational, religiously
unifying, and — critically — **not occult**.

Everything this book's later Parts examine under the heading of esoteric Temple
doctrine was applied afterward, by identifiable people, beginning in the nineteenth
century. The Temple that entered Freemasonry in the 1720s came from a world of
chronologists, architects, natural theologians, and men who thought geometry was
the finest thing the mind could do.

That is a less exciting origin than the alternatives. It has the advantage of being
what the documents show.
