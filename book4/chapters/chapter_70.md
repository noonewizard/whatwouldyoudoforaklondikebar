# CHAPTER SEVENTY
## The Temple in Modern Occultism

By the end of the nineteenth century the Temple had become something it had never
been before: a general-purpose symbol, detachable from Judaism, from Christianity,
and from Freemasonry, available to any system that wanted a name for its inner
structure.

This chapter traces how that happened and what it cost.

---

### The route

Chapter 59 established the hinge and Chapter 54 the system. The distribution
network is worth setting out separately, because it explains the reach.

**Theosophy.** Helena Blavatsky's *Isis Unveiled* (1877) and *The Secret Doctrine*
(1888) proposed a single ancient wisdom underlying all religions, of which
particular traditions are fragments. This framework is the solvent: once every
tradition is a fragment of one original, the Temple can be read as an expression of
a universal principle rather than as a Jewish sanctuary. Theosophy's international
reach carried that framework further than any previous esoteric movement.

**The Golden Dawn and its successors.** The correspondence system, the grade
structure, and the ritual method passed to a long line of orders and to
individuals. Aleister Crowley's *777* (1909) tabulated the correspondences; Dion
Fortune's *The Mystical Qabalah* (1935) explained them; Israel Regardie's
publication of the Order's papers from 1937 put the working material into general
circulation.

**Twentieth-century organizations.** Paul Foster Case's Builders of the Adytum,
from the 1920s, named itself for a temple's inner chamber. The American Rosicrucian
order AMORC, founded in 1915, built substantially and named its buildings
accordingly. A great many groups since have used *Temple* as the name for their
premises, their working group, or the organization itself.

**Modern paganism.** The ritual structures Gerald Gardner and his contemporaries
assembled from the 1950s drew directly on Golden Dawn and Masonic materials — the
circle, the quarters, the graded initiations, the officers — carrying the
vocabulary into traditions with no Jewish or Christian reference at all.

**Popular culture.** The Temple, Masonic secrecy, and hidden knowledge became a
durable commercial genre. Dan Brown's *The Lost Symbol* (2009) is set substantially
in the House of the Temple in Washington — the Scottish Rite headquarters that
Chapter 65 noted is modelled on a Greek tomb.

---

### The mechanism

Why the Temple and not some other symbol?

Because of a property this book has been circling since Part I. **The Temple's
capacity is a function of its vacancy.**

It has a plan, which makes it usable: courts, hall, sanctuary; an outside, an
inside, an innermost. It has parts with names, which makes it mappable. It has an
order of access, which makes it a curriculum.

And it has no contents that anyone can check. The building is gone. The furnishings
are gone. The service is not performed. Its practitioners are not available to
object to what is said about it, and — crucially — **no living institution owns
it.** A modern occultist appropriating the Temple is not appropriating a church's
building or a living order's ritual. There is nobody with standing to say that is
not what it means.

Compare what happens to symbols that are still owned. Christian liturgical material
taken into occult systems generates objection; living indigenous traditions
appropriated by outsiders generate objection, and rightly. The Temple generates
almost none, because the people to whom it most belongs lost it two thousand years
ago and hold it now as an absence.

That is the uncomfortable observation this chapter has to make. The Temple's
availability as a universal symbol rests on a catastrophe, and its usefulness to
systems with no connection to Judaism is a consequence of Judaism's dispossession
of it.

---

### What is lost in the generalization

Three things, and naming them is not an attack on anyone's practice.

**Specificity.** A temple in general is not the Temple. Once the symbol denotes any
inner structure, it no longer carries the particulars — the sacrifices, the
priesthood, the calendar, the covenant — that gave the original its meaning. What
remains is a floor plan.

**History.** The generalized Temple has no date. It is presented as timeless, which
means its actual history — Parts II through VII of this book — becomes invisible,
and with it the possibility of asking when anything was added.

**Loss.** This is the sharpest. The historical Temple is defined by having been
destroyed; the mourning for it is a continuous religious practice with liturgy
attached. The occult Temple is not in mourning. It is a diagram of the self, and
diagrams are not bereaved.

---

### A fair statement

None of the above establishes that modern occult Temple symbolism is worthless or
that its users are frauds. Symbols migrate; traditions borrow; every tradition in
this book is made of borrowed material, including the ones doing the complaining.
The Christian appropriation of the Temple in the first century was not gentler than
the occult one in the nineteenth.

What can be asked of anyone using the symbol is accuracy about provenance. The
system is not ancient. It was assembled between 1854 and roughly 1935 by people
whose names, books, and publication dates are known. It draws on genuine Jewish
material at several removes and through Christian intermediaries with a thesis.

Knowing this makes the symbol no less usable. It makes the user honest, and it
makes the claims testable — which is the whole argument of this book applied to
one case.
