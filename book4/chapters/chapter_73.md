# CHAPTER SEVENTY-THREE
## Geometry and Coincidence

This chapter reports a computation in full, including the parts that did not go
the way this book might have preferred.

The claim under test is the most widely repeated numerical assertion about
Solomon's Temple: that its dimensions encode the golden ratio, φ, approximately
1.618.

---

### The method, fixed in advance

Stated before any number was examined:

Take every dimension explicitly given for the Temple in 1 Kings 6–7. Compute
**every ordered pairwise ratio** — not a selection, all of them. Count a hit when a
ratio falls within **five per cent** of φ. The tolerance and the procedure were
fixed before the run.

Testing all pairs rather than chosen pairs matters. A claim that "the Temple's
length relates to its height as φ" is a claim about two numbers somebody selected.
Testing all pairs asks the honest question: how easy is it to find φ *anywhere* in
this set?

The dimension list is the eleven values of Chapter 71: 60, 20, 30, 20, 10, 20, 20,
20, 5, 6, 7. Eleven values give a hundred and ten ordered pairs.

---

### The first result

**The Temple set produces one hit in a hundred and ten pairs**: 10/6 = 1.6667, an
error of 3.01 per cent against φ.

A reader encountering that in isolation might find it suggestive. A ratio within
three per cent of the golden section, in the Temple's own stated dimensions.

This is exactly where most published treatments stop. It is also exactly where the
work begins.

---

### The controls

An illustrative single control was run first: an ordinary Iron Age four-room house
footprint, structurally unremarkable, with no golden-ratio claim attached to it
anywhere. It produced one hit in fifty-six pairs — 11/7 = 1.5714, an error of 2.88
per cent.

**The control's hit was closer to φ than the Temple's.**

One control is an anecdote, so the test was then run systematically. Ten named
comparison sets were assembled, each of eleven values to match the Temple's
cardinality exactly: an Iron Age house, 'Ain Dara, the Tabernacle, Ezekiel's
temple, the ark of Genesis, the Parthenon, the Pantheon, Stonehenge's sarsen
circle, a typical Gothic bay, and — as a deliberate absurdity — the integers one
through eleven. Alongside them, twenty thousand synthetic sets of eleven random
integers drawn from the Temple's own range.

The results:

| Set | Hits in 110 pairs |
|---|---|
| **Solomon's Temple** | **1** |
| Ten named comparison sets | mean 3.20, median 3, range 1–6 |
| Sets scoring at or above the Temple | **10 of 10** |
| 20,000 random sets scoring at or above the Temple | **95.66%** |

---

### Reading the result honestly

**The Temple did not merely fail to stand out. It came last.**

Every one of the ten comparison sets matched or beat it. The integers one through
eleven — a sequence with no building, no design, and no intention behind it —
produced four hits to the Temple's one. Ninety-six per cent of random number sets
of the same size and range did at least as well.

The method's null distribution, in other words, produces the Temple's result almost
all of the time. A finding that a control reproduces at a comparable rate is not a
finding. It is a description of the search.

---

### Why the Temple underperforms

This diagnostic was run *after* seeing the result and is flagged as post-hoc. It is
an explanation of an observed number, not a prediction that was made in advance,
and it should be weighed accordingly.

The Temple's eleven stated values collapse to only **seven distinct values** — 20
occurs four times, being the house's width and all three dimensions of the Holy of
Holies. Its hundred and ten ordered pairs therefore reduce to only **thirty-five
distinct ratios**, and those ratios are dominated by clean small integers: 3, 2,
1.5, 0.5, 6. Values of that kind sit a long way from 1.618.

A random set of eleven integers has more distinct values, hence more distinct
ratios, hence more chances to approach φ.

So the Temple scores below the controls for a reason that is the chapter's own
argument turned around. **Its dimensions are not merely non-golden. They are the
kind of numbers that make the golden ratio hard to hit** — because they are the
whole-number, rope-and-peg proportions of a building set out by counting, which is
the opposite of what a design around an irrational ratio would produce.

---

### The general rule

Three sentences, and they apply to every numerical claim in this book's subject.

**A positive result without a control is not a result.** The question is never
whether a pattern can be found; patterns can always be found. The question is
whether it is found more often here than in material that certainly does not
contain it.

**Tolerance must be declared before the search.** Five per cent around φ is a band
from 1.538 to 1.699, which is wide. A researcher who settles on the tolerance after
seeing the data has tested every tolerance.

**The search space must be reported.** One hit in a hundred and ten pairs is a
completely different statement from one hit in one pair, and only the second would
be interesting. Almost all published golden-ratio claims report the numerator and
suppress the denominator.

---

### What would have changed the verdict

It is worth saying what evidence this book would have accepted, so that the
conclusion does not look unfalsifiable.

A text from the relevant period stating a proportional intention. A ratio appearing
across many independent buildings of the same tradition at a rate significantly
above the control rate. A dimension set whose φ-approximations were systematically
tighter than chance allows. Or a single ratio so precise — a fraction of a per cent
— that coincidence became implausible even in a large search space.

None of these exists for Solomon's Temple. What exists is one hit in a hundred and
ten, beaten by the numbers one through eleven.
