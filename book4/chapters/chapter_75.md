# CHAPTER SEVENTY-FIVE
## "Letter and Halve It"

Book III established what this phrase describes and this chapter does not re-derive
it. The finding, in one sentence: **it is a delivery convention, not an encoding
rule** — a practice in which a word is communicated between two people
alternately, letter by letter, so that neither says it whole.

Book III rated that **E3**, with a source-verification flag, and the rating stands.

What this chapter adds is the Temple-specific extension, because the same
misreading has been applied to Temple vocabulary with the same results.

---

### Why the practice is not a cipher

A cipher has a rule its users state and share. The rule exists independently of any
particular message, it can be written down, and two competent users applying it to
the same plaintext produce the same ciphertext.

"Letter and halve it" has no such rule, in any source, ever. What the sources
describe — where they describe anything — is a manner of communication: the word
passes between two people who take turns, and it is not uttered whole by either.

The four-step mechanism by which this became a decipherment key was set out in
Book III and recurs here verbatim in structure:

A technical term is met without its context, by someone who has never seen the
exchange performed. A familiar category is supplied — *halve* sounds like an
operation, and the reader knows what ciphers are. A rule is invented to fit, since
none is stated: split at the midpoint, interleave, reverse one half, take alternate
letters. And the output is read as confirmation, because any rule applied to a
short word produces something, and something was what was wanted.

**The diagnostic is the most useful sentence in this Part.** If the rule has to be
reconstructed by the person claiming the decipherment, and if two such people
reconstruct it differently — which they do — then there is no cipher. There is a
text and several readers.

---

### The Temple version

The same treatment has been applied to the Temple's own vocabulary, and the
proposals have the same shape.

The pillar names, being two, invite splitting and recombination. The consonantal
skeletons of Hebrew words invite rearrangement. The Holy of Holies, written as a
word repeated in two forms, invites treatment as a doubled key. Jerusalem, Zion,
Hiram, and Solomon have all been subjected to halvings, interleavings, and
reversals in the popular literature, with results that are presented as recovered
meaning.

Every one of these proposals fails the same test, and it is test three of the
protocol: **the encoding rule is not stated precisely enough that another person
would produce the same output.** The interpreter supplies the rule. Supplying the
rule and then reporting the output as a finding is a closed circuit.

Chapter 74's run is the relevant comparison. Atbash was tested here rather than
dismissed precisely because it *does* have a stated rule — one rule, unambiguous,
attested in the Hebrew Bible. That is what a real technique looks like, and it
returned nothing.

---

### What the practice actually accomplishes

Having established what it is not, it is worth saying what it is, because the
practice is genuinely interesting and Book III's analysis of it applies directly
to this book's Temple material.

**It is a challenge-response protocol.** Neither party can produce the word alone;
each must supply the correct next letter given what the other has just said. A
person who does not know the word cannot bluff through it, because they cannot
anticipate the alternation.

**It marks the word as not ordinary.** A thing that may not be said whole is
thereby declared to be of a different order from things that may — and the practice
performs that status every time it is used, which is more durable than asserting
it.

**And it requires two people.** The word, in its proper form, exists only between
persons. It cannot be rehearsed alone.

That last property is why this chapter belongs in a book about the Temple. Chapter
47 found the Temple functioning as a machine for distributing visual access, and
Chapter 63 noted that its innermost room could be entered by one person once a
year. A word that only exists between two people and a room that only one person
may enter are solving the same problem from opposite directions: **how to make
something real and available without making it common.**

The Temple did it with walls. The practice does it with turn-taking. Neither is a
cipher, and reading either as one loses what is actually happening.

---

### A closing note on the evidence rating

E3, with a flag, is a modest rating, and it should stay modest.

The sources for this practice are thin, the earliest are contested, and the
interpretation above — that it is reverential and protocol-like rather than
cryptographic — rests on how the printed literature explains it where it explains
it at all, which is not often.

A book that has spent this Part insisting on controls and declared tolerances
cannot then assert a favoured reading of a thin source at E1 because the reading is
attractive. The honest statement is that this is the best available account of a
practice we know less about than we would like.
