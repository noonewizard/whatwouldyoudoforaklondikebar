# CHAPTER SEVENTY-SIX
## Pigpen and Masonic Ciphers

The geometric substitution cipher in which letters are replaced by the fragments
of grid and cross that enclose them is commonly called the Masonic cipher, or the
Freemason's cipher, or pigpen.

Book III examined it and its finding is carried here unchanged: **it should not be
called the Masonic cipher without documentary support for the attribution.** This
chapter states what is documented, what is not, and why the cryptography matters
for this book's argument about the Temple.

---

### What the system is

Letters are arranged in grids — typically two tic-tac-toe grids and two X shapes —
and each letter is represented by the shape of the compartment it sits in, with a
dot distinguishing the second grid from the first.

That is the entire system.

---

### The cryptographic assessment

It is worth stating formally, because the formal statement settles the historical
question.

**The key space has size one.** There is no key. There are no variable parameters,
nothing chosen at encryption time, nothing an opponent must guess. Knowing the
system is knowing the message.

Auguste Kerckhoffs stated the governing principle in 1883: a cryptographic system
must remain secure even when everything about it except the key is public
knowledge. Pigpen has no key, so it has nothing to protect. Every printing of the
grids — and they have been printed constantly, for well over a century, in puzzle
books, children's activity books, and works on Freemasonry — destroys the secrecy
of every message ever written in it, retroactively and permanently.

It is also a **monoalphabetic substitution**, which means that even without the
grids it yields immediately to frequency analysis on any text of moderate length.
The technique for breaking such ciphers was described in the ninth century.

**Therefore pigpen was never a security system.** Whatever it was for, it was not
for keeping information from a determined reader, and any account that treats it as
a means of concealment has misidentified its function at the outset.

---

### What is documented

The attribution question, stated carefully.

Masonic use is documented. The system appears in Masonic contexts from the
eighteenth century onward — in records, on monuments, and in printed works, and
some Masonic gravestones carry inscriptions in it. That much is not in dispute.

Masonic *origin* is a different claim and it is much weaker. The system has been
connected to earlier arrangements, including a Hebrew letter-arrangement scheme
sometimes called the nine chambers, and it has also been called the Rosicrucian
cipher. [SOURCE VERIFICATION REQUIRED: the earliest datable description of the
grid system, and the route by which it reached eighteenth-century Masonic use, are
given differently in different sources and should be checked before any claim of
priority is made.]

The honest position is: **documented Masonic use, undocumented Masonic invention.**
A tradition using a cipher is not evidence that the tradition devised it, and the
name a cipher acquires reflects who used it visibly, not who made it.

---

### What it was actually for

Three functions, none of them secrecy, all of them consistent with the evidence.

**Marking.** A message in pigpen announces that it is a message of a particular
kind, by a particular sort of person. On a gravestone this is the entire point: the
inscription is meant to be noticed as belonging to a tradition, and a passer-by who
cannot read it has still received the message that there is one.

**Convenience of the ordinary kind.** A simple substitution keeps casual eyes off
a note, in the way a locked desk drawer does — not against an adversary, against
idle glances.

**Play.** This should not be underrated. A system of shapes that turns writing into
drawing is enjoyable to use, and people have always enjoyed it. That it has spent
the twentieth century mainly in children's puzzle books is not a degradation of its
purpose. It may be quite close to its purpose.

---

### Why this belongs in a book about the Temple

Because the misreading is identical, and Part XV has now documented it four times.

Chapter 71 found the Temple's dimensions taken for a code. Chapter 73 found the
golden ratio read into eleven numbers that cannot support it. Chapter 75 found a
delivery convention reconstructed as an encryption rule. And here, a marker is
taken for a concealment device.

In every case the same mistake: **a thing that is distinctive is assumed to be
hidden, and a thing that is hidden is assumed to be hiding something.**

The Temple's veil is the founding instance. A room that nobody may enter is
assumed to contain a secret worth the exclusion, and the exclusion is read as
evidence of the contents. But the exclusion is the institution. Restricted access
does not imply concealed information; it establishes a structure of authority, of
attention, and of who may speak for what is inside. Chapters 82 and 83 develop
this directly.

A cipher with no key, a room with no contents anyone can report, and a set of
dimensions with no encoded ratio are all the same object seen from different
angles. They are boundaries — and the persistent error of the entire literature
this book examines is to mistake a boundary for a container.
