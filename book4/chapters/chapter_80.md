# CHAPTER EIGHTY
## Architecture as Interface

This Part uses a vocabulary from computing to describe buildings. The vocabulary
is **an analytical analogy and nothing more** — a way of making certain features of
sacred architecture visible that other descriptions leave implicit.

The standing caution applies to all four chapters and is stated once here. Nobody
in the ancient world was thinking in these terms. The Temple was not designed as an
information system, its builders had no such concept, and finding computational
structure in it says something about the analytic vocabulary, not about the
builders' intentions. This is precisely the error Chapter 72 named as retrofitting,
and the only defence against committing it is to declare the analogy as an analogy
and keep declaring it.

With that said, the analogy earns its place, because it makes one thing precise
that is otherwise hard to state.

---

### What an interface is

An interface is the surface at which two systems meet and through which all
exchange between them passes. It has three properties worth carrying over.

**It determines what operations are possible.** Not what is desirable or
intended — what can be done at all. An interface that offers three actions permits
three actions.

**It conceals its implementation.** The user of an interface does not need to know
what lies behind it, and generally cannot find out from the interface itself.

**It is where the system's design becomes the user's experience.** Everything a
designer decided reaches the user only through this surface.

---

### The Temple read this way

The Temple's plan was, in these terms, an interface specification of unusual
clarity.

**Available operations were enumerated and graded.** One could enter the outer
court, or not. One could bring an offering, of specified kinds, at specified times,
and hand it across at a specified point. One could go no further than one's
category permitted. The building did not merely discourage other actions; for most
people it made them physically impossible, which is the strongest form an
interface constraint takes.

**The implementation was concealed.** What happened in the innermost room was not
observable. Chapter 47 traced the consequence: description substituted for sight,
and description was carried by people, which generated authority as a by-product of
the plan.

**And the design reached the worshipper only through the surface.** Whatever the
Temple's theology, an ordinary person's access to it was the court, the queue, the
smoke, the sound, and the handing over of an animal. The building was the argument
as far as almost everyone was concerned.

---

### The lodge read this way

The same reading applies, and Chapter 47 anticipated the finding: the lodge
achieves comparable interface properties with almost no architecture.

The available operations are enumerated by the ritual and enforced by officers
rather than by walls. A candidate may do what the ceremony provides for and
nothing else, and he is moved by people whose function is to move him. The
implementation is concealed in the sense that the candidate does not know what
comes next. And the design reaches him only through the sequence of things that
are said and done to him.

**Substituting procedure for architecture is the whole of what the lodge did**, and
saying it in interface terms makes clear why it worked without a building: the
constraints were never in the masonry. They were in the specification, and a
specification is portable.

---

### What the analogy makes visible

Three things that the ordinary symbolic description tends to bury.

**Access is prior to meaning.** Whatever the Temple signified, the first thing it
did was determine who could go where. Symbolic readings of the building — the
cosmic, the moral, the mystical — are all downstream of a set of facts about
permitted movement, and those facts are the same in every reading.

**Concealment is structural, not informational.** This has been the recurring
finding of the last three Parts. The veil does not conceal a fact; it constitutes a
relation. A cipher with no key, examined in Chapter 76, does the same thing: it
marks rather than hides.

**And the interface can outlive the system.** When the building fell, the
specification survived in text, and it was the specification that everything
afterward worked with. Chapter 64 put it as an empire being able to demolish a
building but not a plan. This chapter is the same observation in a different
vocabulary: **what persisted was the interface, not the implementation** — and
nobody ever had access to the implementation anyway.

---

### The limit of the analogy

It should be said plainly where this way of speaking stops being useful.

It has nothing to say about what anybody felt. An interface description of a
temple captures constraint, sequence, and access perfectly and captures awe,
grief, devotion, and boredom not at all. Chapter 51's finding about memory and
Chapter 45's about moral formation cannot be stated in these terms.

It also flattens intention. Interfaces are designed; the Temple accreted, was
destroyed, was rebuilt differently, was expanded by a king with a legitimacy
problem, and was described by writers with theological programmes. Reading the
result as a specification implies a specifier who did not exist.

The analogy is a lens. It is held up deliberately, for three more chapters, and
then put down.
