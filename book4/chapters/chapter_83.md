# CHAPTER EIGHTY-THREE
## Architecture as Governance

The last chapter of this analogy, and the one where it does the most work, because
it names something that runs through every level of this book's five-level model.

A building that controls access is making decisions about people, and those
decisions do not require anyone to be present to enforce them.

---

### Five questions a sacred building answers

Every sacred structure in this book answers the same five questions, and its plan
*is* the answer.

**Who may enter?** The Temple's courts graded this by category. The lodge's door
grades it by membership.

**Who may approach?** Proximity was regulated separately from entry. One could be
inside the Temple precinct and still be a long way from the altar.

**Who may speak?** In the Temple, liturgical speech was restricted to those with
standing. In the lodge, the officers speak the ritual and the candidate answers in
prescribed forms.

**Who may touch?** The furnishings of the Temple were handled by specified persons
only, and the narratives about improper contact are among the sharpest in the
Hebrew Bible.

**Who may know?** This follows from the others, as Chapter 47 showed: where sight
is restricted, knowledge of what is inside becomes a report, and reporting is a
privilege.

---

### Governance without a governor

The essential property of architectural governance is that it operates
automatically.

A rule written in a book must be read, remembered, invoked, and enforced. A rule
built into a wall enforces itself, continuously, without anyone deciding to do so
on any particular occasion, and without the possibility of favor, error, or
mercy.

This is a genuine advantage from the institution's point of view and a genuine cost
from the person's. The wall does not know that this supplicant has traveled a long
way. It does not make exceptions. Its impartiality and its indifference are the
same property.

Chapter 46 made the point in the register of environmental psychology — that
buildings determine what is physically possible, and that this is the
best-supported thing architecture does to behavior. Governance is what that
determination looks like when the behaviors in question are social.

---

### The lodge's version, and its consequence

The lodge answers all five questions with procedure rather than with structure, as
Part VIII established. And the substitution has a consequence worth drawing out.

**Procedural governance is revisable in a way that architectural governance is
not.** A wall must be demolished; a rule may be amended. Masonic jurisdictions have
in fact amended a great deal over three centuries — ritual, qualifications,
recognition, the wording of obligations.

That revisability is why "Freemasonry teaches" is always a malformed sentence
without a jurisdiction and a date attached. A tradition governed by walls would
have a fixed answer. A tradition governed by procedure has as many answers as it
has governing bodies, which is a great many.

---

### The inner Temple's version

The five-level model's final level is the Temple within, and the governance
question can be asked there too, though it becomes metaphorical.

What may be admitted to attention? What may be approached? What may be spoken?
What may be touched? What may be known about oneself?

The traditions examined in Part IX all answer these, and they answer them with
architecture: an inner structure with rooms, an order of access, and a center.
Chapter 42 noted the limitation — a person is not a building, and the metaphor
imports a coherence that people do not have.

But it also noted what the metaphor does well, and governance is part of it. The
claim that a person's inner life has *rules of access* — that not everything is
available to everything else, that some things are approached and not entered — is
not obviously false, and it is the sort of claim a purely descriptive psychology
struggles to state.

---

### Putting the lens down

Four chapters of a deliberate analogy, and the promise was to set it aside at the
end.

What it made visible: that access is prior to meaning; that concealment can be
structural rather than informational; that the Temple's plan outlived its stones
because a specification is more portable than an implementation; that buildings
store well because minds store spatially; that Masonic secrecy is authentication
rather than exclusion; and that a plan governs automatically, without mercy and
without a governor.

What it could not touch: grief, awe, devotion, the experience of standing in a
place, the reason anyone cared. Every one of those is a larger part of this
subject than everything the analogy captured.

The last Part is about those.
