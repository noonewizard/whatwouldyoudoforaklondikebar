# CHAPTER EIGHTY-FOUR
## The Temple Destroyed

Everything in this book depends on a fact that is easy to state and easy to pass
over: the building is not there.

It was destroyed twice, by two empires, five and a half centuries apart, and the
second destruction was final. No tradition examined in these pages would exist in
its present form if either building were standing.

---

### 586 BCE

The Babylonian campaign against Judah ended with the capture of Jerusalem, the
burning of the Temple and the royal palace, the breaking of the city walls, and
the deportation of a substantial part of the population.

The event is attested in 2 Kings, in Jeremiah, in Lamentations, and — independently
of the biblical tradition — in Babylonian sources. It is among the best-established
facts in the whole of this book's subject matter. Chapter 62's chronology gives the
sequence; what matters here is what it did.

**It ended a system.** The sacrificial service required that building and no other.
A religion organized around offerings at a single authorized site, whose site has
been burned, faces a problem that no amount of piety resolves.

**It ended a dynasty's claim.** Chapter 61 set out the arrangement: Temple and
throne were two faces of one assertion. The assertion could not survive the
building.

**And it produced a literature.** Lamentations is a sustained poetic response to
this destruction. Psalm 137 is another. Ezekiel's visionary temple, which Chapter
67 found Villalpando mistaking for a description of the first, belongs to the
period of the exile and is a response to the loss — a building described by a man
who had seen the real one fall.

That last point is worth holding onto. **The Temple's most detailed
architectural description in the Hebrew Bible was written about a building that did
not exist, by someone in exile.** Ezekiel is the first imagined reconstruction, and
every one Chapter 66 surveyed descends from the same impulse.

---

### 70 CE

The Second Temple, rebuilt from 538 and massively expanded by Herod from around 20
BCE, was destroyed at the end of the First Jewish Revolt.

Josephus, who was present on the Roman side, describes the burning at length.
Chapter 64 followed the aftermath: the spoils paraded in Rome, the Arch of Titus
with its relief of the menorah carried in procession, the Temple tax redirected to
Jupiter Capitolinus.

**This destruction was different in kind**, for one reason. The first was followed
by a return and a rebuilding within seventy years. The second was not. The site was
refounded as a Roman colony; the sacrificial service ceased permanently; and the
religious world that depended on it had to reconstitute itself entirely.

Rabbinic Judaism is, among other things, the answer to that problem. Chapter 41
described the mechanism: Temple categories — sacrifice, priesthood, purity,
atonement — redirected onto prayer, study, the table, and the person. Christianity,
developing in the same period, made a different set of moves from the same
material, reading the Temple as fulfilled and superseded.

Both are responses to a building not being there.

---

### The date

Jewish tradition assigns both destructions to the ninth of the month of Av, and
observes that day as a fast.

Whether the two events actually fell on the same date is not historically
established, and this book will not assert that they did. What matters is what the
tradition did with the assertion: it **collapsed two catastrophes into one
anniversary**, creating a single recurring day on which the loss is not merely
remembered but re-entered.

That is a technology, and Chapter 85 examines it. A tradition that wants a loss to
survive across generations must give it a date, a liturgy, and a form of observance
— because without those, grief dies with the people who felt it.

---

### What was actually lost

It is worth being precise, because the tradition has not always been.

**A building**, whose physical remains have never been identified, as Chapter 63
established.

**A service**, which was specific, detailed, and cannot be performed elsewhere.
The Mishnah preserves its regulations in extraordinary detail.

**A priesthood's function.** Priestly descent continued to be tracked and carries
liturgical significance still, but the office's central activity ceased.

**A place of pilgrimage**, and with it a set of practices organized around
arriving somewhere.

**And a certainty about where God's presence was located.** This is the subtlest
loss and the one with the longest consequences. A religion with an address can
answer the question *where?* After 70 the question had to be answered differently,
and every answer this book has examined — the heavenly Temple, the inner Temple, the
community as Temple, the body as Temple — is a version of that answer.

---

### The unintended consequence

Rome's purpose in 70 was to end a cult by ending its address, and Chapter 64 noted
what actually happened instead.

Detached from a location, the Temple's categories became portable, and portable
things travel. Within four centuries the Temple had been made into a visionary
palace in Hekhalot literature, a type of the Church in Christian exegesis, a figure
of the soul in both, and a symbol available to anyone. Within seventeen it was the
organizing image of a fraternity in London.

**None of that could have happened to a building that was still standing.** A
standing Temple has custodians, and custodians have opinions, and opinions
constrain. The Temple became universal because it became vacant, and it became
vacant because it was burned.

That is an uncomfortable thing for this book to have found, and it will be stated
again at the end, because everything the remaining chapters describe rests on it.
