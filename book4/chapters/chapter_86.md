# CHAPTER EIGHTY-SIX
## The Temple Rebuilt in Imagination

Since 70 CE the Temple has been rebuilt continuously, by people who never intended
to lay a stone.

This chapter surveys where, briefly, because the scale of the phenomenon is the
point. A building that stood for a few centuries has been under construction in
the imagination for twenty.

---

### In text

The rebuilding begins inside the biblical corpus. Ezekiel's visionary temple —
composed, as Chapter 84 noted, by a man in exile who had seen the real one fall —
is the first, and it is the most dimensionally detailed description of a temple in
the Hebrew Bible. The Revelation of John ends with a city descending from heaven,
measured and gated, in which there is explicitly no temple.

Rabbinic literature rebuilt it as specification, in Middot and Kodashim. Hekhalot
literature rebuilt it as a sequence of heavenly palaces, as Chapter 52 described.
Christian exegesis rebuilt it typologically, its every furnishing corresponding to
something else.

Milton's *Paradise Lost* has Pandemonium raised by fallen angels in a single
night, a building explicitly compared with the works of human architects, and the
epic tradition generally keeps the Temple close to hand as the measure of what a
building can be.

### In paint and stone

Renaissance painters who needed a temple in a picture drew one, and what they drew
was the architecture of their own moment. The idealized centrally-planned temple
that appears in the backgrounds of Italian paintings of the Marriage of the Virgin
is a fifteenth-century architectural fantasy, not a reconstruction — and it is
nonetheless what a great many people's image of a biblical temple was formed from.

Churches were built as new temples, sometimes explicitly. The Escorial, in Chapter
57, was one. The identification of a church's parts with the Temple's — sanctuary,
holy place, veil — is standard Christian architectural symbolism, and it made every
substantial church a partial reconstruction.

### In model

Chapter 66 covered these and they deserve a second mention because they are the
literal case. Jacob Judah Leon's model in the 1640s; Gerhard Schott's enormous one
in Hamburg at the end of the century, built partly to serve as an opera set. People
paid to walk around a Temple that had been gone for sixteen centuries.

### In ritual

Freemasonry's version, which Parts VII and VIII documented: not a model but a
*declaration*, in which a room is stated to be the Temple and the statement is
accepted for the duration of a ceremony. Chapter 65 found that this required no
architectural resemblance whatever, and that the buildings Freemasonry actually
erected were Art Deco, Gothic, and Greek Revival.

### In system

The occult rebuilding of Chapters 54 and 70: the Temple as a diagram, its parts
mapped onto a correspondence system assembled between 1854 and the 1930s. And
Chapter 58's finding, worth repeating, that when the Hermetic tradition actually
constructed a room it built the Rosicrucian vault and not the Solomonic Temple.

### In popular culture

Films, novels, and games in which the Temple, its treasure, or its secret drives a
plot. Chapter 70 noted the most commercially successful recent instance is set in a
Masonic building modelled on a Greek tomb.

---

### The pattern across all of them

Every one of these reconstructions has three properties in common, and the
consistency is striking given how little else they share.

**Each is made of its own period's materials.** Ezekiel's temple has the
architectural vocabulary of the ancient Near East. Villalpando's has the classical
orders. The Renaissance painters' has Bramante's geometry. The Golden Dawn's has
Victorian Egyptology. Nobody has ever reconstructed the Temple in anything other
than the architecture they already knew.

**Each is more complete than the sources.** Chapter 66 stated the mechanism: a
drawing removes the uncertainty that a text contains. Every reconstruction is
therefore more confident than its evidence, and the confidence is invisible in the
result.

**And each serves a purpose that is not archaeological.** Ezekiel's serves a hope
of restoration. Villalpando's serves an argument about the origin of the orders.
The Masonic one serves a ceremony. The occult one serves a curriculum. None of them
was primarily trying to find out what the building looked like, and the two that
came closest — Newton's and the modern comparative work of Chapter 5 — are the two
least known.

---

### What this says about the original

Something that has been implicit for eighty-five chapters and should now be said
directly.

**We have no access to Solomon's Temple that is not a reconstruction.**

The biblical accounts are themselves composed centuries after the events they
describe, by writers with theological programmes, and they disagree with each other
about the numbers. There are no remains. There is no contemporary depiction. Every
image anyone has ever had of this building has been made by somebody, out of
something else, for a reason.

That is not a counsel of despair, and Part II did not conclude that nothing can be
said. Comparative archaeology constrains the possibilities; the texts, read
critically, yield real information; the honest range of what the building might
have been is narrower than total ignorance.

But the Temple as an object of imagination has never been in contact with the
Temple as a possible building. They have run in parallel for two thousand years,
and only one of them has done anything.
