# CHAPTER EIGHTY-NINE
## The Unfinished Temple

The most important thing about the Masonic Temple is that it is not finished, and
that nobody expects it to be.

This is unusual. Most symbolic structures in most traditions are complete — a
mandala, a cross, a tree, a city. An emblem that is permanently under construction
is a strange thing to organize an institution around, and it is worth asking what
it accomplishes.

---

### Incompletion as a practice, not a failure

Three instances from this book, none derived from the others.

**The Jewish custom** described in Chapter 85: leaving a portion of a house
unfinished or unplastered, deliberately and permanently, in remembrance of the
destruction. Incompletion here is a memorial. The gap is the message.

**The cathedral timescale.** Major medieval churches were built across periods
longer than a working life. A mason who began on a cathedral at twenty could be
certain of dying with it incomplete, and a great many cathedrals were in fact never
finished as designed — towers unbuilt, transepts truncated, west fronts left
plain. The modern instance everyone knows, the Sagrada Família, has been under
construction since 1882 and became a tourist attraction while unfinished.

**The Masonic emblem.** The rough ashlar, the work in progress, the building that
is being raised and is never reported as raised.

---

### What an unfinished structure does that a finished one cannot

**It does not expire.** A completed project has an end, and after the end there is
either maintenance or nothing. A structure understood as permanently under
construction supplies an indefinite amount of future work, which is precisely what
an institution intending to outlast its members requires.

**It accommodates the participant's actual condition.** Chapter 44 made this point
about the rough ashlar and it generalizes. A person invited to identify with a
finished structure is invited to a comparison they will lose. A person invited to
identify with a building under construction is invited to an accurate description
of themselves.

**It distributes credit and defers judgement.** No one person raises an unfinished
building, and no one moment settles whether it was well built. This is a
considerable kindness, and it is also, less comfortably, a considerable evasion —
a project that is never complete is never assessable.

**And it converts loss into a stage.** This is the deepest function and it connects
the three instances above. A building that was destroyed is a finished thing that
ended. A building that is unfinished has not ended; it is between states. Recasting
a ruin as a work in progress is the single move that makes the Temple's destruction
bearable, and Chapter 29 found the magical-construction legend performing exactly
the same operation by a different route.

---

### The cost

This book has tried to state the cost of every symbol it has credited, and this one
has a real one.

**Permanent incompletion can excuse permanent non-delivery.** An institution that
describes itself as always building is an institution that never has to show a
building. A moral programme that is by definition unfinishable cannot fail, because
failure requires a standard that a deadline supplies and this one has none.

Freemasonry has been susceptible to this. So has every tradition of gradual
self-improvement: the language of the journey, the process, the practice, the work,
all of which are true and all of which can be used to defer indefinitely the
question of whether anything has actually changed.

The honest version of the symbol has to keep the square in it. **Unfinished is not
the same as unassessable.** A partially built wall can still be tried for plumb,
and a course that is out of true is out of true whether or not the building is
done. Chapter 44's insistence that the Masonic tools are instruments of assessment
rather than production is the safeguard against this failure mode, and it is built
into the tradition's own equipment.

---

### Book III's ending, and this one

Book III concluded that the Lost Word is a device for holding a loss open without
resolving it — a way of saying *something is missing* that can be transmitted
across generations without the missing thing ever being supplied.

The unfinished Temple is the same device in the other register. The Word says the
essential thing is absent. The unfinished building says the essential thing is not
yet. Both refuse closure; both are transmissible precisely because they refuse it;
and a tradition can hold both at once, which Freemasonry does.

What the two have in common is the property this book has now arrived at from six
directions. **They are structures that cannot be completed, and therefore cannot be
finished with.**
