## WHAT IS WELL-SUPPORTED (E1–E2)

**Spatial cognition and wayfinding**: extensively studied. Humans build cognitive
maps of navigated space; landmark salience, sightlines, and sequential
room-to-room movement measurably affect navigation confidence and route memory.
Standard reference literature: Kevin Lynch's foundational urban-design work
(*The Image of the City*, 1960) and the subsequent environmental psychology
literature it spawned.

**Attention and architectural framing**: controlled sightlines (a single doorway
revealing a distant focal point, as opposed to an open plaza with many competing
foci) measurably direct visual attention — well-supported by basic visual
attention research (the same "clear a surface for the next thing to land on"
mechanism Book III ch.4 examined for silence, applied here to sightline).

**Threshold effects**: the psychological salience of boundary-crossing (doorways,
gates) has support in environmental psychology (the "doorway effect" literature on
event-segmentation in memory — moving through a doorway measurably disrupts and
re-segments working memory, a replicated if debated finding). Directly relevant to
ch.48.

**Light/dark contrast on attention**: well-established basic visual science — dark
adaptation, contrast sensitivity — reused from Book II ch.20's treatment of the
same mechanism in ritual darkness, now applied to architectural illumination
design specifically (ch.50).

## WHAT IS CONTESTED AND MUST CARRY ITS CONTESTATION (per Book II's standing
practice with Aronson & Mills and the "cathedral effect")

**"High ceilings promote abstract thinking"** (Meyers-Levy & Zhu, 2007) — already
flagged as replication-contested in Book II; reused here with the same caution,
now directly relevant to ch.49 (Height and Depth) and any claim that the Temple's
30-cubit height was "designed" to produce a cognitive effect research does not
reliably support.

**Specific "sacred architecture activates X brain region" claims** — this is
exactly the neuro-mysticism the master prompt prohibits. Where legitimate
neuroscience exists on ritual/religious experience generally (e.g., studies on
meditative or contemplative states), it concerns PRACTICE (sustained attention,
breath, repetition — Book II's actual subject), not ARCHITECTURE as such, and
claims that a building's proportions or geometry directly produce a specific
neurological state are, at the time of this research, **not supported by credible
peer-reviewed evidence** and must be reported as such (E6) wherever the popular
literature makes them.

## THE METHODOLOGICAL BRIDGE TO BOOK II (ch.46, stated explicitly)

**Book II examined ritual transformation — what is DONE to a person over time.
Book IV examines architecture as a COGNITIVE ENVIRONMENT — what a SPACE does to
attention, memory, and expectation independent of any ritual being performed in
it.** These are related but distinct questions and the chapter must state the
distinction rather than silently re-covering Book II's ground. The clean test: an
empty, unlit Masonic lodge room still has measurable effects on a person moving
through it (wayfinding, sightline, threshold) that do not depend on any ceremony
occurring — that is Book IV's subject. The SAME room during initiation adds
ritual-specific effects (obligation, staged narrative, social witnessing) — that
was Book II's subject. The two are complementary, not redundant, and ch.46 exists
specifically to make that non-redundancy visible to a reader who has read Book II.
