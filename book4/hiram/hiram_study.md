## 1. HIRAM, KING OF TYRE (E1–E2)

Named in 2 Samuel 5:11, 1 Kings 5, 1 Kings 9, 1 Chronicles 14. A Phoenician king,
ally of David and then Solomon, supplier of cedar and cypress timber, gold, and
skilled labor for the Temple project, in exchange for territorial and commodity
concessions (1 Kings 9:10–14, the "Cabul" territory transfer — itself a passage
suggesting the alliance had real, recorded friction, not merely cordial trade).
**Tyrian king-lists** (preserved via Josephus quoting the Hellenistic historian
Menander of Ephesus, *Against Apion* I.17–18) give a Phoenician chronology for a
king named Hiram that is broadly compatible with, though not proof of, the
biblical Solomon's dates. This is the strongest external corroborating thread for
any part of the Solomon/Temple narrative and deserves more weight in the book than
popular treatments usually give it, while still falling short of independent proof.

## 2. HIRAM THE CRAFTSMAN (E1–E2)

1 Kings 7:13–14 names a second Hiram — son of a widow of the tribe of Naphtali,
his father a Tyrian bronzeworker — "filled with wisdom, and understanding, and
cunning to work all works in brass," brought to cast the two pillars, the Sea, and
the bronze furnishings. 2 Chronicles 2:13–14 gives a parallel account calling him
**Huram-abi** and expanding his craft skills to gold, silver, iron, stone, timber,
and textile work, and his lineage to Dan on his mother's side (a discrepancy with
Kings' Naphtali that the book must report, not smooth over — see ch.21).

**The "Abi" element** ( Hebrew אבי, "my father") in Chronicles is grammatically a
term of address/honor, not necessarily a name-component — this is the seed of the
Masonic "Abiff," and Book IV must trace exactly when and how "my father" became a
surname-like suffix, which is a philological question with a findable documentary
answer (ch.22, Appendix F) rather than something to leave vague.

## 3. HIRAM ABIFF — THE MASONIC FIGURE (documentary chronology required)

**Not attested by name in this developed form before the 1720s**, consistent with
Book III's dating of the whole third-degree legend. Prichard's *Masonry Dissected*
(1730) gives an early printed version of the murder narrative. The precise
documentary steps between the Chronicles "Huram-abi" and the fully developed
Masonic "Hiram Abiff, Grand Master" — including when the three-ruffian murder
narrative, the acacia sprig, and the "raising" sequence each first appear in
print — must be traced chapter by chapter (ch.20–24) rather than asserted as a
single leap, because the master prompt explicitly requires tracing "the
documentary history rather than assuming antiquity," and a single leap is exactly
the assumption to avoid.

## 4. DO NOT EQUATE

Per the master prompt: the book must not automatically identify Hiram Abiff with
Osiris, Christ, Adonis, Tammuz, Mithras, Attis, or Dionysus. Where comparative
mythology proposes such parallels (and it has, extensively, from the 19th century
onward), the book demonstrates rather than asserts, using the same six-question
transmission protocol from Book I (chronology / geographical contact / textual
evidence / transmission mechanism / similarity / difference) plus the "count the
alternatives" rule from Book II/III (a dying-and-returning narrative shape is a
low-cardinality feature — most cultures' foundational hero/builder narratives
either end in death or don't, and "ends in death" is not by itself informative).

**Preliminary assessment, to be developed fully in ch.24**: the comparative
mythology literature on Hiram-as-dying-god is overwhelmingly 19th–20th century
(consistent with the broader post-1854 occult vocabulary chain established in
Books II–III), draws on Frazerian methodology now considered obsolete in the
discipline (per Book III ch.17's caution on Frazer), and has not, so far as this
research has found, produced a documented transmission route from any specific
ancient mystery cult to the 1720s Masonic legend's composers. **E5–E6 pending
fuller review.**
