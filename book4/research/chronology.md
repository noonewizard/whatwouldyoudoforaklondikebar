## WHAT THE COLUMNS SHOW

**1. The physical Temple and the Masonic Temple are separated by roughly 2,700
years and by every documentary link this series has tested and found absent.**
Nothing connects the building described in 1 Kings to the eighteenth-century
ritual except the *text* — the Hebrew Bible itself, available in English from 1611
and earlier in Latin — which is availability, not transmission, exactly as Book
III's Kabbalah chapters established for a different body of material.

**2. Solomon's Temple entered Masonic ritual centrality later than the words and
grip did.** The catechisms of 1696–1714 (Book III ch.1) show no developed Temple
mythology; Anderson's 1723 traditional history is where the Temple becomes
structurally load-bearing for Masonic self-description. The gap between the
earliest Masonic documents and the Temple's ritual centrality is itself evidence
against an unbroken "Temple tradition" running through medieval operative masonry
— it had to be *elaborated*, and elaborated relatively late.

**3. Villalpando (1604) is the hinge between the biblical Temple and the Masonic
Temple that almost every subsequent tradition drew from, directly or at several
removes, more than the biblical text itself.** His reconstruction imposed Vitruvian
classical order onto Ezekiel's visionary Temple description (not even Solomon's —
a related but textually distinct problem, see ch.66–67) and its engravings
circulated for two centuries as *the* image of "Solomon's Temple," shaping
Masonic and popular imagination alike, independent of what archaeology or even
careful biblical philology would later support.

**4. The occult vocabulary in which the Temple is now popularly discussed —
Kabbalistic correspondences, hidden geometry, Solomon as ceremonial magician —
is, like the vocabulary examined in Book III, substantially nineteenth-century**,
arriving through the same Lévi→Pike→Golden Dawn chain, applied to Temple material
that the eighteenth-century ritual had already made central for entirely different
(architectural-moral) reasons.
